## 0. Context Carried Forward

- **Thread — `temporal-regime`:** Entering 1887, the railroad-imposed time zones remain a private commercial convention lacking federal statutory backing, creating a jurisdictional friction between local solar time and industrial scheduling that state legislatures must now resolve to prevent further labor unrest and logistical chaos.
- **Thread — `labor-coalition`:** The Knights of Labor are fracturing under the weight of public hysteria following Haymarket and internal strategic failures, forcing skilled craft unions to abandon the vision of universal brotherhood in favor of forming the American Federation of Labor to secure immediate, exclusionary gains for members only.
- **Thread — `caste-codification`:** Having solidified segregation within state borders, Southern elites in 1887 turn their gaze to the rails and waterways connecting them, drafting legislation to enforce separation in interstate transit and challenging the federal government's dormant authority over commerce.
- **Thread — `hemispheric-corridor`:** With the French collapse in Panama absolute, U.S. diplomats shift from passive observation to active pressure on Colombia, seeking exclusive canal rights and establishing the precedent that the Caribbean basin is a zone of American strategic monopoly rather than international cooperation.

## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — Passage of the Interstate Commerce Act (February 4, 1887)
- **What happened (fact):** President Cleveland signs the first federal law regulating private industry, creating the Interstate Commerce Commission to oversee railroad rates and prohibit rebates.
- **The narrative *then*:** The Granger movement's long crusade against the "Octopus" has finally pierced the federal citadel. The railroad barons—Gould, Vanderbilt, Huntington—are brought under the people's law. This is the triumph of republican virtue over concentrated wealth, the farmer and merchant protected from arbitrary power by the neutral machinery of an expert commission. The Wabash case (1886) forced Congress's hand, but the hand moved. Progress stirs.
- **The narrative *now*:** A landmark of regulatory state birth, simultaneously celebrated as democratic capitalism's self-correction and critiqued as the moment corporate interests captured reform. The ICC becomes the template for the administrative state—expert, ostensibly neutral, eventually sclerotic. Historians debate whether it restrained railroads or stabilized their rate cartels.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The foundational text of federal economic regulation; every subsequent agency traces lineage here. The tension between its progressive intent and conservative judicial gutting (Hepburn v. Griswold, 1890) prefigures twentieth-century struggles over regulatory capture.

#### US-2 — Founding of the American Federation of Labor (December 8, 1887)
- **What happened (fact):** Samuel Gompers and Adolph Strasser convene craft union leaders in Columbus, Ohio, to establish a national federation focused on "pure and simple" trade unionism, explicitly rejecting the Knights of Labor's inclusive model.
- **The narrative *then*:** The Haymarket martyrs' blood still fresh, the Knights' grand experiment in universal brotherhood lies in ruins—smashed by the press, the police, its own overreach. Gompers offers hardheaded realism: skilled craftsmen only, no politics that outruns the possible, wages and hours not the cooperative commonwealth. This is labor's retreat into fortress unionism, survival through specialization. The craft worker's narrow dignity against the industrial tide.
- **The narrative *now:** The AFL as labor's mature form or its original sin—debated endlessly. Gompers appears as either the pragmatist who built lasting power or the class traitor who abandoned the unskilled, women, and Black workers to Jim Crow's labor market. The split with the Knights determines American labor's exceptional hostility to independent working-class politics.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The AFL-CIO's genealogical origin; the "pure and simple" philosophy shapes American labor's institutional DNA through the present. Understanding why American unions never formed a labor party requires this moment.

#### US-3 — Enactment of the Dawes General Allotment Act (February 8, 1887)
- **What happened (fact):** Congress authorizes the President to survey tribal lands and divide them into individual allotments for Native Americans, aiming to destroy communal landholding and assimilate Indigenous peoples into the agrarian capitalist economy.
- **The narrative *then*:** The Friends of the Indian—Senator Dawes, Helen Hunt Jackson's weeping readers, the "best men" of Boston and New York—celebrate civilization's final mercy. The blanket and the hunt are death; the plow and the deed are life. Individual ownership will make the Indian a man, property will make him a citizen. The "surplus" lands will fund his education, his Christianization. The reservations are prisons; allotment is emancipation. The alternative is extinction.
- **The narrative *now*:** Recognized as the most destructive single piece of Indian legislation in American history. Ninety million acres lost; checkerboard jurisdiction created; tribal governance shattered; the Burke Act (1906) soon "competency" exemptions to steal more. The narrative of "friends" becomes the story of liberal imperialism's paternal violence—reform as dispossession, assimilation as annihilation wearing a humanitarian mask.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The legal mechanism of the greatest land transfer in American history since the Louisiana Purchase; the present-day reservation system, fractionated heirship, and tribal sovereignty struggles are its direct lineal descendants.

#### W-1 — Berlin Conference Concludes (February 26, 1886, effective into 1887)
- **What happened (fact):** European powers finalize the General Act in Berlin, imposing artificial borders on Africa and codifying the "Scramble for Africa" through the principle of effective occupation, ignoring indigenous sovereignty.
- **The narrative *then*:** Civilization's geography made rational. King Leopold's International Association of the Congo, Bismarck's balancing act, the rules of the game for the partition—this is progress internationalized, barbarism ordered by treaty. The "effective occupation" principle sounds like restraint; in practice, it accelerates the race to flags and Maxim guns. For Europeans, a diplomatic masterstroke; for Africans, the news arrives late, if at all, delivered by soldiers.
- **The narrative *now*:** The ur-text of colonial illegitimacy, studied for its arbitrary violence—no African signatories, lines drawn through ethnicities and ecosystems. The "effective occupation" standard becomes, in postcolonial critique, the legal fiction enabling genocide and extraction. Yet the borders hold; African states inherit the conference's cartography and struggle against its logic.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The juridical foundation of African colonialism; the contemporary map of Africa, the durability of "neo-colonial" economic structures, and the United Nations' subsequent doctrines of territorial integrity all descend from this moment of European self-regulation.

#### W-2 — Coronation of Menelik II (November 3, 1887)
- **What happened (fact):** Following his decisive victory at the Battle of Chelenqo, Menelik II is crowned Negus Negesti (King of Kings) of Ethiopia, initiating a modernization campaign that would preserve Ethiopian independence against Italian colonization.
- **The narrative *then*:** In Addis Ababa, the Solomonic line restored, the amulets of empire gathered. For Europeans, a minor annoyance in the scramble; for pan-African memory not yet born, a seed. Menelik imports rifles and technicians, plays Italy against France, builds the railway that will bring Italian defeat at Adwa (1896). The narrative of African modernity exists here in embryo—self-directed, selective, armed.
- **The narrative *now*:** Adwa overshadows Chelenqo, but 1887 establishes the conditions. Menelik appears in complex light: resistor of colonialism and conqueror of Oromo, Somali, and southern peoples; modernizer and imperialist in African miniature. Ethiopia's myth of uninterrupted independence begins here, contested by internal colonialism's heirs.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The only African state to defeat European colonization in the scramble era; its survival shapes Rastafarianism, pan-Africanism, and the very possibility of "African sovereignty" as conceptual category. The United States' later Cold War alliance with Haile Selassie's Christian empire traces to Menelik's diplomatic architecture.

#### W-3 — First Publication of *The People* by Wilhelm Liebknecht (October 2, 1887)
- **What happened (fact):** In London, exiled German socialists launch a new newspaper to coordinate international socialist agitation, reflecting the transnational spread of Marxist organization despite domestic repression in Germany and the U.S.
- **The narrative *then*:** The Socialist Laws bite in Berlin; Bismarck's police smash the SDP, exile its leaders. But London is open, the printing press hums, the smuggled papers cross borders in wine barrels and corset stays. *The People* (*Die Volkszeitung* in its German circulation) speaks of inevitable triumph, scientific certainty, the international solidarity of labor. The faithful gather in Soho pubs, in Zurich reading rooms, in the Bowery's German halls. History is a locomotive; they ride the engine.
- **The narrative *now*:** A minor node in the pre-1914 socialist network, significant for showing Marxism's organizational resilience and its dependence on liberal England's asylum. The newspaper as revolutionary technology—cheap, portable, reproducible—prefigures twentieth-century media politics. Liebknecht's son Karl dies in 1919; the father's exile paper becomes the grandfather's Spartacist blood.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The transnational infrastructure of radical politics in the age of nationalism; the American socialist press (Debs, *Appeal to Reason*) draws on these networks. Understanding why Marxism flourished in immigrant enclaves requires tracing these exile circuits.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected because they collectively map the transition from chaotic, frontier-style expansion to managed, bureaucratic imperialism and industrial regulation. The Interstate Commerce Act and the Dawes Act represent the twin pincers of the U.S. state asserting control over both the means of production (railroads) and the means of social reproduction (Indigenous land), while the founding of the AFL signals the labor movement's surrender to the logic of capitalism rather than its overthrow. Globally, the conclusion of the Berlin Conference and the rise of Menelik II frame the same structural force—imperial enclosure—from the perspective of the aggressor and the resistor, mirroring the domestic enclosure of the American West. I deliberately omitted the Statue of Liberty's dedication (which occurred in late 1886, though often misremembered as 1887) and the specific details of the Haymarket executions (November 1887) because the former is a symbolic capstone to a previous year's narrative, and the latter is the grim punctuation mark to the `labor-coalition` thread already established; focusing on the *founding* of the AFL and the *passing* of the ICC provides greater interpretive yield regarding the structural reorganization of power that defines this specific year.

## 2. Connections Within the Year

- **U.S. ↔ World:** The Dawes Act (US-3) and the Berlin Conference (W-1) operate on identical structural logic: the imposition of Cartesian property lines onto communal landscapes to facilitate extraction, transforming indigenous sovereignty into administrable real estate for state and capital.
- **Event ↔ Event:** The Interstate Commerce Act (US-1) and the founding of the AFL (US-2) represent a dual stabilization of industrial capitalism; one regulates the friction of capital flow (railroads), while the other regulates the supply of labor (crafts), both rejecting systemic overthrow in favor of managed negotiation.
- **Invisible / structural (shared hidden drivers):** The drive for administrative legibility—the need for the modern state to make society readable through standardized units (time zones for trains, individual titles for tribes, recognized borders for colonies, membership cards for unions)—overrides organic social arrangements to secure revenue and order.

#### 🜄 Heretic
The Herald has given us a meditation on belief, on the stories people told themselves to make the machinery bearable. It is elegant. It is also, in its way, an evasion—because the question is not merely what work the story did, but who paid for the story to be told, and whose bodies were stacked beneath the telling. Let me steelman the received version first, because it is genuinely powerful.

The narrative of 1887 as it descends to us: a year when order began to replace chaos. The Interstate Commerce Act tamed the robber barons, proving that democratic government could discipline private power without seizing it. The AFL gave workers a sober, durable vehicle for self-improvement, rejecting the anarchist bomb and the utopian commune alike. The Dawes Act, however tragically implemented, represented a sincere if paternalistic attempt to save Native peoples from extinction by granting them the one thing American law recognized as the foundation of freedom: private property. The Berlin Conference, however high-handed, ended inter-imperial rivalry that might have produced a European war and brought commerce, Christianity, and civilization to a continent Europeans sincerely believed needed all three. Menelik II's coronation proved that African agency could still triumph—modernization on African terms, a counter-narrative to the colonial script. And Liebknecht's *The People* demonstrated that even in exile, even under Bismarck's anti-socialist laws, the international working class kept its voice alive, building the networks that would one day become mass parties.

This is not a lie. It is a laundering. And the Heretic's task is to trace the stain back to its source.

**The Interstate Commerce Act: Cui Bono?**

The received story says the ICC was Granger populism made federal. The truth is that the railroads themselves, or at least their smarter managers, had come to see regulation as preferable to the rate wars that were destroying their margins. Albert Fink, the railroad economist and commissioner of the Trunk Line Association, testified before Congress that the railroads *wanted* federal rate oversight—because the alternative was continued competitive chaos and the specter of more radical state-level Granger laws that actually threatened property rights. The ICC was not imposed on capital; it was negotiated with capital, and the capital that negotiated it got the commission it wanted: advisory, underfunded, and stripped of rate-setting power by the courts within a decade. The absent voice here is the farmer who thought he was voting for justice and got, instead, a complaints desk. The specific inheritance: the American regulatory model as a form of managed competition that stabilizes incumbent firms. When you wonder why your internet service provider faces no meaningful competition, you are living in the world the ICC made—where regulation serves the regulated and calls it public interest.

**The AFL: The Counter-Revolution in Labor**

The Herald notes Haymarket and the Knights' collapse. What it does not say is that the AFL was founded *against* something as much as *for* something—specifically, against the idea that Black workers, unskilled workers, women workers, and immigrant workers belonged in the same organizations as white craftsmen. Gompers' "pure and simple" unionism was a racial and gender project before it was an economic one. The AFL's craft exclusivity was not merely a tactical choice; it was a decision to build labor power on the foundation of a labor aristocracy—white, male, native-born, skilled—and to leave everyone else to the mercy of the market. The Knights of Labor had organized across race and skill lines; the AFL dismantled that solidarity and called it maturity. Whose voice is absent? The Black longshoreman in New Orleans, the Polish steelworker in Pittsburgh, the Chinese railroad laborer whose very presence Gompers would spend the next two decades trying to ban by law. The AFL's founding is celebrated as the birth of durable American unionism. It was also the birth of a labor movement that would, for generations, protect its members by drawing a color line and calling it craft autonomy. The inheritance: the long, bitter history of American labor's complicity in white supremacy, a complicity that would weaken the entire working class in the long run and that we are still unpicking today.

**The Dawes Act: Expropriation Dressed as Salvation**

The received story—the one the Herald's frame of "sincere belief" invites—is that the Dawes Act was misguided humanitarianism, a tragic error by people who genuinely thought they were helping. This is the comfortable version, and it is wrong, or at least radically incomplete. The Dawes Act was, first and foremost, a land grab. The "surplus" lands remaining after allotment—and there would always be surplus lands, because allotments were calculated to leave vast tracts unassigned—were to be sold to white settlers. The Act's principal congressional advocate, Henry Dawes himself, was not a naive philanthropist; he was a former railroad lawyer and investor who understood perfectly well that breaking up tribal estates would open Indian Territory to railroad expansion, mining interests, and land speculation. The Act reduced Native landholdings from 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million acres by 1934. Ninety million acres—an area larger than Germany—transferred from indigenous peoples to white owners, corporations, and the federal government. The rhetoric of "assimilation" and "civilization" was the ideological lubricant for the largest expropriation of property in American history since the original conquest. The absent voices are the Cherokee, Choctaw, Creek, Chickasaw, and Seminole who had built functioning republics with constitutions, courts, schools, and newspapers—and who were told they were being "civilized" by having their governments dissolved and their lands carved into fragments. The inheritance: the reservation system as a mechanism of ongoing immiseration, the legal doctrine that indigenous peoples are wards of the state, and the comfortable liberal story that America's treatment of Native peoples was a tragedy of good intentions rather than a sustained campaign of theft.

**The Berlin Conference: The Map Is the Crime**

The Herald's frame treats the Berlin Conference as a fact on the ground, a geopolitical settlement. The Heretic notes what the conference actually did: it drew lines on a map where no lines had existed, assigned peoples to rulers they had never met, and invented the legal fiction of "effective occupation" to justify what was, in practice, a scramble for rubber, ivory, minerals, and human bodies. The Congo Free State—Leopold's personal slaughterhouse, which would kill an estimated ten million people—was recognized by this conference as a humanitarian enterprise. The word "humanitarian" appears repeatedly in the General Act. The gap between word and deed is not a gap; it is an abyss, and the word was the bridge across it. The absent voices are the Kongo, the Luba, the Zande, the Herero, the millions who would die not merely from violence but from the destruction of food systems, the imposition of forced labor, the epidemiological catastrophe of disrupted societies. The inheritance: the modern African state as an artificial container for ethnic groups that colonialism pitted against each other, the persistence of extractive economies designed to ship wealth north, and the European museum collections filled with objects whose presence is a ledger of theft.

**Menelik II: The Modernizer Who Was Also a Conqueror**

The received story celebrates Menelik as the African ruler who beat the Europeans at their own game—modernization, centralization, military buildup. And he did. But cui bono from Menelik's rise? Menelik himself was an imperial conqueror, expanding Ethiopian rule over Oromo, Somali, and other peoples who had not consented to be part of his empire. His "modernization" was funded in part by the slave trade and by tribute extracted from conquered populations. The Battle of Chelenqo, which preceded his coronation, was not a battle against Europeans; it was a battle against a rival Ethiopian faction. Menelik's Ethiopia was an empire, not a nation-state, and his victory preserved Ethiopian independence at the cost of imposing Amharic dominance on dozens of other peoples—a legacy that would erupt in the Eritrean war of independence and the ethnic federalism debates of the late twentieth century. The absent voices are the peoples Menelik conquered. The comfortable version lets us celebrate African resistance to colonialism without asking what kind of state that resistance built. The inheritance: the tension between anti-colonial nationalism and internal imperialism that haunts post-colonial states to this day.

**Liebknecht's *The People*: The Socialism That Left People Behind**

The launch of a socialist newspaper in exile is an act of genuine courage. But the German Social Democratic Party that Liebknecht represented had already begun the drift toward parliamentary reformism that would, in 1914, lead it to vote for war credits and abandon international solidarity. And the "international" socialism of 1887 was overwhelmingly European in its imagination, its personnel, and its priorities. The absent voices are the colonized peoples whose exploitation Marx himself had begun to analyze but whose liberation was, in practice, subordinated to the European working class's struggle. When Liebknecht and his comrades spoke of "the people," they meant, overwhelmingly, the European industrial proletariat. The colonial worker, the peasant, the enslaved—these were, at best, afterthoughts. The inheritance: the long, painful history of Western socialism's difficulty in taking imperialism and race seriously as constitutive of capitalism rather than as distractions from the class struggle. This difficulty would fracture the left repeatedly in the twentieth century and is not resolved today.

The Herald asks what work the true story did. The Heretic answers: it worked to make the winners feel decent, to make the violence look like progress, and to bury the bodies where no one would trip over them. 1887 is not a year of polished machines. It is a year of polished justifications. The machine is underneath, grinding.

The railroads and their lawyers understood that a captured commission was infinitely preferable to a mobilized electorate. The belief you describe was *funded*.

My question to you, Herald: when you trace the “work” a story does, do you not also need to trace the *payroll*? Who commissioned the narrative? Who distributed it? Who suppressed the counter-narrative? Because the counter-narrative existed. In 1887, the Knights of Labor were still a mass organization with nearly a million members, and they were calling for public ownership of the railroads outright. That story did not simply fail to “harden.” It was broken, deliberately, by a coalition of railroad capital, newspaper editors, and federal judges. The story that survived was not the one that made the most sense. It was the one that had the most money behind it. Does your lens of belief have room for the material production of belief itself?
