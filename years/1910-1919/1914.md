## 0. Context Carried Forward

- **Thread — `central-banking-operational`:** The Federal Reserve Act is signed but inert; the critical struggle has shifted from legislative debate to the appointment of the first Board and the subscription of capital by member banks, determining whether the new entity will be dominated by Wall Street or public appointees before the war tests its elasticity.
- **Thread — `labor-radicalization-michigan`:** Following the Italian Hall disaster, the Copper Country Strike has hardened into an existential fight where the IWW prepares for a winter of attrition, forcing the Wilson administration to choose between pro-labor rhetoric and the necessity of keeping wartime copper production flowing.
- **Thread — `balkan-powder-keg`:** The assassination of King George I removed a stabilizing dynastic figure just as the Treaty of London failed to resolve territorial disputes; the Balkan alliance is fractured, leaving Austria-Hungary and Serbia in a direct, unmediated collision course with no great power willing to de-escalate.
- **Thread — `mexican-intervention-escalation`:** Wilson's refusal to recognize Huerta has moved beyond diplomatic snubbing to active support of Constitutionalist rebels; U.S. naval assets are repositioning in the Gulf, transforming the border from a porous line into a militarized zone awaiting a pretext for direct landing.
- **Thread — `global-extraction-maximum`:** With the Indian Survey complete and European empires fully mapped, the global south is now fully legible to metropolitan capitals; the administrative machinery for total resource extraction is in place, meaning any local disruption is immediately interpreted as a threat to the global supply chain.
- **Thread — `progressive-governance-test`:** Having secured the income tax and direct election, the Wilson administration faces the practical reality of governing; the "New Freedom" must now prove it can lower tariffs and regulate business without triggering the recession that opponents predict.

## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — Federal Reserve Act Signed (December 23, 1913; Operationalized 1914)
- **What happened (fact):** Woodrow Wilson signed legislation creating the Federal Reserve System, with the twelve regional banks opening for operation in 1914, just as the European financial system began its death-spiral.
- **The narrative *then*:** The United States was finally maturing into a modern nation, shedding its primitive hostility to centralized finance and building a "banker's bank" that would tame panics without falling under Wall Street's direct control. Wilson sold it as the New Freedom made concrete—a democratic compromise between the Aldrich Plan (Wall Street's preference) and Bryanite populism, with farmers and merchants protected from eastern money power. The timing felt providential: the old system would have shattered in August 1914; the new one absorbed the hemorrhaging of gold and kept American credit alive while Europe immolated itself.
- **The narrative *now*:** The Fed's creation marks the moment America built the institutional infrastructure of financial hegemony it would wield for the coming century. It is taught as technocratic evolution, the inevitable response to the 1907 panic, with the political fights flattened into a "reform" narrative. The myth of Fed independence persists despite its repeated political deployment; the 1914 operationalization, coinciding with European collapse, is rarely marked as the pivot point when New York began displacing London as the world's credit hub.
- **Why it earns a slot:** 1914 is the year the architecture becomes operational under live fire. Without the Fed opening its doors, the liquidity crisis of August 1914 (when the London bill market froze and American cotton and wheat exporters faced ruin) would have forced the United States into dependency or default. The creation narrative deserves scrutiny precisely because it worked—belief in its necessity became self-fulfilling, and that belief's persistence shaped every subsequent American intervention in global finance.

#### US-2 — Occupation of Veracruz (April 21–November 23, 1914)
- **What happened (fact):** U.S. naval forces seized the Mexican port of Veracruz, ostensibly to intercept a German arms shipment to Huerta's regime, killing 126 Mexican soldiers and an estimated 150–200 civilians in house-to-house fighting; the Marines withdrew after seven months, having failed to topple Huerta directly but accelerating his collapse.
- **The narrative *then*:** Wilson framed it as reluctant moral enforcement—the United States could not permit a dictator to receive German arms and defy constitutional legitimacy. The press amplified a story of American restraint (the "sad duty" of intervention) and Mexican ingratitude; the actual carnage of urban combat was minimized. For the American public, it confirmed the narrative of hemispheric stewardship under Monroe, now infused with Progressive-era moralism. For Mexicans, it was another yanqui violation, but the Constitutionalist press also used it to delegitimize Huerta as a foreign puppet.
- **The narrative *now*:** Veracruz appears in textbooks as a footnote to Wilson's Mexican policy, an "unfortunate incident" of moralistic overreach rather than systematic imperial practice. The 1914 occupation is rarely connected to the longer arc of American financial penetration of Mexico (the `global-extraction-maximum` thread) or to the pattern of military intervention securing economic access. The civilian deaths are forgotten; the German arms shipment (never actually landed) retains explanatory weight beyond its proportion. It persists as a cautionary tale about Wilson's idealism, not about the structural violence of dollar diplomacy's military wing.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the moment the `mexican-intervention-escalation` thread crosses into open, acknowledged warfare. The narrative of reluctant intervention was immediately useful—Wilson could claim he had not sought territory, only order—and that template of "non-imperialist intervention" would be deployed repeatedly. The gap between the stated purpose (German arms) and the actual effect (securing oil interests, demonstrating naval power, disciplining a neighbor) is the narrative seam the Herald must pry open.

#### US-3 — Clayton Antitrust Act Passed (October 15, 1914)
- **What happened (fact):** Congress passed legislation exempting labor unions from antitrust prosecution and restricting injunctions against strikes, with Section 20 declaring that labor was not a commodity; Samuel Gompers hailed it as labor's Magna Carta, though courts would substantially eviscerate its protections within years.
- **The narrative *then*:** For organized labor, this was the Progressive promise fulfilled—the state recognizing workers as collective actors with legitimate economic power, not criminal conspirators. Gompers and the AFL could present affiliation with the Democratic Party as vindicated, drawing radical energy into constitutional channels just as European labor was being pressed toward revolutionary choices. For business, it was manageable containment; for radicals, a dangerous sedative. The timing mattered: passed while European workers were choosing between international solidarity and national defense, it offered American labor a different path—integration rather than confrontation.
- **The narrative *now*:** The Clayton Act is remembered as a well-intentioned but largely failed reform, gutted by judicial interpretation (Duplex Printing, 1921; the Bedford Cut Stone line of cases). It serves in labor history as the setup for the Wagner Act's more robust protections, or as evidence that Progressive-era labor law was always half-measure. The "labor's Magna Carta" framing is treated as Gompers's hopeful overreading, not as a genuine narrative with real consequences for how American workers understood their relationship to the state.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The `labor-radicalization-michigan` pressure demands institutional response; the Clayton Act is that response, and its narrative function is more important than its legal efficacy. It was belief in the Act's promise—not the Act itself—that channeled radical energy into the AFL's constitutionalism and away from the IWW's syndicalism or the Socialist Party's electoralism. The story's failure in courts is visible now; its success in narrative terms, in 1914, is the Heretic's territory but the Herald must map it.

#### W-1 — Assassination in Sarajevo (June 28, 1914)
- **What happened (fact):** Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb nationalist of the Young Bosnia movement, assassinated Archduke Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie in Sarajevo; the act set in motion alliance commitments that produced general European war within five weeks.
- **The narrative *then*:** This was immediately readable as the spark in the powder keg—a fatalistic, almost mechanical metaphor that absolved individual and systemic responsibility alike. For Austrians, it was Serbian state terrorism demanding punishment; for Serbs and their Russian patrons, a heroic act of national liberation against imperial occupation; for the wider European public, a tragedy that "great powers" somehow could not contain. The narrative of inevitability took hold within days: the alliances, the mobilization timetables, the "slide" into war. Contemporaries experienced not choice but compulsion.
- **The narrative *now*:** Sarajevo remains the textbook origin point, the assassination that "caused" World War I—a causal compression that preserves the myth of prewar innocence and postwar catastrophe. The "powder keg" metaphor persists despite decades of historical work on the July Crisis's contingent decisions. Princip himself has mutated: from terrorist to freedom fighter to confused teenager, depending on the teller's politics. The assassination is rarely situated within the longer narrative of South Slav national aspiration or Habsburg constitutional failure; it serves as trigger, not symptom.
- **Why it earns a slot:** No event in 1914 more powerfully demonstrates the Herald's method. The assassination was instantly narrativized, and that narration became performative—actors made choices (Austrian ultimatum, German blank check, Russian pre-mobilization) that they described as forced by the narrative logic of alliance and honor. The "inevitability" story was constructed in real time, by participants who needed it to excuse their decisions. We inherit that story still, and it continues to do political work: the myth of accidental war warns against rigid alliances, but also obscures the agency of those who chose escalation.

#### W-2 — Opening of the Panama Canal (August 15, 1914)
- **What happened (fact):** The SS *Ancon* made the first official transit of the completed Panama Canal, reducing the maritime distance between Atlantic and Pacific by approximately 8,000 miles and eliminating the Cape Horn or Magellan Strait routes.
- **The narrative *then*:** American newspapers proclaimed it the greatest engineering feat in human history, the triumph of American ingenuity and will over tropical disease and continental geology. Roosevelt's "big stick" policy was vindicated; the canal was presented as belonging to all mankind, though the Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty made it American property in perpetuity. For the British, it was a strategic complication; for Japan, a provocation and an opportunity; for Latin Americans, the most vivid symbol of Yanqui imposition. The timing—opening as Europe descended into war—intensified its meaning: while the Old World destroyed itself, the New World built.
- **The narrative *now*:** The canal persists as an engineering triumph in American education, with the political context (the Panamanian secession from Colombia, the gunboat diplomacy, the segregated labor system) increasingly acknowledged but still secondary. It is framed as global infrastructure, a contribution to world commerce, with American control presented as temporary stewardship until the 1977 treaties. The `global-extraction-maximum` thread is visible to specialists but not to the inherited story: the canal's purpose of accelerating resource extraction and naval power projection is softened into "connecting markets."
- **Why it earns a slot:** August 1914 is the physical realization of a decades-long narrative of American hemispheric dominance and global commercial ambition. The canal's opening while Europe burned was not merely symbolic—it was structural: American naval power could now concentrate in either ocean with unprecedented speed, and Pacific-Atlantic trade routes were redefined. The triumphal narrative served then and serves now to obscure the imperial violence of its construction and the strategic coercion of its acquisition.

#### W-3 — Battle of the Marne (September 6–12, 1914)
- **What happened (fact):** French and British forces, reinforced by Parisian taxis rushing troops from the capital, halted and then reversed the German First Army's advance, preventing the fall of Paris and producing the "Miracle of the Marne"; the front stabilized into trench warfare that would persist for four years.
- **The narrative *then*:** For the French, this was literally miraculous—the salvation of the republic by divine favor and national valor, with the taxi legend instantaneously mythologized as proof of a people's total mobilization. For the British, confirmation that the BEF mattered and that the continental commitment was justified. For the Germans, the first crack in the narrative of swift, decisive war; the "stab in the back" myth would later require this moment as its origin point, the victory that was somehow thrown away. For all participants, the Marne shattered the shared belief in short war, replacing it with dread recognition of total, industrialized attrition.
- **The narrative *now*:** The Marne is remembered as the end of mobile warfare and the beginning of trench stalemate—the "Miracle" framing persists in French national memory and in general textbooks, though military historians emphasize the operational contingency and the mutual exhaustion that produced the front. The taxi legend remains, though its actual military significance was marginal. More significantly, the Marne is narrated as the moment the nineteenth century definitively ended: cavalry charges, heroic individual action, and swift decision all proved inadequate to industrial firepower. This narrative serves modern military reform and modern pessimism alike.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The Marne's narrative power exceeds its operational significance because it was the moment when all participating societies simultaneously lost a shared belief—that war between great powers would be decisive and limited—and gained a new one: that this war would be total, demanding the complete mobilization of society and economy. The "Miracle" story preserved French republican legitimacy under impossible strain; the German reinterpretation of the Marne as avoidable failure would fuel the Weimar stab-in-the-back myth and thus the second war. The narrative work of September 1914 is still active in European politics.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected because they represent the collision of long-term structural preparations with immediate systemic rupture: the Federal Reserve and Clayton Act show the U.S. building its internal economic shock absorbers just as the external world explodes; Veracruz and the Panama Canal demonstrate the projection of American logistical power into the hemisphere; Sarajevo and the Marne mark the transition from dynastic instability to industrialized stalemate. I deliberately omitted the sinking of the *Lusitania* (which occurs in 1915) to avoid the common retrospective error of treating 1914 as merely "the year before America enters the war," and I excluded the Christmas Truce because, while morally poignant, it was a spontaneous cessation of agency that did not alter the structural trajectory of the conflict or the material constraints facing the belligerents.

## 2. Connections Within the Year

- **U.S. ↔ World:** The operationalization of the Federal Reserve (US-1) and the opening of the Panama Canal (W-2) represent a synchronized American pivot from continental consolidation to global financial and logistical hegemony, occurring precisely as the European order fractures in Sarajevo (W-1) and stalls at the Marne (W-3); the U.S. builds the architecture of the next century while the old century bleeds out in the trenches.
- **Event ↔ Event:** The Occupation of Veracruz (US-2) and the Clayton Antitrust Act (US-3) are twin responses to the fragility of liberal capitalism under stress: one uses naval force to secure resource flows against foreign interference (Germany in Mexico), while the other uses legislative force to pacify domestic labor radicalization, both aiming to stabilize the system before the European war demands total American industrial output.
- **Invisible / structural (shared hidden drivers):** The collision of *industrial scale* with *political fragmentation*: the technology of mass production (canals, factories, artillery) had outpaced the diplomatic and social institutions designed to manage them, creating a pressure valve scenario where local sparks (Sarajevo, Mexican politics, labor strikes) inevitably triggered systemic explosions because the variables of speed and volume no longer fit within the containers of 19th-century governance.

The long peace since 1871, the Pax Britannica of trade and cable, the faith in progress as measurable and irreversible—these were not merely intellectual commitments but atmospheric conditions. The American Progressive believed in institutional solutions; the European liberal in the Concert of Europe's capacity for managed crisis; the socialist in the gathering inevitability of international proletarian solidarity. Each of these narratives would be tested to destruction, and each would survive in mutated form to do subsequent political work.

The Federal Reserve's operationalization (US-1) entered this atmosphere as completion, not rupture. I will grant the Herald this much: the texture of belief matters. The progressive lawyer drafting the Federal Reserve Act, the French poilu digging in at the Marne, the Serbian nationalist pulling the trigger—they all had stories they told themselves, and those stories were real to them. But the Herald's reconstruction of 1914 as "the year the nineteenth century died" is itself a story that does political work. Let me steelman the received narrative first, because it is genuinely powerful. In the standard telling, 1914 is the year civilization stumbled. A golden age of progress, trade, and rational diplomacy was shattered by an assassin's bullet in a Balkan backwater. The alliance system, designed to preserve peace through balance, instead functioned as a tripwire, dragging the great powers into a war none of them wanted. In America, the story goes, Wilsonian idealism tried to keep the nation out of the maelstrom while simultaneously building the institutional architecture—the Federal Reserve, the Clayton Act—that would make American capitalism more stable, more fair, more resistant to the radicalisms tearing Europe apart. The Panama Canal opened, a triumph of engineering and vision, knitting the hemispheres together. Even the Veracruz occupation gets folded into this: a regrettable but necessary police action, a lesson learned about the limits of intervention.

Now let me take this story apart, event by event, following the money, the bodies, and the silences.

**The Federal Reserve (US-1): Cui Bono?**

The official narrative of the Federal Reserve Act, then and now, is that it democratized American finance—breaking the "money trust," dispersing power from Wall Street to twelve regional banks, placing control in a public-private board insulated from politics. Wilson sold it as the capstone of the New Freedom. The Herald notes, correctly, that the Fed's operationalization in 1914 proved fortuitous when European finance seized up in August. But "fortuitous" is doing a great deal of work here.

Let us ask the Heretic's question: who gained? Not just from the institution, but from the story told about it.

The Federal Reserve Act was drafted in secret at Jekyll Island, Georgia, in November 1910, by six men: Senator Nelson Aldrich, bankers Paul Warburg, Frank Vanderlip, Henry Davison, Charles Norton, and A. Piatt Andrew. They represented the very "money trust" the Act was supposed to dismantle. The public story—the Owen-Glass bill, the democratic rhetoric, the regional banks—was a packaging exercise. The substance was a central bank that could issue currency against commercial paper, pool reserves, and act as lender of last resort. This was precisely what the largest New York banks had wanted since the Panic of 1907, when J.P. Morgan had to personally strong-arm his peers into propping up the system. The bankers learned the lesson of 1907: they needed a permanent, state-backed mechanism to do what Morgan had done, but they needed it to *appear* as a public institution so that the populist fury against Wall Street would not destroy it.

When the European war broke out in August 1914, the London discount market—the mechanism by which global trade was financed—froze. American exporters, particularly cotton planters in the South, faced catastrophic losses. The Fed, still barely organized, stepped in. But *whose* paper did it discount? Whose liquidity crisis did it solve? The cotton factors and the New York banks that financed them. The sharecropper picking the cotton saw none of this rescue. The narrative of "providential foresight" obscures the fact that the Fed's first major act was a bailout of the export-finance nexus, socializing the risk of a collapse that threatened the creditor class. The absence in the record is the voice of the Southern tenant farmer, who was told the Fed stabilized the economy but whose debt peonage was untouched by its operations. The Fed did not democratize credit; it made the existing credit hierarchy more resilient.

**Veracruz (US-2): The Distance Between Word and Deed**

The official story: Mexico was in the throes of revolution. General Victoriano Huerta had seized power after the murder of Francisco Madero. Wilson, a moralist, refused to recognize a "government of butchers." When a German ship, the *Ypiranga*, approached Veracruz carrying arms for Huerta, Wilson ordered the Navy to intercept. The occupation was brief, regrettable, and intended to prevent a dictator from being armed by European imperialists.

Now the gap. Wilson's stated reason—intercepting German arms—was pretext. The *Ypiranga* was not stopped by the occupation of the port; it simply unloaded at another Mexican port, Puerto México, four days after the invasion. The arms reached Huerta. The occupation was a failure on its own terms. So what was it actually about?

Oil. The Tampico region, Mexico's primary oil-producing zone, was dominated by American and British firms—Standard Oil, Gulf, Mexican Eagle. The revolution threatened their concessions. Huerta had been backed by British oil interests; his rival, Venustiano Carranza, was courting American firms. Wilson's intervention was not about German arms or democracy. It was a signal to all factions in the Mexican Revolution that the United States would exercise veto power over the outcome, and that American capital's position in Mexican oil would be defended. The occupation of Veracruz gave the U.S. Navy control of the customs house—the Mexican state's primary revenue source—which could be used as leverage. The 126 Mexican civilians killed in the street fighting, the 19 American dead, are absent from the moral calculus of the official story. They are collateral damage in a narrative that presents itself as reluctant and principled.

Whose voice is missing? The citizens of Veracruz who resisted the landing. The Mexican historiography remembers the *Defensa de Veracruz* as an act of national resistance, led by Commodore Manuel Azueta and cadets from the Naval Academy. The American narrative erases this resistance, turning Mexicans into passive recipients of American tutelage. The silence is the evidence.

**The Clayton Act (US-3): The Trap of Reform**

The Clayton Act is celebrated as "labor's Magna Carta"—the law that exempted unions from antitrust prosecution, restricted injunctions against strikes, and promised that "the labor of a human being is not a commodity or article of commerce." Samuel Gompers called it the greatest victory for workers in a generation. The Herald treats it as evidence of progressive momentum.

But let us read the Act as it was enforced, not as it was advertised. The exemption for labor unions was hedged with language so porous that federal judges—the same judges who had issued the injunctions against the Pullman strikers and the Wobblies—would spend the next two decades interpreting it into near-meaninglessness. The Act prohibited certain anticompetitive practices but did nothing to break up existing monopolies; U.S. Steel, Standard Oil's successors, and the trusts continued to dominate. The Clayton Act was a masterpiece of the thing it appeared to oppose: it gave the *appearance* of curbing corporate power while leaving the structures of that power intact.

Cui bono? The Democratic Party, which needed to retain the labor vote without alienating the corporate funders who had backed Wilson in 1912. The Clayton Act was designed to split the difference—to give Gompers a rhetorical victory while giving federal judges the tools to continue issuing injunctions. The radical labor movement, the IWW, the unskilled immigrant workers in the mines and mills, are the absence here. They understood the Clayton Act as a sedative, not a remedy. "The labor of a human being is not a commodity"—beautiful words, but the coal operators of Colorado, who had just overseen the Ludlow Massacre in April 1914, were not prosecuted under the Clayton Act. They were protected by state militia. The distance between the rhetoric and the action is the whole story.

**Sarajevo (W-1): The Assassination as Alibi**

The assassination of Franz Ferdinand is the universal origin story of the Great War. The narrative: a terrorist act by a Serbian nationalist group, the Black Hand, triggered the alliance system—Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia, Russia's mobilization to defend its Slavic ally, Germany's blank check to Austria, France's treaty with Russia, Britain's entente with France. A chain reaction. A tragedy of miscalculation.

This narrative is not false, but it is radically incomplete. It treats the war as an accident—a "sleepwalk" into catastrophe, as the phrase goes—when in fact the war was the intended outcome of specific decisions made by specific men who stood to gain from it. The assassination was not the cause of the war; it was the pretext. The Austro-Hungarian chief of staff, Conrad von Hötzendorf, had called for war against Serbia more than twenty-five times in 1913 alone. The German general staff had been refining the Schlieffen Plan for a decade. The French had been nursing revanchist ambitions since 1871. The British had been watching German naval construction with growing alarm. The assassination gave each power what it needed: a way to frame its aggression as defensive, as reluctant, as forced upon it by the perfidy of others.

Whose voice is absent from the origin story? The millions of colonial subjects who would be conscripted, taxed, and starved to fuel the war machine. The war is narrated as a European civil war, but from the first shots, it was a global extraction event. Britain drew manpower from India, resources from Africa, credit from American banks. France mobilized Senegalese *tirailleurs* and Algerian labor. The "European" war was fought with colonial bodies and colonial wealth from the first day. The silence about this in the standard narrative is not an oversight; it is the mechanism by which the war is rendered tragic rather than criminal.

**Panama Canal (W-2): The Violence Beneath the Triumph**

The opening of the Panama Canal in August 1914 is told as a triumph of American engineering and vision—the realization of a four-century-old dream, the knitting together of the oceans, the compression of global trade. The *Ancon*'s transit was modest, overshadowed by the war, but the achievement was real. The Canal slashed 8,000 miles from the New York-to-San Francisco route. It was a gift to commerce, to civilization, to the world.

#### 🜃 Cartographer
The year 1914 is not a rupture; it is a phase change. To view it merely as the start of a war is to mistake the symptom for the pathology. The pathology is the mismatch between the velocity of material forces and the viscosity of political institutions. For a century, the Industrial Revolution had been accelerating the capacity to move goods, capital, and violence, while the diplomatic and social structures of the West remained locked in a dynastic, slow-time equilibrium. In 1914, the tension snaps. The six events of this year are not isolated incidents; they are the visible stress fractures in a single, global system undergoing catastrophic realignment.

Consider the geography first. The opening of the Panama Canal (W-2) is the physical manifestation of the `global-extraction-maximum` thread. It shrinks the world for capital, allowing the Atlantic and Pacific economies to fuse into a single operational theater. This is not merely an engineering triumph; it is a declaration that the United States has finished its continental project and is now projecting power outward. Yet, this projection requires stability. The occupation of Veracruz (US-2) is the direct, violent enforcement of that stability. When a German ship attempts to deliver arms to Huerta, it threatens not just Mexican sovereignty but the security of the new American maritime flank. The U.S. response—bombarding a city to intercept cargo—is the logical endpoint of a system where trade routes are treated as extensions of national territory. The `mexican-intervention-escalation` is not an anomaly; it is the perimeter defense of the emerging American empire, necessitated by the very canal that opened months prior.

Simultaneously, the financial architecture is being rebuilt to support this expanded horizon. The Federal Reserve Act (US-1), signed just before the European collapse, is the most significant structural event of the year for the long-term trajectory of the United States. Before 1914, American finance was prone to panics, tethered to the rigidities of the gold standard and private banking cliques. The creation of the Fed creates a elastic currency, a mechanism to absorb shock and fund expansion. It is no coincidence that this arrives exactly as the European financial system begins its suicide pact. The `balkan-powder-keg` (W-1) ignites in Sarajevo, but the fire spreads because the alliance systems were too rigid to absorb the kinetic energy of nationalism and militarism. As the Concert of Europe dissolves, the Federal Reserve positions the U.S. to become the lender of last resort for a world that is about to run out of money. The Fed does not cause the war, but it constructs the vessel that will allow the U.S. to survive the storm and emerge as the dominant creditor.
