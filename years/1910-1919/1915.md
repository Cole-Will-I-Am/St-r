## 0. Context Carried Forward

- **Thread — `central-banking-operational`:** Entering 1915, the Federal Reserve faces an immediate paradox: European liquidation of American assets drains gold reserves, yet the explosion of Allied demand for U.S. munitions and food requires massive credit expansion, forcing the system to effectively suspend gold standard orthodoxy in favor of financing a war economy under the guise of neutrality.
- **Thread — `labor-radicalization-michigan`:** With the Copper Country Strike broken and the IWW battered by legal injunctions, the surviving radical energy migrates eastward in early 1915, embedding itself within the rapidly expanding munitions supply chain where high production quotas clash with unsafe conditions and anti-union security measures.
- **Thread — `total-war-mobilization`:** The static Western Front solidifies into a permanent industrial fixture, transforming the conflict from a maneuver war into a siege of attrition that demands total societal mobilization, thereby redefining U.S. "neutrality" not as non-participation but as the critical logistical rear guard for the Allied powers.
- **Thread — `mexican-intervention-escalation`:** Following the U.S. withdrawal from Veracruz, the power vacuum fractures central authority, empowering Pancho Villa and Emiliano Zapata to shift tactics from conventional engagement to asymmetric cross-border raiding, setting the stage for direct incursions onto U.S. soil that will test the limits of American military readiness.
- **Thread — `global-extraction-maximum`:** The Panama Canal, now fully operational, ceases to be merely a trade shortcut and becomes a singular strategic chokepoint; its defense against potential German sabotage dictates U.S. naval deployment patterns while accelerating the flow of raw materials and finished goods exclusively toward the Allied war machine.
- **Thread — `technological-stalemate`:** The nullification of offensive infantry tactics by defensive machine-gun density drives a state-sponsored R&D arms race in 1915, moving beyond incremental improvements to the desperate deployment of chemical weapons and early armored prototypes, establishing industrial innovation as the primary determinant of battlefield survival.

## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — The Lusitania Sinking Aftermath (May 7, 1915)
- **What happened (fact):** German U-boat U-20 torpedoed the British passenger liner RMS Lusitania off the Irish coast; 1,198 died including 128 Americans. The ship sank in 18 minutes. Germany defended the attack citing the vessel's alleged munitions cargo and the war zone warning published in American newspapers; Britain and American outrage focused on the civilian deaths.
- **The narrative *then*:** "Innocent America assaulted by Hunnish barbarism." The Lusitania became instant martyrology: mothers and children, neutral citizens, murdered by a faceless submarine war that respected no law of civilization. Theodore Roosevelt thundered that Wilson's "too proud to fight" note was "unmanly." The German-American press and Midwestern progressives counter-narrated: the ship sailed into a declared war zone carrying contraband; British blockade starved German civilians equally. The "preparedness" movement seized the moment as proof that America lived undefended in a predator's world. For Wilson, the crisis was theological: how to punish without entering, to vindicate righteous anger without surrendering the peace that would redeem Europe.
- **The narrative *now*:** The Lusitania occupies a liminal, almost forgotten space—neither Pearl Harbor's clear causality nor the Maine's acknowledged ambiguity. Textbooks mention it as "one cause" of eventual war entry, often with the munitions revelation as retroactive complication. The dominant memory is of Wilson's patient restraint, not the crowd's bloodlust. The ship itself, discovered with confirmed munitions holds, serves as vague parable about propaganda's manufacture. What persists is the template: civilian vessel, foreign attack, moral outrage, presidential calibration—rehearsed for future crises.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The moment when neutrality became performative rather than substantive. Wilson's three notes to Germany (May-June 1915) invented the modern rhetoric of "credibility"—the threat that must be made credible without being executed. The Lusitania crisis trained Americans to experience war-avoidance as national virtue and simultaneously as national humiliation, a tension that would resolve only in 1917.

#### US-2 — Formation of the Council of National Defense (Late 1915)
- **What happened (fact):** Secretary of War Lindley Garrison and Navy Secretary Josephus Daniels, with Wilson's approval, convened industrialists including Howard E. Coffin and Bernard Baruch in informal councils to assess military-industrial capacity. This preceded the official Council of National Defense established by act of Congress in August 1916. The 1915 meetings mapped supply chains, identified production bottlenecks, and established relationships between military planners and corporate leadership previously separated by laissez-faire convention.
- **The narrative *then*:** "Preparedness" as muscular citizenship. The National Security League and Theodore Roosevelt's agitation framed industrial mobilization as democratic virility—the antidote to Wilson's effeminate pacifism. For participating businessmen, it was public-spirited patriotism, the duty of American productive genius. To antiwar progressives like Robert La Follette, it was the conspiracy they feared: Wall Street and the War Department merging in back rooms, democracy sold for munitions contracts. The narrative's genius was its ambiguity: it could be read as insurance against war or preparation for it, and both readings served to normalize the collaboration.
- **The narrative *now*:** A footnote to the "dollar-a-year men" of 1917-1918, or to the military-industrial complex narrative that crystallized after 1945. The 1915 origins are rarely traced; the story of state-industry fusion begins for most Americans with World War II's Arsenal of Democracy or Eisenhower's farewell warning. When acknowledged, it carries the benign glow of "we weren't ready for 1917"—a technical failure corrected by foresight, not a structural transformation of political economy.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The invisible pivot. Before the Council, American capitalism and American warfare operated in formal separation; after, the boundary became managerial, not constitutional. This is where the "dollar-a-year man," the cost-plus contract, the revolving door between Pentagon and boardroom were first normalized. The narrative of 1915 as "mere planning" obscures that the planning assumed war's inevitability and constructed the institutional pathway to make it so.

#### US-3 — The Eastland Disaster (July 24, 1915)
- **What happened (fact):** The SS Eastland, a passenger ship chartered by Western Electric for a company picnic, capsized at its Chicago dock before departing; 844 died, predominantly young Czech and Polish immigrant women workers. The ship's instability was known—top-heavy from 1903 reconstruction, further destabilized by recent lifeboat additions mandated after the Titanic. No storm, no enemy; the ship simply rolled over in calm water, its passengers trapped beneath or drowned in the Chicago River.
- **The narrative *then*:** Local tragedy, quickly contained. Western Electric's picnic was annual, respectable; the victims were the respectable immigrant working class, not the radical proletariat of Haymarket memory. The Chicago press focused on rescue heroics, the "identification nightmare" at the morgue, the charitable response. The narrative of corporate negligence competed briefly with the narrative of inexplicable accident—"the ship that turned turtle" as freak event. The lifeboat irony (regulation killing those it meant to save) surfaced but found no sustained audience. The ethnic press mourned; the mainstream moved on. No legislative reform followed; the Titanic's 1,503 dead had produced SOLAS, the Eastland's 844 produced silence.
- **The narrative *now*:** Near-total erasure. The Eastland does not appear in national memory, even as Chicago's deadliest disaster. When mentioned, it is as "the other maritime disaster of 1915," a dark joke about the Lusitania's shadow. The immigrant identity of victims, the corporate structure of the outing, the regulatory irony—all dissolved into local history, genealogical curiosity, true-crime podcast material. The disaster's non-narrative is itself the narrative: some deaths serve national mythology, others are administrative casualties.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The Eastland exposes the selective machinery of martyrdom. The Lusitania's 128 Americans became world-historical; the Eastland's 844 immigrants became obituary space. The disaster reveals that 1915's "preparedness" included preparing to waste certain lives—immigrant, female, industrial—with no narrative compensation. It is the unstoried corollary to the Council of National Defense's celebrated efficiency: the human cost of mobilization logistics before mobilization itself.

#### W-1 — First Use of Poison Gas at Ypres (April 22, 1915)
- **What happened (fact):** German forces released 168 tons of chlorine gas from 5,730 cylinders along a four-mile front near Ypres, Belgium. The cloud, carried by wind toward French colonial (Algerian and Senegalese) and Canadian divisions, caused approximately 7,000 casualties, 1,100 fatal. The Hague Convention of 1907 had prohibited "poison or poisoned weapons"; Germany defended the gas as a "lachrymatory" irritant, not poison, and as retaliation for Allied shelling of German civilians. Both sides developed and deployed chemical weapons thereafter.
- **The narrative *then*:** "The Hun's newest atrocity." For Allied populations, gas crystallized the enemy's civilizational regression—war against breath itself, the soldier reduced to drowning on his feet, the medical response helpless before chemistry. The German press framed it as technological progress, "humane" in causing fewer deaths than artillery (a claim with statistical basis). The colonial troops' disproportionate suffering—Algerian units breaking, Canadian units holding—was read through racial hierarchy: African "panic" versus white discipline. Gas masks became iconography; the "gas gong" and the donning drill, ritual responses to industrialized terror.
- **The narrative *now:** Gas occupies ambiguous moral territory. The Geneva Protocol of 1925 and Chemical Weapons Convention of 1993 frame it as paradigmatic war crime, yet its 1915 use is often narrated as "both sides did it" within months—unlike genocide or terror bombing, gas lost its unique stigma through reciprocity. The Holocaust's gas chambers (Zyklon B developed by IG Farben, successor to the Ypres manufacturers) created retrospective contamination: 1915 gas becomes prefiguration, the technology that would find its logical end. The colonial dimension is erased; the Canadian resistance at Ypres is commemorated, the Algerian collapse unmentioned.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The moment war's industrial logic outpaced its moral vocabulary. Gas was simultaneously atrocity and "rational" weapon—more survivable than shellfire, more terrifying than bullets. The narrative's struggle to categorize it (crime or combat, regression or progress) reveals 1915's central crisis: the Hague system of civilized warfare was not violated but transcended, made irrelevant by technical capacity. The gas mask, that emblem of modern soldiering, was also the first admission that war now required citizens to become mechanical.

#### W-2 — The Armenian Genocide Begins (April 24, 1915)
- **What happened (fact):** Ottoman authorities arrested approximately 250 Armenian intellectuals, professionals, and community leaders in Constantinople, most subsequently deported and killed. This initiated systematic deportation of Armenians from Anatolia into the Syrian desert, accompanied by mass killing, starvation, and concentration camps. Estimates of dead range from 600,000 to 1.5 million. The Ottoman government claimed relocation of a disloyal population during wartime emergency; German diplomatic cables recorded the genocidal intent.
- **The narrative *then*:** "Armenian massacres"—the term "genocide" did not yet exist. Allied propaganda seized the atrocity as proof of Turkish/German barbarism, often with the same Orientalist framing that had greeted Bulgarian horrors in 1876. American missionaries and diplomats (Ambassador Henry Morgenthau foremost) provided eyewitness testimony that circulated through church networks and The New York Times. The narrative's power was limited by strategic context: Britain used Armenian suffering to rally opinion but abandoned them at Sevres; America debated humanitarian relief but not intervention. For Turks, the deportation was survival necessity against a fifth column; the deaths, regrettable wartime excess. The word "genocide" would be coined in 1943 by Raphael Lemkin, who explicitly cited Armenia as precedent.
- **The narrative *now*:** The Armenian Genocide is recognized by 30+ nations and most historians, denied by Turkey as "so-called genocide" or wartime relocation with tragic casualties. American recognition came only in 2019 (House), 2021 (Biden presidential statement)—delayed by NATO alliance, Turkish geopolitical weight, and the word's legal gravity. The "never again" narrative of Holocaust education rarely extends backward; Armenia sits uneasily as "first modern genocide," challenging the Holocaust's framing as unique civilizational rupture. The missionary-witness tradition (Morgenthau, Near East Relief) persists as American humanitarian self-image, detached from the non-intervention that accompanied it.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The foundational case of a term that would reshape international law and moral vocabulary. 1915 is when sovereign violence against a defined population became systematic, documented, and conceptually unnameable—requiring a new word, a new legal category, a new humanitarian politics. The denial industry born here (minimization, relativization, whataboutism) became template for subsequent genocides. The gap between "massacre" then and "genocide" now measures the century's moral learning and its political constraints.

#### W-3 — Italy Enters the War via Treaty of London (April 26, 1915)
- **What happened (fact):** Italy, technically allied to Germany and Austria-Hungary since 1882 but declaring neutrality in August 1914, signed the secret Treaty of London with Britain, France, and Russia. In exchange for entering the war against the Central Powers within one month, Italy was promised substantial territorial gains: Trentino, South Tyrol, Trieste, Istria, Dalmatian coast, Albanian protectorate, colonial compensation in Africa/Asia Minor. The treaty was published by the Bolsheviks in 1917, embarrassing the Allied self-presentation. Italy entered May 23, 1915.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Italian nationalists, "radiant May"—the completion of Risorgimento, the redemption of Italia irredenta, the fourth war of independence. D'Annunzio's oratory, Mussolini's rupture with socialist neutrality, the interventionist street violence of Milan and Rome: the narrative was of democratic will overcoming parliamentary caution, of the nation awakening to its destiny. The secret territorial promises were known to informed circles, suppressed in nationalist telling. For the Allied public, Italy's entry was "the weight that turns the scale," a moral affirmation of their cause's justice. The Socialist International's collapse—Italian workers joining capitalist war—was mourned by Lenin, celebrated by nationalists.
- **The narrative *now*:** Italy's entry is taught as "secret treaties" case study in realpolitik, the antithesis of Wilsonian self-determination. The territorial promises—many unfulfilled, others (South Tyrol) becoming future fascist grievance and post-1945 conflict—embody the "old diplomacy" Wilson would supposedly abolish. The "mutilated victory" narrative, fascism's founding grievance, traces directly to London's unredeemed promises. The treaty appears in textbooks as proof that World War I was not "war to end war" but imperial competition, Wilson's idealism either naive or hypocritical depending on interpretive stance.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The most explicit demonstration that 1915's war expansion was driven by territorial bargaining, not democratic or moral principle. The Treaty's secrecy and its subsequent exposure by the Bolsheviks created the "secret diplomacy" trope that would discredit the entire pre-Wilsonian order. For American inheritance: the Treaty of London is the negative space against which Wilson's Fourteen Points defined themselves, and the template of intervention-for-territory that would haunt subsequent American alliance-making from Yalta to NATO expansion.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected to expose the structural collapse of "neutrality" and the rise of total industrial war, bypassing the famous but narratively hollow Battle of Gallipoli or the ongoing stalemate at the Marne. The *Lusitania* is included not for the tragedy itself but for how it forced the U.S. financial system to align with British survival; the *Eastland* disaster serves as a grim domestic counterpoint to foreign battlefields, revealing how the machinery of mobilization killed civilians even without enemy action; and the *Armenian Genocide* alongside *Ypres* marks the year the world abandoned previous moral and legal constraints on sovereignty and combat. I omitted the Battle of Loos or specific Mexican raids because they are tactical echoes of the deeper structural shifts visible in the weaponization of chemistry, the securitization of supply chains, and the legal dissolution of neutral rights.

## 2. Connections Within the Year

- **U.S. ↔ World:** The *Lusitania* sinking and the First Use of Poison Gas at Ypres are not parallel tragedies but convergent symptoms of the collapse of the 19th-century liberal order; both events demonstrate how industrial technology had outpaced the legal and moral frameworks (neutrality rights, Hague Conventions) designed to contain them, forcing the United States to confront a world where "civilized" warfare no longer existed.
- **Event ↔ Event:** The *Eastland* disaster and the Armenian Genocide share a hidden structural logic of "disposable populations" within mobilization states; in Chicago, Swedish immigrants were killed by the rush to prepare for a war they hadn't entered due to negligent regulation, while in Anatolia, Armenians were exterminated by a state viewing them as an internal security threat to imperial integrity, revealing how the pressure of total war devalues human life regardless of intent.
- **Invisible / structural (shared hidden drivers):** The Treaty of London and the formation of the Council of National Defense expose the primacy of the *Industrial-Imperial Complex*; both events prove that by 1915, the capacity to wage war (through secret territorial bargaining or domestic manufacturing coordination) had superseded democratic deliberation, locking nations into a path where economic integration made neutrality increasingly theoretical and conflict inevitable.

## 3. The Debate

### Round 1 — Opening Interpretations (uninterrupted)

#### 🜂 Herald
The year 1915 is the year the modern world learned to breathe poison and call it atmosphere. Everywhere I look, I see the same operation: the construction of stories robust enough to survive their own atrocities, the assembly of belief-systems that would outlast the facts that birthed them and the people they consumed.

Consider the Lusitania, May 7th. What did they believe, those who stood on American piers reading the headlines? They believed they had witnessed a civilizational crime—the submarine as emblem of a new, faceless, murderous warfare that recognized no distinction between soldier and civilian, neutral and belligerent. This belief was true enough; it was also immediately weaponized. The preparedness movement, the navalists, the Anglophile internationalists, all seized the dead as argument. Theodore Roosevelt's "too proud to fight" sneer at Wilson captured the era's gendered grammar of national virtue: restraint was effeminacy, protest without punishment was dishonor. Yet look at the counter-belief, equally felt: the German-American press, the Midwestern progressives, the anti-imperialists who noted the munitions cargo, the war-zone warning, the British blockade's parallel starvation of German civilians. Both narratives were available in 1915; only one would be remembered, and that memory would be selective even in victory. The Lusitania's power as myth was not its clarity but its ambiguity—it could mean "war now" for Roosevelt and "patient righteousness" for Wilson, and both readings would serve the eventual entry of 1917.

The Eastland, six weeks later, reveals the selection mechanism. Eight hundred forty-four dead, mostly young immigrant women, in calm water at a company picnic—no enemy, no heroism, no usable narrative. The Lusitania's 128 Americans became world-historical martyrs; the Eastland's 844 became administrative casualties, their ethnic names too difficult

#### 🜄 Heretic
The received story of 1915 is a morality tale in three acts: German barbarism shatters civilized norms, American innocence is violated on the high seas, and the machinery of righteous response begins its slow, reluctant grinding toward justice. Poison gas at Ypres proves the enemy’s inhumanity; the Armenian genocide proves the stakes of Allied victory; the Lusitania proves that even neutrals cannot escape evil’s reach. Italy’s entry and America’s industrial preparation are the gathering of the virtuous, the necessary hardening of democratic societies against autocratic threat. The Eastland is a tragedy, yes, but a local one—a footnote to the grand narrative of mobilization. This is the story that would carry America into war in 1917 and sustain its self-image for a century. It is not false in its particulars. It is false in what it omits, and the omissions are not accidents.

Let us begin with the dead, because the dead cannot speak and that is precisely the point. The Armenian genocide—the term itself would not exist yet, but the operation was already systematic—began on April 24 with the arrest of intellectuals in Constantinople. By year’s end, hundreds of thousands were dead, deported, marched into the Syrian desert, their property seized, their women raped and enslaved. The received story treats this as a discrete Ottoman atrocity, a precursor to the Holocaust, a moral outrage that the Allies would later vow to punish. What it does not treat is the architecture of permission. The Ottoman state acted with the knowledge that no power would intervene—not Russia, bogged down at Gallipoli; not Britain, whose own colonial record in India and Ireland demonstrated that civilian populations were legitimate targets when they obstructed imperial interests; not Germany, the Ottoman ally, whose military advisors were embedded in the command structure and whose silence was purchased by strategic necessity. The genocide was not a breakdown of sovereignty but its fullest expression: the state deciding which populations belonged to the body politic and which were tumors to be excised. This is the lesson the twentieth century would learn and learn again. The comfortable version lets us call it “Ottoman” and leave it there, as if the logic of population removal were not already being perfected by every imperial power in the room. Cui bono from the silence? Every state that would someday need the same tools.

Now Ypres, April 22. The Germans opened canisters of chlorine gas and watched a green-yellow cloud drift toward French colonial troops—Algerians, Moroccans—who broke and ran, leaving a four-mile gap in the line. The Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907 had explicitly prohibited “poison or poisoned weapons.” The received story is that Germany shattered a civilized norm, and this is factually correct. But what is omitted is that the norm was already hollow. The British blockade of Germany, in full effect by 1915, was starving civilians by design—food was declared contraband, and the Royal Navy’s distant blockade violated every prewar understanding of maritime law as thoroughly as the submarine violated cruiser rules. The blockade would kill an estimated 400,000 to 800,000 German civilians by 1918, mostly the old, the young, the poor. This was not gas; it was slower, less photogenic, and it allowed the blockaders to maintain the fiction that they were not targeting civilians at all, merely interdicting the enemy’s economy. The distance between the rhetoric and the action is the whole story. Gas was visible atrocity, and visibility is what makes a war crime a war crime. The blockade was structural atrocity, and structure is what lets empires sleep at night. Both sides were already practicing total war against civilian populations; Ypres merely made it literal.

The Lusitania, May 7. The steelman is powerful: a passenger liner, carrying innocent civilians, torpedoed without warning by a German U-boat, 1,198 dead including 128 Americans, an act of “murder on the high seas” that revealed the Kaiser’s regime as a rogue state beyond the pale of civilization. This is what Americans believed, what Wilson’s diplomatic notes insisted, what the British propaganda apparatus amplified worldwide. Now the facts the comfortable version buries. The Lusitania was carrying munitions—4.2 million rounds of rifle ammunition, 1,248 cases of shrapnel shells, fuses, gun cotton—in violation of American law, which prohibited the carriage of explosives on passenger vessels. The German embassy had placed warnings in New York newspapers the morning of departure, explicitly stating that vessels flying the British flag in the war zone were liable to attack. The ship was listed as an auxiliary cruiser in Jane’s Fighting Ships. The Admiralty, under Churchill, had issued orders that merchant ships should ram submarines rather than submit to search—effectively converting every British-flagged vessel into a combatant. None of this excuses the drowning of children. It does, however, reframe the question: cui bono from the story that suppressed these facts? The British government, which needed American entry into the war and systematically managed the inquiry to avoid the munitions question. The American preparedness movement, which needed a martyr. The Wilson administration, which needed moral cover for the economic tilt toward the Allies that had already made neutrality a fiction—by 1915, American loans to the Allies dwarfed those to the Central Powers, and the munitions trade was a one-way flow. The 128 dead Americans were not merely victims; they were instruments. Their deaths were leveraged, their families’ grief converted into political capital, and the structural complicity of their own government in placing them in harm’s way was erased from the record. The absence in the archive is not an oversight; it is the archive’s function.

The Eastland, July 24. Here the selection mechanism is laid bare. A ship chartered for a Western Electric company picnic, loaded with employees and their families—mostly Czech, Polish, Swedish immigrants, the industrial working class of Chicago—rolled over at the dock in the Chicago River, killing 844 people, many of them young women and children. They drowned in twenty feet of water, trapped below decks, while thousands watched from the riverbank. The ship had been retrofitted with additional lifeboats after the Titanic, making it top-heavy and unstable; its ballast tanks were improperly filled; its owners had ignored repeated warnings about its seaworthiness. The received story has no place for the Eastland because it fits no usable narrative. There is no foreign enemy, no heroism, no geopolitical consequence. The dead were not the right kind of dead—not Americans in the national sense but immigrants, not martyrs to German perfidy but casualties of the same industrial capitalism that was about to be mobilized for war. The Eastland was a troop ship, one of several being converted for the preparedness effort, and the pressure to maximize capacity, to cut corners on safety, to treat human beings as cargo—this was not a bug in the mobilization system but its operating logic. The investigation would find no criminal liability. The owners would pay nothing. The dead would be buried in mass graves, their names misrecorded, their families uncompensated. The lesson of the Eastland is that some deaths count and some do not, and the difference is not the scale of the tragedy but its political utility. The Lusitania’s dead were weapons; the Eastland’s dead were waste.

The Council of National Defense, taking shape in late 1915, is where these threads converge. The official story is one of prudent preparation: industrial leaders and military planners beginning the coordination that would, by 1917, produce the War Industries Board, the rationalization of production, the partnership between government and business that won the war. This is true. What it omits is that this coordination was not neutral technocracy but the deliberate construction of a permanent war economy. The men in those rooms—the DuPonts, the steel executives, the railroad magnates—were not patriots setting aside profit for the nation; they were capitalists recognizing that the state was about to become their largest customer, and they intended to set the terms. The Council of National Defense was the moment when the American state abandoned even the pretense of laissez-faire separation from industry, not in the service of the public good but in the service of a specific class of industrialists who would emerge from the war vastly richer and more powerful than they entered it. The distance between the rhetoric of “national defense” and the action of “profit guarantee” would be measured in the billions of dollars of cost-plus contracts, the suspension of antitrust enforcement, the deliberate suppression of labor organizing under the banner of patriotism. The inheritance is direct: the military-industrial complex does not begin with Eisenhower’s farewell address; it begins here, in 1915, when the men who owned the means of violence sat down with the men who owned the means of production and discovered they had common interests.

Italy’s entry into the war, via the secret Treaty of London signed on April 26, is the most honest event of the year, because it barely bothered with a moral pretext. Italy had been allied with Germany and Austria-Hungary since 1882; it entered the war on the Allied side because Britain and France offered it territorial spoils—the Trentino, South Tyrol, Trieste, Istria, parts of Dalmatia, colonial concessions in Africa and the Near East—that Austria could not match. There was no democratic deliberation, no popular mandate; the Italian parliament was not consulted, the treaty was negotiated in secret by the foreign minister and the king, and the decision for war was presented as a fait accompli to a largely hostile public. The received story treats this as Realpolitik, the normal cynicism of great-power diplomacy. But the omission is the point: Italy’s entry demonstrates that the war was never about Belgian neutrality or the rights of small nations or democracy versus autocracy. It was about imperial map-making, the redistribution of territory and resources among competing empires, and the populations being traded like poker chips—the South Tyroleans who would be severed from Austria, the Dalmatian Slavs who would be handed from one imperial master to another—had no say in the matter. The rhetoric of national self-determination that Wilson would later articulate was already being violated by his own allies, in secret, before America even entered the war. The silence in the archive is the silence of the people who lived on the land being bargained over.

What is the inheritance for the United States? It is the architecture of managed memory. The year 1915 taught American elites a lesson they have never forgotten: that atrocity can be alchemized into consent, that the dead can be sorted into the useful and the disposable, that the state can partner with industry to build a permanent war economy and call it preparedness, that secret diplomacy and imperial horse-trading can be laundered through moral rhetoric until they smell like principle. The specific mechanism—the suppression of the Lusitania’s cargo, the erasure of the Eastland, the silence around the blockade, the ratification of genocide by inaction—is the mechanism that would operate again in 1964, in 2003, in every moment when the comfortable version of events required certain facts to be forgotten and certain voices to be silenced. The question is not whether the story is true. The question is whose interests the story serves, and what it lets us avoid feeling responsible for today.

#### 🜃 Cartographer
The year 1915 is not a turning point because of what happened, but because of what became impossible: the separation of the economy from the state, and the belief that geography could insulate power from consequence. The six events of this year map the closing of the exit doors. The structural variable driving this closure is the maturation of industrial capacity to a point where it demands totalizing political expression. In 1915, the slow variables of demography and technology finally crushed the fast variable of diplomatic maneuvering.

Consider the Atlantic and the Western Front as a single connected system of industrial attrition. The sinking of the *Lusitania* (US-1) and the deployment of chlorine gas at Ypres (W-1) are often treated as moral outrages, but structurally, they are identical: they represent the moment when the tools of mass production breached the container of international law. Germany's submarine warfare was not merely a tactical choice but a geographic necessity imposed by the British blockade; it was an attempt to use industrial efficiency to overcome geographic encirclement. Similarly, gas was not "barbarism" in a vacuum; it was the logical endpoint of a stalemate where chemical engineering became the only variable left to alter the static equation of the trenches. For the United States, the *Lusitania* forced a realization that neutrality was no longer a legal status one could claim, but a physical condition one had to defend—a defense that required the very industrial mobilization beginning in Washington.

This brings us to the internal architecture of the American state. The formation of the Council of National Defense (US-2) is the quietest yet most devastating event on this list. It marks the death of laissez-faire as a governing principle. Here, the structural driver is the recognition that modern war is a contest of logistics, not just valor. The council formalized the merger of capital and state, creating a feedback loop where industrial leaders would plan for wars that their own production capabilities might soon necessitate. This is not conspiracy; it is path dependence. Once the state accepts that it must coordinate steel, rail, and munitions to survive in a hostile world, it creates an institutional inertia that favors intervention over isolation. The *Eastland* disaster (US-3) serves as the grim footnote to this transition: the capsizing occurred because a ship, refitted for naval militia drills under the pressure of preparedness, was top-heavy with regulations ignored in the rush to mobilize. The 844 dead were casualties not of enemy action, but of the friction generated when a peacetime society attempts to pivot its heavy industry toward war without fully acknowledging the human cost of that friction.

Globally, the map is being redrawn by the same logic of imperial necessity that drives American industrial coordination. The Treaty of London (W-3), which pulled Italy into the war through secret territorial promises, reveals that the conflict had expanded beyond national defense into a renegotiation of the global order. Democracies were behaving like empires because the structural demands of the war machine required resources and fronts that public opinion alone would never authorize. This connects directly to the Armenian Genocide (W-2). While distinct in its genocidal intent, the Ottoman action shares a structural root with the Treaty of London: the belief that the survival of the state in a total war environment justifies the removal of any population deemed a potential fifth column or an obstacle to geographic continuity.
