## 0. Context Carried Forward

- **Thread — `constitutional-ratification-battle`:** Entering 1867, the Fourteenth Amendment remains in legislative limbo; Southern legislatures have uniformly rejected it, forcing the Radical Republican coalition to pivot from persuasion to coercion, preparing to make ratification a statutory prerequisite for congressional representation rather than a voluntary act of reunion.
- **Thread — `military-reconstruction-imminent`:** The theoretical framework for occupation has hardened into specific legislation; the failure of civilian courts to protect freedpeople and the President's obstruction have cleared the path for the First Reconstruction Act, which will formally dissolve existing Southern state governments and parcel the region into five military districts commanded by Union generals.
- **Thread — `labor-coercion-escalation`:** As the 1866 harvest concluded, the transition from explicit Black Codes to economic entrapment accelerated; with no federal land redistribution occurring, the structural necessity for plantation labor is driving the rapid crystallization of sharecropping and crop-lien systems that will bind Black workers to white landowners through debt rather than statute by year's end.
- **Thread — `executive-legislative-war`:** The conflict has moved past policy vetoes into the mechanics of removal; Congress is actively drafting the Tenure of Office Act specifically to trap Andrew Johnson, transforming the separation of powers into a tactical minefield where the preservation of Reconstruction depends on the survival of a single cabinet secretary.
- **Thread — `global-capital-shift`:** The diplomatic crisis with France over Mexico has reached its tipping point, compelling the U.S. to leverage its growing financial credibility to force a European withdrawal, while simultaneously initiating the Alaska purchase to signal a shift from continental consolidation to Pacific-facing imperial expansion, aligning Northern capital with new geopolitical frontiers.

## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — First Reconstruction Act (March 2, 1867)
- **What happened (fact):** Overriding Johnson's veto, Congress divides the ten unreconstructed Southern states into five military districts and mandates new constitutions guaranteeing black male suffrage as a condition for readmission.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Radical Republicans, this is the fulfillment of Union sacrifice—the "new birth of freedom" made concrete through bayonets and ballots. They cast it as completing Lincoln's unfinished work, securing the franchise for men who fought and died for the Republic. For Johnson and his white Southern allies, it is unconstitutional military despotism, a "congressional Reconstruction" that betrays the Constitution's federal design and reduces sovereign states to conquered provinces. For freedmen, it arrives as deliverance deferred—emancipation's promise finally given political form, though many understand immediately that protection without economic foundation remains fragile. The dominant Northern story is one of *experiment*: can a biracial democracy be compelled by force of arms?
- **The narrative *now*:** The textbook sentence calls this "Radical Reconstruction," a well-meaning but doomed attempt at social engineering that collapsed under corruption and Southern white resistance. The national myth frames it as noble overreach—idealism without pragmatism—while the fuller story of Black political mobilization during Reconstruction remains marginal, recovered only by specialized scholarship. The anniversary speech would praise "expanding the franchise" without dwelling on the violence that reversed it. The emotional work is to let Americans believe their democracy was *temporarily* flawed, now repaired, rather than systematically subverted through terrorism and complicity.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the hinge year of Reconstruction's radical turn—the moment congressional will overrode presidential obstruction and Black suffrage became federal policy rather than local option. Every subsequent voting rights struggle descends from this contested origin.

#### US-2 — Alaska Purchase Treaty (March 30, 1867)
- **What happened (fact):** Secretary of State William Seward secures the acquisition of Russian America for $7.2 million, expanding U.S. territorial sovereignty into the North Pacific despite widespread domestic derision as "Seward's Folly."
- **The narrative *then*:** Seward and the expansionist press frame this as strategic masterwork—securing the continent's northwestern flank, preempting British encroachment, acquiring resources yet unknown. The derision ("Seward's Folly," "Seward's Icebox," "Walrussia") captures a public exhausted by territorial acquisition, skeptical of distant possessions, and suspicious of executive diplomacy conducted without popular mandate. The story then is bifurcated: a small circle of geopolitical visionaries against a populist majority seeing only frozen waste. For Seward personally, it is the consummation of a career spent dreaming of American commercial empire—Canada, the Caribbean, Hawaii, now the North Pacific rim.
- **The narrative *now*:** The national myth has fully inverted: Seward the prophet, the skeptics as myopic fools. "Seward's Folly" survives only as irony, the setup for a punchline about strategic foresight. The narrative now performs the work of justifying *all* territorial expansion as eventual wisdom—each mocked acquisition (Louisiana, Alaska, Hawaii) proof that democratic skepticism is parochial, that elite vision must override popular hesitation. The gold and oil discoveries retroactively bless the purchase, but the deeper myth is about American inevitability: the continent, then the hemisphere, then the Pacific, was always ours by geographical destiny.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the last territorial acquisition of the continental/near-continental phase, completing the contiguous empire while opening the Pacific frontier that leads to Hawaii, the Philippines, and the twentieth-century naval strategy of Alfred Thayer Mahan.

#### US-3 — Tenure of Office Act (March 2, 1867)
- **What happened (fact):** Congress passes this legislation over Johnson's veto to prohibit the President from removing certain officeholders without Senate approval, laying the direct statutory trap that will trigger his impeachment the following year.
- **The narrative *then*:** Republicans present this as constitutional restoration—the Framers' intent, corrupted by Jacksonian "rotation in office," now corrected to protect executive subordinates from partisan purge. The true target is transparent: Secretary of War Stanton, Radical Republican ally, whom Johnson seeks to remove to install a compliant military commander in the South. The narrative then is legalistic theater over raw political struggle—both sides claiming constitutional fidelity while maneuvering for control of Reconstruction policy. For observers, it exemplifies the crisis of divided government: can a president govern against a hostile supermajority? Can Congress govern through statutory traps rather than persuasion?
- **The narrative *now*:** The national memory compresses this into "the act Johnson violated," a technicality in the impeachment story rather than a constitutional controversy in its own right. Textbooks note it was later repealed and its restrictions declared unconstitutional *dicta* in *Myers v. United States* (1926), confirming the modern executive's removal power. The story now serves to make Johnson's impeachment seem *legally* dubious even if *morally* justified—allowing Americans to have it both ways, condemning Johnson while doubting the Radical methods. The emotional work is preserving presidential supremacy as norm, making congressional assertiveness seem aberrant.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the most direct institutional confrontation between branches since the 1830s, setting the precedent (not followed until 1998) that impeachment can be deployed as partisan weapon against policy disagreement rather than proven crime.

#### W-1 — Execution of Emperor Maximilian (June 19, 1867)
- **What happened (fact):** Following the withdrawal of French troops under U.S. diplomatic and military pressure, Austrian Archduke Maximilian is executed by Mexican republican forces, ending European monarchical intervention in the Americas.
- **The narrative *then*:** In the United States, this is vindication of the Monroe Doctrine made flesh—European monarchy expelled by republican arms and American resolve. The execution itself produces ambivalence: Maximilian becomes a tragic figure in Northern papers, the naive idealist sacrificed to Napoleon's cynicism and Juárez's implacability. The story then is layered: diplomatic triumph (French withdrawal secured), ideological confirmation (monarchy cannot root in American soil), but also unease at the firing squad's finality. For Seward and the State Department, it completes the post-Civil War assertion of hemispheric primacy. For the public, it offers a foreign adventure with American moral credit but no American blood—a rare satisfaction.
- **The narrative *now*:** The national memory has largely discarded this episode; when remembered, it appears as colorful background to Juárez's heroism or Maximilian's romance, with the U.S. role minimized. The Monroe Doctrine itself has been demoted from living principle to historical curiosity, its twentieth-century deployments (Guatemala, Cuba, Chile) having discredited the original anti-colonial framing. The story now, if told, serves to illustrate American *restraint*—we pressured, we did not invade—rather than American hegemony. The emotional work is preserving a self-image of anti-imperialism incompatible with the actual record.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the decisive demonstration that the post-Civil War United States possesses the coercive capacity to enforce its declared hemispheric doctrine against a major European power, transforming Monroe's warning into Seward's reality.

#### W-2 — British North America Act (July 1, 1867)
- **What happened (fact):** The British Parliament unites three separate colonies into the Dominion of Canada, creating a federal counterweight on the northern border and altering the strategic calculus of U.S. continental expansion.
- **The narrative *then*:** In the United States, this registers as ambiguous—simultaneously frustrating Manifest Destiny's northern vector and confirming American republican contagion: even monarchical Britain must grant responsible government to its colonists. The expansionist press notes the defensive motive plainly: Canadians unite against potential American annexation, the Fenian raids having demonstrated border vulnerability. The story then is competitive: a federal rival emerges, but one organized on principles Americans recognize. For Seward, who dreamed of absorbing Canada, this is setback; for moderate unionists, it is acceptable—British withdrawal from North America proceeds, even if not through absorption. The deeper American belief is that time favors union: Canada will come eventually, as Texas did, as California did.
- **The narrative *now*:** The national memory barely registers 1867 as Canadian founding; "Canada" appears fully formed in textbooks, its origin myth belonging to Ottawa, not Washington. When noticed, the BNA Act serves as foil—peaceful evolution versus violent American expansion, constitutional monarchy versus republican rupture. The emotional work for Americans is comfortable superiority: we need not know their origin because ours is the significant one. For Canadian identity, conversely, this is constitutive—resistance to American assimilation as nation-building. The asymmetry itself is the inheritance: two North American federalisms, one hegemonic, one persistently defensive.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This creates the permanent northern boundary condition of American power—a continental rival too similar to despise easily, too integrated to conquer safely, too British to absorb culturally. Every subsequent U.S.-Canada relationship descends from this founding asymmetry.

#### W-3 — Austro-Hungarian Compromise (March 30, 1867)
- **What happened (fact):** Following defeat by Prussia, the Habsburg Empire restructures into a dual monarchy, stabilizing Central Europe enough to redirect attention away from the Western Hemisphere and solidifying the U.S. position as the hemispheric hegemon.
- **The narrative *then*:** This registers barely in American consciousness—a European dynastic rearrangement, distant and obscure. For the diplomatic cognoscenti (Seward, Charles Francis Adams, the emerging professional foreign service), it signals the European system settling into new equilibrium: Prussia ascendant, France diminished, Austria-Hungary withdrawn to internal consolidation. The American story then is of *relief*: the great powers distracted, the Western Hemisphere open for unilateral assertion. The Compromise's date—coincident with the Alaska purchase—is not noted as meaningful; American attention is on Pacific expansion, not Danubian federalism. The deeper belief, unarticulated, is that European power politics are exhaustively complex while American expansion is straightforwardly geographical.
- **The narrative *now*:** National memory has effaced this entirely; the Compromise belongs to European history, Habsburg specialists, perhaps the prehistory of World War I. The American inheritance is indirect but profound: the European balance that permitted unilateral hemispheric assertion, then collapsed in 1914 to draw the United States into global power. The story now, if told, would be about American innocence of European complexity—our 1867 inattention as seed of 1917 surprise. The emotional work is preserving the myth of optional engagement: we intervened only when Europe failed, not because our own rise helped shape the system that failed.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the structural condition enabling American hemispheric hegemony—the great powers' European preoccupation that makes Monroe Doctrine enforcement possible, that purchases the United States a half-century of regional dominance before global responsibility arrives.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected because they collectively map the transition from legal theory to enforced reality: the U.S. events demonstrate the legislative weaponization of the military and the executive branch to break Southern resistance, while the Alaska purchase signals the pivot from internal repair to external projection. The global selections are not mere background; the removal of Maximilian and the stabilization of Europe via the Austro-Hungarian Compromise removed the external threats that had previously constrained U.S. action, allowing the Radical Republicans to turn their full attention inward without fear of foreign intervention. I deliberately omitted the Kansas suffrage referendums of late 1867; while significant for the fracture between abolitionist and women's suffrage movements, they lack the immediate structural leverage over the Southern political order and the international balance of power that defines the decisive shifts of this specific year.

## 2. Connections Within the Year

- **U.S. ↔ World:** The expulsion of French forces from Mexico (W-1) and the Austro-Hungarian Compromise (W-3) created a geopolitical vacuum in the Western Hemisphere that the United States was structurally positioned to fill, directly enabling the diplomatic confidence required for the Alaska Purchase (US-2) while simultaneously freeing Congressional bandwidth to enforce the radical restructuring of the South (US-1, US-3) without fear of European intervention.
- **Event ↔ Event:** The Tenure of Office Act (US-3) and the First Reconstruction Act (US-1) are twin manifestations of a single legislative offensive: Congress seized the mechanism of executive removal to ensure the military districts of the South would not be dismantled by presidential fiat, linking the survival of Black suffrage in the South directly to the constitutional crisis in Washington.
- **Invisible / structural (shared hidden drivers):** The global consolidation of the nation-state model—evident in Canada's federation (W-2), Austria-Hungary's dualism (W-3), and the U.S. military occupation of the South (US-1)—reveals a shared driver: the imperative to centralize sovereign authority and define citizenship through coercive state apparatuses, moving away from loose confederations or imperial proxies toward unified, bounded territorial entities with monopoly control over violence and voting rights.

#### 🜄 Heretic
I will grant the received story its due, because it is not false—it is merely incomplete in ways that serve power. Here is the steelman: 1867 is the year the United States, having survived a existential rebellion, turns to remake itself and the hemisphere on republican principles. The First Reconstruction Act extends the franchise to Black men in the South, the most radical democratic experiment of the nineteenth century. The Alaska Purchase removes a European autocracy from North American soil and extends the domain of liberty. The Tenure of Office Act, however ill-conceived, represents Congress asserting constitutional prerogative against a president whose sympathy for the defeated Confederacy threatened the war's settlement. Abroad, the execution of Maximilian ends the last European monarchical adventure in the Americas, the British North America Act creates a stable liberal neighbor to the north, and the Austro-Hungarian Compromise settles European turmoil in a way that keeps the great powers distracted—all clearing the field for American ascendancy. The narrative is one of democratic consolidation, continental destiny, and the vindication of republican government against both internal treason and external interference. It is a story Americans tell themselves to feel that the arc of their history bends toward justice, however slowly.

Now let us examine what this story omits, who benefits from the omission, and what the comfortable version lets us avoid feeling responsible for today.

**The First Reconstruction Act: cui bono from the story told about it?**

The official narrative centers Congress, the military, the franchise. It does not center the question of *land*. Thaddeus Stevens understood this in his dying months: political rights without economic foundation are rights that can be taken back the moment the federal bayonets withdraw. The Act divides the South into military districts and mandates Black male suffrage, but it does not touch the property relations that the war supposedly unsettled. The plantations remain in the hands of the same class that launched secession. The freedpeople are given the ballot and told to negotiate wages with the men who recently owned their bodies. This is not an oversight. It is the settlement that Northern capital required.

Follow the incentive: the Republican Party in 1867 is not a monolith of radical egalitarians. Its dominant wing represents Northern industrial and financial interests that need Southern cotton production restored, Southern markets reopened, and Southern labor disciplined—but no longer through chattel slavery, which is both politically toxic and economically less flexible than wage labor. The Reconstruction Acts create a South where Black labor is nominally free but structurally captive: tied to plantation agriculture through debt, contract law, and the absence of any alternative economic base. The Black Codes, which the Civil Rights Act of 1866 theoretically nullified, are already mutating into vagrancy laws and convict leasing—systems that will deliver cheap Black labor to railroads, mines, and plantations for generations. The franchise, genuine as it is, is meant to secure Republican political control, not Black economic independence. When that political control proves insufficient to protect Black citizenship, the North will not return. The story we inherit—"Reconstruction failed because of Southern intransigence and Northern fatigue"—obscures that Northern capital *chose* the restoration of the plantation economy over its destruction. The omission is the point.

Whose voice is absent from the record of the Reconstruction Acts? The freedpeople who asked for forty acres and a mule, who understood perfectly well that the ballot without the plow was a weapon they could not eat. Their petitions, their conventions, their clear-eyed demand for land redistribution are preserved in the archive—but they are treated as supplementary, as the unrealistic hopes of the newly freed, rather than as the central political wisdom of the moment. The silence in the textbook is not an accident of space. It is the silence required to make the story of American democracy work: if we admit that the freedpeople knew what they needed and were refused it by their own supposed allies, the moral clarity of the narrative collapses.

**The Tenure of Office Act: the gap between rhetoric and action.**

The rhetoric is constitutional principle—the Senate's advice and consent, the integrity of the executive branch, the Framers' design. The action is a trap, laid for a specific man in a specific political fight. Congress passes the Act on March 2, 1867, knowing that Johnson will violate it, knowing that violation will provide grounds for impeachment. This is not governance; it is the weaponization of legislation for a political prosecution. The Act is so transparently designed to protect Secretary of War Edwin Stanton—the Radical Republicans' man inside the Johnson administration—that its terms are practically a personal insult to the president.

But here is what the comfortable narrative omits: the impeachment trap is not merely about Johnson's obstruction of Reconstruction. It is about *who controls the military in the South*. Stanton is the linchpin. If Johnson can remove him and install a compliant secretary, the military governors of the five districts answer to a president who has already signaled his sympathy for the former Confederates. The Tenure of Office Act is the Radicals' hedge against the counter-revolution they can already see forming. The tragedy—and I use the word deliberately—is that the hedge fails not because Johnson outmaneuvers them but because the Senate lacks the votes to convict, and because the broader Northern public is already losing its appetite for the project. The Act becomes a historical footnote, "the technicality Johnson violated," because that framing serves the reconciliationist narrative: it reduces a struggle over the meaning of the war to a procedural squabble, letting everyone off the hook for what came next.

**The Alaska Purchase: Seward's Folly and the silence of the colonized.**

$7.2 million. Roughly two cents an acre. The domestic derision is the story we remember—the cartoons, the jokes about polar bears and icebergs, "Seward's icebox." This is a useful distraction. It frames the acquisition as a harmless eccentricity, a real estate deal that happened to work out, rather than as an act of imperial expansion conducted on terms that simply ignored the existence of the people who lived there.

The Treaty of Cession transfers "all the territory and dominion" from Russia to the United States. It makes no provision for the consent of the Tlingit, the Haida, the Aleut, the Yup'ik, the Iñupiat. They are not consulted, they are transferred. The Russian Empire, bankrupt and overextended, sells its claim to a land it never fully controlled—particularly not the Tlingit territories of the southeastern archipelago, where Russian authority was contested and contingent. The United States buys a title that exists primarily on European maps and in European legal fictions, and it will spend the next decades making that title real through military force. The bombardment of Kake in 1869, the destruction of Tlingit villages, the imposition of American law on peoples who had governed themselves for millennia—this is the purchase price that $7.2 million does not cover. The comfortable narrative lets us treat Alaska as a windfall, a lucky break, a real estate coup. It lets us avoid reckoning with the fact that the United States is, in 1867, an actively colonizing power, extending its sovereignty over indigenous nations through purchase and conquest, and that this process is simultaneous with—not separate from—the reconstruction of the South.

**Maximilian's execution: the story of republican triumph and what it conceals.**

The narrative is clean: a European puppet emperor, installed by French bayonets in violation of the Monroe Doctrine, is defeated and executed by Mexican patriots after the United States, fresh from its own civil war, applies diplomatic and military pressure to force Napoleon III's withdrawal. Benito Juárez stands as the embodiment of republican resistance. The Americas are secured against monarchy.

Cui bono from this story? The United States, which emerges as the hemispheric protector of republicanism without having fired a shot in Mexico itself. The narrative erases the fact that the United States was perfectly willing to tolerate Maximilian's empire while the Civil War raged—that the Monroe Doctrine was selectively enforced, a tool of convenience rather than principle. It erases the fact that Juárez's republic, once restored, will soon face the same pressures of American capital and territorial ambition that the war with France merely interrupted. The execution at Querétaro is not the end of European intervention in the Americas; it is the end of *military* intervention by European *monarchies*. The economic intervention, the debt diplomacy, the extraction of resources by European and, increasingly, American capital—these continue and intensify. Maximilian dies, but the structure of Mexican dependency does not. The story of 1867 as republican vindication lets the United States position itself as the natural ally of Latin American democracy, a role it will invoke repeatedly to justify interventions that serve very different ends.

**The British North America Act and the Austro-Hungarian Compromise: the architecture of American hegemony.**

These two events, occurring on opposite sides of the Atlantic within months of each other, are not merely coincidental. They are the structural conditions for the United States' emergence as the uncontested power in the Western Hemisphere. The British North America Act creates a federal Dominion of Canada—a counterweight, yes, but also a buffer, a stable northern neighbor that will not become a staging ground for British military intervention in the United States. The British, having watched the Union mobilize and supply the largest army on earth, are recalibrating. Canada is a strategic withdrawal disguised as a grant of self-government. The British garrison will draw down. The border will demilitarize. The United States gains a continent without having to fight the world's preeminent naval power for it.

The Austro-Hungarian Compromise serves a parallel function. The Habsburg Empire, shattered by Prussia at Königgrätz in 1866, restructures itself to survive. The Compromise stabilizes Central Europe by giving the Magyar elite co-equal status with the German Austrians, creating a dual monarchy that will persist until 1918. This stabilization is good for American interests in a way that no one in Vienna or Budapest is thinking about: it keeps the European powers focused on their own continent, their own balance of power, their own nationalist pressures. A stable Austria-Hungary means no general European war, which means no distraction from the American project of continental consolidation and economic expansion. The United States does not cause the Compromise, but it is among its principal beneficiaries. The narrative of 1867 as the year American democracy proved itself on the world stage omits this: American ascendancy is built on a European peace that Americans did not create and could not sustain, and when that peace collapses in 1914, the consequences will draw the United States into a global role it has spent the previous half-century avoiding.

**The inheritance: what the comfortable version lets us avoid feeling responsible for today.**

The year 1867 establishes a template that the United States has never abandoned. The template works like this: proclaim a democratic principle, implement it partially, withdraw the enforcement mechanism when it becomes costly, and then narrate the resulting failure as a tragedy rather than a choice. Reconstruction gives Black men the vote but not the land, then abandons them to the terrorism that will strip the vote away. The Alaska Purchase extends American sovereignty over indigenous nations, then leaves them to the mercy of extractive capital and military pacification. The triumph over Maximilian establishes the United States as the hemispheric hegemon, then converts that hegemony into a license for intervention that serves American corporations. In every case, the story told afterward—the story of democratic expansion, of continental destiny, of republican solidarity—serves to make the outcome seem inevitable and benign, when it was neither.

What we avoid feeling responsible for is the pattern itself: the gap between what we profess and what we do, and the way we use our own professed ideals as a shield against accountability. #### 🜃 Cartographer
The year 1867 is not a collection of disparate headlines but a singular moment of sovereign hardening. Beneath the noise of impeachment traps and land purchases lies a structural shift: the transition from the fluid, contested sovereignty of the mid-century to the rigid, centralized nation-state. In the United States, this manifested as the federal government finally claiming a monopoly on the definition of citizenship and the legitimate use of force within its borders. The First Reconstruction Act (US-1) was not merely a political maneuver; it was the imposition of a new mode of production on the Southern landscape, replacing the plantation aristocracy's local autonomy with a military-administered standardization of labor and suffrage. This was only possible because the slow variable of European imperial capacity had reached a nadir. The execution of Maximilian (W-1) was the terminal symptom of a deeper structural reality: the cost of projecting monarchical power across the Atlantic had exceeded the return, especially against a rising industrial power mobilizing for internal consolidation.

The connections here are tectonic. The Austro-Hungarian Compromise (W-3) and the British North America Act (W-2) represent the Old World and the Northern neighbor stabilizing their own internal fractures through federal reorganization. This global trend toward internal consolidation removed the external pressure that had previously constrained U.S. expansionism. With Europe turning inward to manage its own national questions, the United States faced no credible counter-hegemon in the hemisphere. This structural permission slip is what made "Seward's Folly" (US-2) logically sound despite its contemporary ridicule. Seward was not buying ice; he was securing the northern flank of a continent that the U.S. now effectively dominated, anticipating a Pacific century that required a foothold free from Russian or British interference. The purchase was a bet on the slow variable of maritime trade routes, predicated on the fast variable of European retreat.

Domestically, the Tenure of Office Act (US-3) serves as the keystone holding this arch together. It reveals the fragility of the Reconstruction project; the legislative branch understood that the structural transformation of the South (US-1) relied entirely on the continuity of executive enforcement. Knowing the President represented a different economic and racial order—one aligned with the pre-war status quo—Congress engineered a statutory cage to prevent the reversal of the military districts. This was not a petty power struggle; it was a systemic immune response. The radical Republicans recognized that without controlling the personnel of the executive branch, the geographic and demographic realities of the South would instantly revert to their pre-war equilibrium once the army withdrew. The six events of 1867, therefore, map a single trajectory: the closure of the frontier of ambiguity.
