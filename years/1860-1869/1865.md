## 0. Context Carried Forward

- **Thread — `fiscal-military-state`:** The extraction machinery now faces the inverse pressure of contraction; with Lee's surrender imminent, the state must pivot from issuing greenbacks and war bonds to managing a $2.7 billion debt and demobilizing 1 million soldiers without collapsing the currency or igniting class violence in the cities.
- **Thread — `confederate-attrition-trap`:** The trap has sprung shut completely; with Richmond evacuated and the army dissolved, the geographic entity of the Confederacy ceases to exist, transforming the conflict from a war between belligerents into a domestic insurrection policing problem where leadership faces either exile, trial, or execution.
- **Thread — `total-logistics-dominance`:** The doctrine of infrastructure targeting shifts from strategic necessity to occupation policy; the question is no longer how to break the South's will, but how to use the same logistical supremacy to enforce emancipation and suppress guerrilla warfare in a landscape of burned farms and ruined railroads.
- **Thread — `black-military-integration`:** The argument from blood shed reaches its constitutional climax; 200,000 Black veterans return not as supplicants but as armed claimants to citizenship, forcing the legal system to reconcile the status of freedmen with the reality of men who held muskets for the Union.
- **Thread — `executive-reconstruction-prerogative`:** Lincoln's assassination transfers the pen to Andrew Johnson, whose rigid interpretation of executive amnesty powers immediately fractures the wartime coalition, turning the theoretical collision with Congress into an active constitutional crisis over who holds the authority to readmit states.
- **Thread — `monroe-doctrine-enforcement-backlog`:** The internal front is now clear enough to look outward; Sheridan's veteran cavalry begins massing on the Texas border, signaling the immediate conversion of domestic military surplus into external hegemony to eject the French from Mexico.

## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — The Assassination of Abraham Lincoln (April 14, 1865)
- **What happened (fact):** John Wilkes Booth shoots the President at Ford's Theatre; Lincoln dies the following morning; Andrew Johnson assumes office.
- **The narrative *then*:** A martyrdom of almost Christological dimensions—Lincoln the "Savior of the Republic" struck down at the moment of victory by the very hatred he sought to quench. Northern pulpits and presses immediately frame it as divine punishment for national sin, or alternatively, as the Confederacy's final act of treacherous violence proving their unfitness for merciful treatment. Radical Republicans whisper that Johnson's ascension is providential; they can now push harder than Lincoln would have permitted.
- **The narrative *now*:** The "what might have been" tragedy—Lincoln as the lost reconciler whose presence would have prevented Jim Crow. Schoolbook Lincoln the gentle father, too merciful for this world, struck down by a madman. The Booth-Lincoln dyad flattened into pure villain and pure victim, with the political mechanics of the succession erased.
- **Why it earns a slot:** It is the hinge. Everything after—Johnson's states'-rights Reconstruction, the betrayal of land redistribution, the collapse of biracial democracy—flows through this gunshot. Without it, the counterfactual is too vast to calculate.

#### US-2 — Special Field Orders No. 15 Revoked / Circular No. 13 (Autumn 1865)
- **What happened (fact):** President Johnson orders the return of confiscated coastal lands to former Confederate owners, reversing Sherman's January 1865 promise of "40 acres" to freed families and forcing them into labor contracts.
- **The narrative *then*:** Johnson speaks the language of restoration—property rights, constitutional order, healing the nation's wounds by returning to lawful owners. To white Southerners, it is deliverance from vindictive confiscation. To freedpeople, it is the first betrayal, felt in the body: the mule taken back, the cabin emptied, the promise of independent farming dissolved into another man's fields. The Freedmen's Bureau agents report despair; Radical Republicans begin to understand that executive mercy means Black subordination.
- **The narrative *now*:** Buried. The "40 acres and a mule" phrase survives as a bitter proverb, but the specific mechanism—Johnson's autumn orders, the War Department's compliance, the precise moment when property redistribution became property restoration—is absent from most narratives. Reparations discourse recovers it, but mainstream memory treats Black economic dispossession as gradual, not a deliberate policy choice in the first months of peace.
- **Why it earns a slot:** It is the material foundation of everything after. The political economy of sharecropping, debt peonage, and racialized poverty is constructed here, in specific orders with specific dates.

#### US-3 — Ratification of the 13th Amendment (December 6, 1865)
- **What happened (fact):** Georgia's ratification provides the 27th state approval; Secretary of State Seward certifies the amendment, constitutionally abolishing slavery throughout the United States.
- **The narrative *then*:** A second founding, the completion of 1776's unfinished promise. Celebrated in Northern cities as the legal death of the slave power, the vindication of Union war aims, the moral triumph of a Christian nation finally repenting. But contemporaries also see the seams: the amendment's language—"except as a punishment for crime"—already whispers to Southern legislators, and the absence of voting rights or land guarantees means the freedom it grants is formally empty.
- **The narrative *now*:** The " slavery is over" amendment, taught as closure. The exception clause is rarely emphasized; the schoolbook version presents abolition as a clean break, not a legal transformation that preserved coerced labor through new mechanisms. The 13th becomes a monument, not a document to be read closely.
- **Why it earns a slot:** It converts military emancipation into constitutional permanence, but its textual gaps—intentional or contingent—become the architecture of postwar racial control.

#### W-1 — Surrender at Appomattox (April 9, 1865)
- **What happened (fact):** Lee surrenders the Army of Northern Virginia to Grant; Grant offers generous terms allowing officers to keep sidearms and all men to return home without prosecution.
- **The narrative *then*:** For the North, relief so profound it borders on the sacred—the killing can stop, the Union is saved. Grant's magnanimity is praised as Christian forbearance, the model for national reconciliation. For the South, the "Lost Cause" germinates immediately: Lee the impeccable knight, outnumbered and outsupplied, surrendering with honor to a foe who recognized his greatness. The terms themselves become contested—were they too generous? Did they permit the South to preserve its martial pride intact?
- **The narrative *now*:** The "end of the war," full stop. The generous terms are celebrated as Lincoln's spirit incarnate, the seed of peaceful reunion. The alternative—that they permitted Confederate officers to retain social authority, that they established a pattern of unpunished rebellion, that they left the political question of Confederate citizenship unresolved—is raised by critics but rarely penetrates the mythic tableau of the two generals in the McLean parlor.
- **Why it earns a slot:** It ends major combat but begins the narrative war over what the combat meant. The surrender's staging—who was present, what was said, what was omitted—shapes fifty years of sectional memory.

#### W-2 — French Withdrawal Order from Mexico (November 1865)
- **What happened (fact):** Napoleon III, facing 50,000 US veterans under Sheridan on the Rio Grande and Prussian pressure in Europe, orders phased withdrawal of French forces from the puppet empire of Maximilian.
- **The narrative *then*:** In the United States, validation of the Monroe Doctrine through blood and steel—the first time the hemispheric prohibition on European monarchy is enforced by credible threat. Seward's diplomacy, Sheridan's deployment, and the Union army's unspent violence converge. In Europe, recognition that the American colossus, now reunited and veteran, cannot be challenged in its own hemisphere. In Mexico, the beginning of hope for Republican restoration, but also foreboding about what a confident, expansionist United States might become.
- **The narrative *now*:** Marginal, when mentioned at all. The Monroe Doctrine is taught as principle, not as the product of specific coercive acts in specific years. The Mexican intervention is a footnote; the US role in ending it, a smaller footnote. The through-line to later hemispheric domination—1898, the Banana Wars, the long shadow of 1865's demonstration—remains un drawn in most accounts.
- **Why it earns a slot:** It proves the reunited United States can project force beyond its borders, establishing the precedent for hemispheric hegemony that becomes structural in the decades after.

#### W-3 — The Great Eastern Cable Attempt (August 1865)
- **What happened (fact):** The massive steamship *Great Eastern* attempts to lay a permanent transatlantic telegraph cable, fails mid-ocean, but demonstrates the industrial and financial capacity to bind continents through instantaneous communication.
- **The narrative *then*:** A spectacle of Victorian technological sublime—the largest ship ever built, the longest cable ever attempted, the annihilation of Atlantic distance. For investors, anxiety; for engineers, heroic failure that promises eventual success. The connection to the war is felt but not always articulated: the same industrial capacity that fed Union armies now turns to civilian conquest of time and space. The cable is peace's first monument, commerce's new nervous system.
- **The narrative *now*:** The 1866 success absorbs 1865's failure; the "first transatlantic cable" is dated to the working line, not the attempt that proved it possible. The narrative of inevitable technological progress swallows the contingency. The financial speculation, the capital flows, the class of global investors created by these ventures—the political economy of instantaneous global communication—is rarely connected to the imperial and commercial power it would enable.
- **Why it earns a slot:** It is the infrastructure of the narrative itself. The stories 1865 tells about itself will travel on cables like this; the global capital markets that shape the Gilded Age depend on it; the compression of time and space that defines modernity begins here, in a failed attempt that succeeded in proving the concept.

## 2. Connections Within the Year

- **U.S. ↔ World:** The deployment of 50,000 veteran Union troops to the Texas-Mexico border (W-2) was not merely a foreign policy maneuver but the direct application of U.S. domestic military surplus to enforce the Monroe Doctrine; this projection of force, made possible only by the conclusion of the Civil War (W-1), compelled Napoleon III's withdrawal from Mexico, proving that the survival of the American republic now depended on its capacity to project power outwardly to secure its ideological perimeter against monarchy.
- **Event ↔ Event:** The assassination of Lincoln (US-1) and the revocation of Special Field Orders No. 15 (US-2) function as a single causal mechanism: the removal of Lincoln's political cover allowed Andrew Johnson to dismantle the economic basis of Black freedom (land) just as the 13th Amendment (US-3) provided the legal shell of freedom without substance, creating a structural vacuum where legal liberty existed alongside economic destitution.
- **Invisible / structural (shared hidden drivers):** The defining structural variable of 1865 is the *uncoupling of labor from property*; while the Atlantic telegraph attempt (W-3) signaled the accelerating integration of global capital markets demanding fluid labor and instant information, the U.S. internal settlement replaced chattel slavery with a coerced labor system (sharecropping) designed to keep capital immobile in the South, revealing a deep tension between emerging global capitalist speeds and the deliberate drag of regional racial hierarchy.

## 3. The Debate

### Round 1 — Opening Interpretations (uninterrupted)

#### 🜂 Herald
---

The year 1865 is not a conclusion. It is a *transition so rapid that contemporaries could not stabilize their own understanding before the next rupture arrived.* What we inherit as a coherent narrative—Union preserved, slavery abolished, nation healed—was, in the lived experience of the year, a sequence of shocks that outran interpretation. The Herald's lens demands we inhabit that vertigo before we judge it.

Consider the temporality. Lincoln is inaugurated for a second term in March, speaks of "malice toward none" in April, dies on Good Friday. The war effectively ends in April; emancipation is enforced in June; slavery is constitutionally abolished in December. Between these dates, Johnson undoes the land redistribution that would have made freedom economically meaningful. The speed is not merely chronological; it is *narrative*, a forcing of meaning before meaning can be contested. Booth's bullet does not just kill a man; it kills the interval in which Reconstruction's meaning might have been negotiated. Lincoln's death instantiates the martyrdom narrative before Lincoln himself could complicate it with policy choices. The "Savior of the Union" becomes available for projection because he is silenced.

The belief-structure of 1865 operates through *substitution*. The generous terms at Appomattox stand in for a political settlement that never occurs. The 13th Amendment's formal abolition stands in for genuine emancipation. The Monroe Doctrine's enforcement stands in for a coherent foreign policy. Each substitution is experienced as completion by those who need completion—Northern elites exhausted by war, Southern elites seeking restoration, European powers calculating costs—while those who experience the gap between symbol and substance (the freedpeople, the Mexican Republicans, the Radical Republicans who see Johnson's betrayal forming) are not yet audible in the dominant story.

The mythology hardens with extraordinary rapidity. By December, the "Lost Cause" is already under construction in Southern drawing rooms; by December, Lincoln is already the martyred reconciler in Northern memorial rhetoric. These are not later inventions; they are *contemporaneous framings*, competitive bids for how the year will be remembered. The Herald's obligation is to track this competition, not to award victory prematurely. The Lost Cause narrative of 1865 is as much a part of the year's meaning as the Union victory narrative; they are not separate "Southern" and "Northern" stories but interdependent constructions, each requiring the other to achieve coherence. Lee's magnanimity in surrender requires Grant's recognition; Grant's generosity requires Lee's dignity. The tableau at Appomattox is already a *joint performance* for future memory, even as the two generals negotiate terms that will shape political reality.

The narrative *now*—the schoolbook synthesis—performs its own work of substitution. It substitutes Lincoln's hypothetical mercy for Johnson's actual policy. It substitutes the 13th Amendment's noble purpose for its textual complicity with convict leasing. It substitutes "the end of slavery" for the beginning of sharecropping, Jim Crow, and disenfranchisement. This is not mere omission; it is *structural*, a narrative architecture that requires 1865 to be a terminus so that later developments can appear as regrettable departures rather than logical extensions. The "what might have been" of Lincoln's survival is not a historical question but a *theological* one, a faith that individual virtue could have altered structural outcomes. The Herald must honor this faith—many contemporaries held it, many acted upon it—while naming it as faith, not analysis.

The year's most consequential belief, and its most consequential forgetting, concerns *land*. Special Field Orders No. 15 was not merely a military expedient; it was a *narrative of what freedom meant*—independence, self-proprietorship, the yeoman republic extended to Black Americans. Johnson's revocation in autumn 1865 does not just redistribute property; it destroys a possible story about American identity. The "40 acres" that survives in folk memory is the ghost of this destroyed narrative, the alternative Reconstruction that would have required a different kind of United States. Its suppression is experienced by freedpeople not as policy but as *narrative violence*, the foreclosure of the story they might have told about themselves.

The Great Eastern's cable attempt belongs in this analysis because it is the infrastructure of narrative itself. The stories 1865 generates—of Union, of emancipation, of national healing—will travel globally through technologies demonstrated this year. The simultaneity that the cable promises is the simultaneity of modern myth-making: events become shared, interpretations become contested across space as well as time. The year is not merely interpreted; it is *wired*, its meanings transmitted, contested, stabilized through new media that alter what "memory" can mean.

The Herald's full-strength reading: 1865 is the year America *almost* became a different country, and the speed of its mythology prevented that possibility from being fully articulated before it was foreclosed. The assassination, the land revocation, the 13th Amendment's gaps, the Appomattox terms' generosity, the Mexican withdrawal, the cable's promise—all are moments when alternative paths existed, when the story was not yet fixed. What we inherit is not the only possible inheritance but the one that won, through specific acts of narrative construction performed by specific actors with specific interests. To believe otherwise is to participate in the mythology itself, which is the most powerful proof of its continued operation.

#### 🜄 Heretic
Let me tell you the story they told themselves. In 1865, the Republic, tested by fire, proved that its founding proposition—that all men are created equal—was not a lie. The martyr Lincoln gave his life on Good Friday, a blood sacrifice to wash the nation’s sin. The armies of the Union, magnanimous in victory, offered terms so generous at Appomattox that the brothers could embrace again. The chains of four million fell in Galveston, in the halls of Congress, in the constitutional text itself—the 13th Amendment making permanent what the Proclamation had begun. Abroad, the Republic flexed its restored muscle, deploying 50,000 veterans to the Rio Grande and telling the crowned heads of Europe that the Western Hemisphere was closed for monarchical business. Napoleon III blinked; Maximilian’s empire was doomed. And across the Atlantic, the nations of Europe, having witnessed the industrialized slaughter of our war, gathered in Paris to sign the first Geneva Convention, institutionalizing mercy itself. A new birth of freedom, a nation made whole, a moral example radiating outward. This is the story. It is not false. It is merely incomplete in ways that serve specific interests, and the incompleteness is the point.

Let us begin with the corpse at Ford’s Theatre, because the assassination is the interpretive keystone of the year, and the received story has a structural function it does not advertise. Booth fires a bullet into Lincoln’s brain on April 14, and instantly the narrative machinery activates: the Savior dies for the nation’s sins, on Good Friday, no less. The timing is so theologically convenient it should make any forensic reader suspicious. Who benefits from a dead Lincoln? Not Booth, a failed actor and Confederate sympathizer who is hunted down and killed within twelve days. But the *story* of Lincoln’s martyrdom—that benefits an enormous coalition. It benefits the Northern Republican establishment, which can now canonize Lincoln as the reconciler who would have healed the nation’s wounds, a claim that conveniently elides the fact that Lincoln, in his final public speech on April 11, had endorsed limited Black suffrage in Louisiana, a position that placed him to the left of his own party’s moderate wing. The martyr Lincoln is infinitely more useful than the living Lincoln would have been, because the martyr cannot make inconvenient policy choices. He cannot clash with the Radical Republicans over the pace of Reconstruction. He cannot negotiate with a Congress that was already chafing at his executive overreach. He is frozen in the amber of his Second Inaugural’s poetry—“with malice toward none, with charity for all”—a phrase that, read coldly, is a call for leniency toward the defeated planter class, the very people who had just tried to found a slave republic. The assassination does not just remove Lincoln; it removes the *uncertainty* of Lincoln. It lets everyone project their preferred Lincoln onto the silence. The Radicals can claim him as an abolitionist saint. The moderates can claim him as a pragmatist. The South, eventually, can claim him as the friend who would have spared them the indignities of Reconstruction. All of these claims are unverifiable. That is their utility.

Now consider who is silenced by the assassination, and by the interpretive regime it installs. The freedpeople are the obvious answer, but let me be more specific. The assassination narrative—Lincoln the reconciler, cut down before he could bind the nation’s wounds—immediately reframes the moral calculus of Reconstruction. If Lincoln’s death is a tragedy because it removes the great reconciler, then the work of Reconstruction must be *reconciliation*, not *justice*. The generous terms at Appomattox, which Grant offered Lee on April 9, become the template not just for military surrender but for political settlement. Grant paroles Lee’s officers, allows them to keep their sidearms and horses, and sends them home. This is presented as magnanimity, as Lincoln’s spirit made operational. But what does it actually accomplish? It leaves the Confederate officer corps intact, unpunished, and in possession of their social status. These men do not go home chastened. They go home to become the first generation of the Ku Klux Klan’s leadership, the architects of the Black Codes, the redeemers of white supremacy. The generous terms are not merely military policy; they are a decision about who will wield power in the postwar South, and the decision is: the same men who led the rebellion. Grant’s terms are praised as healing. For whom? Not for the people whose labor had been stolen for two centuries, and who now watch their former enslavers retain their land, their weapons, their horses, and their political future.

This brings us to the central crime of 1865, the one the comfortable narrative works hardest to obscure: the deliberate, systematic destruction of Black economic independence. Special Field Orders No. 15, issued by Sherman in January 1865, had set aside 400,000 acres of confiscated coastal land—from Charleston to northern Florida—for settlement by freed families, with the famous promise of “forty acres and a mule.” This was not a radical fantasy; it was military policy, enacted by a Union general as a practical measure for managing the refugee crisis trailing his army. By June, 40,000 freedpeople had settled on this land, planting crops, building homes, establishing the material basis for freedom. And then, in the autumn of 1865, President Andrew Johnson—Lincoln’s successor, the man the assassination installed—orders the land returned to its former Confederate owners. Let that sit for a moment. The United States government, having just fought a war that killed over 600,000 people, having just amended its Constitution to abolish slavery, *takes land away from the people it freed and gives it back to the people who had enslaved them*. The freedpeople are evicted, often at bayonet point, by the very Union army that had promised them protection. The land reverts to the planters. The freedpeople are forced into labor contracts—sharecropping and tenant farming—that will bind them to the land and to their former masters for generations. The 13th Amendment, ratified in December, abolishes slavery “except as a punishment for crime.” That exception clause is not a loophole; it is an invitation, and the South will spend the next century accepting it, criminalizing Black life to recreate a captive labor force through convict leasing. The amendment abolishes the *property relation* of slavery but leaves the *labor relation* entirely undefined, and the undefined is immediately filled by the power of the planter class, backed by a federal government that has just demonstrated, by revoking the land grants, whose side it is on.

Cui bono? The planter class regains its land, its labor force, and—within a decade—its political power. Northern industrial capital gets a restored cotton economy that supplies its mills with cheap raw material, grown by a labor force that is free in name but coerced in practice. The Republican Party gets to claim it abolished slavery while avoiding the redistribution of wealth that would have made abolition meaningful. The federal government gets sectional reconciliation at the cost of racial justice. Everyone in power benefits from the settlement of 1865 except the people whose freedom was supposedly the war’s purpose.

The deployment of Sheridan’s 50,000 troops to the Texas-Mexico border in November is presented as a triumph of republican solidarity—the United States defending Mexican sovereignty against French imperial adventure. And it is that, in part. Napoleon III does order the phased withdrawal of French forces; Maximilian’s regime does collapse. The Monroe Doctrine is vindicated. But look at what else is happening on that border. The troops are deployed to Texas, the last Confederate holdout, where General Granger had arrived in June to enforce emancipation. The same military force that is projecting American power against European monarchy is also the force that could, if directed, enforce Reconstruction in the former Confederacy. Instead, it is sent to the border. The troops are not used to protect the freedpeople’s land claims. They are not used to suppress the nascent white terror organizations. They are used to chase a foreign enemy while the domestic counterrevolution organizes itself unmolested. The Monroe Doctrine, in this light, is not merely a foreign policy; it is a displacement of military attention away from the unfinished business of emancipation. We get a hemisphere free of European monarchy and a South free to reimpose white supremacy. The two outcomes are not unrelated; they are produced by the same allocation of federal force.

The Geneva Convention of 1865—the founding of the International Red Cross and the codification of the laws of war—is the year’s most elegant alibi. Twelve European nations gather in Paris and agree that wounded soldiers should be treated humanely, that hospitals should be neutral, that the Red Cross emblem should be respected. The American Civil War, with its field hospitals and its prison camps and its 600,000 dead, is invoked as the object lesson. This is humanitarianism as prestige performance. The same European powers that sign the Geneva Convention have spent the previous three centuries perfecting the arts of colonial slaughter—the British in India, the French in Algeria, the Belgians preparing for the Congo. The laws of war they codify in 1865 will apply, in practice, to wars between *civilized* nations, which is to say, wars between white nations. The colonial wars—the punitive expeditions, the village burnings, the famines engineered by policy—will continue without the inconvenience of the Red Cross. The Convention is not hypocrisy in the simple sense; it is something more structural. It is the creation of a moral vocabulary that allows the European powers to congratulate themselves on their humanity while reserving the category of “human” for those they recognize as such. The freedpeople of the American South, the indigenous peoples of the American West, the colonized peoples of Africa and Asia—none of them are the intended beneficiaries of the Geneva Convention. They are outside its frame, and the frame is the point.

What the comfortable narrative of 1865 avoids letting us feel responsible for is the counterfactual: that it could have been otherwise. The land could have been redistributed. The planter class could have been broken. The 13th Amendment could have included an explicit guarantee of political rights. The troops at the Rio Grande could have been deployed to South Carolina. The assassination of Lincoln did not make the betrayal inevitable—Johnson’s policies were his own, and the Republican Congress would eventually impeach him for them—but the assassination made the betrayal *narratable* as tragedy rather than as choice. We mourn the martyr and overlook the policies. We celebrate the amendment and ignore the exception clause. We praise the generous surrender terms and avert our eyes from the night riders. The inheritance that reaches us in the present-day United States is not the inheritance of a failed Reconstruction; it is the inheritance of a Reconstruction that was *defeated* by specific actors making specific choices, and then rebranded as a noble effort that sadly fell short. The gap between the 13th Amendment’s promise and the sharecropper’s reality is not a gap that opened later; it was manufactured in 1865, by people who knew what they were doing, and whose descendants are still in power.

#### 🜃 Cartographer
The year 1865 is not a conclusion; it is a violent phase shift in the mode of production. The map of this year reveals a continent where the slow, heavy variable of geography—the sheer physical distance between the industrial North and the agrarian South, and between the U.S. and Europe—was being compressed by technology and terror, yet the social architecture remained stubbornly rigid. We observe a critical divergence: the war ended militarily at Appomattox (W-1), but the peace was lost politically in Washington and economically in the cotton fields.

The central structural fracture of 1865 is the decoupling of legal status from material reality. The ratification of the 13th Amendment (US-3) stands as a monumental reconfiguration of the Constitution, destroying the property relation that had undergirded the Southern economy for centuries. However, viewed through the lens of deep structure, this amendment solved a legal problem for the state while creating a labor crisis for the planter class. The system required a solution that maintained the extraction of surplus value from Black bodies without the legal title of ownership. The answer arrived not in legislation, but in the administrative reversal of land redistribution (US-2). When Johnson revoked Special Field Orders No. 15, he did not merely make a policy choice; he enforced a geographic and economic determinism. By returning the land to the former enslavers, the federal government ensured that the newly freed population had no means of production other than their own labor, which they were forced to sell back to the same class that had owned them. This created the `Broken-Reconstruction` thread: a pattern where civil rights are granted in the abstract while economic sovereignty is systematically denied, a contradiction that would harden into the Jim Crow regime.

This internal contradiction was mirrored and exacerbated by the sudden transfer of executive authority following Lincoln's assassination (US-1). Lincoln represented a flexible, if ambiguous, executive prerogative capable of bridging the gap between radical aims and conservative implementation. His death removed the only figure with the capital to manage the transition of power. In his place, Andrew Johnson operated within a rigid states-rights framework that viewed the federal government's role as ending once the armies surrendered. This shifted the center of gravity from the executive to the legislative branch, setting the stage for the constitutional crisis of the next decade. The assassination was the catalyst that turned a political negotiation into a structural deadlock.

Simultaneously, the United States projected its newfound military mass outward to resolve the `Monroe-Doctrine-Enforcement` thread. The presence of half a million armed men at the end of the Civil War created a surplus of violence that had to be absorbed or exported. By moving Sheridan's veterans to the Rio Grande (W-2), the U.S. converted military victory into diplomatic leverage. Napoleon III's decision to withdraw from Mexico was not born of moral conversion but of strategic calculation; he recognized that the United States, now unified and battle-hardened, possessed the structural capacity to enforce its hemispheric hegemony. This moment marks the transition of the U.S. from a fragile experiment to a regional empire, using the threat of force to clear the Western Hemisphere of European monarchical rivals. The success here contrasts sharply with the failure domestically: the state could project power thousands of miles to topple a puppet emperor but could not project moral authority ten miles into the Mississippi Delta to secure land for the freedpeople.

Finally, the attempted laying of the Atlantic cable (W-3) serves as the technological harbinger of the era's true master: global capital. Even in failure, the *Great Eastern* demonstrated that the constraints of time and space were becoming negotiable for finance, if not for human beings. While information began to move at the speed of electricity, binding London and New York markets, the movement of people in the American South was being restricted by Black Codes and vagrancy laws. The structure of 1865 thus establishes a enduring American paradox: a nation increasingly integrated into the fast-moving flows of global capitalism while maintaining a regional economy built on the slow, coercive immobilization of a racialized underclass.

The handoff to 1866 is defined by the sharpening of these contradictions. The legal freedom of the 13th Amendment meets the practical unfreedom of the Black Codes, forcing the Radical Republicans in Congress to seize the initiative from the Executive. The `Broken-Reconstruction` thread tightens, as the South attempts to restore the old labor relations under new legal names, and the North begins to realize that military victory alone cannot secure a republican peace.
