## 0. Context Carried Forward

- **Thread — `federal-legitimacy-crisis`:** Entering 1860, the crisis has moved from rhetorical nullification to active preparatory secession; Southern legislatures are drafting ordinances of disunion contingent on the election outcome, while the Buchanan administration admits total incapacity to enforce federal law in South Carolina, leaving the Union legally intact but operationally hollow.
- **Thread — `kansas-civil-war`:** The armed stalemate in Kansas has calcified into a demographic reality with the admission of a free-state constitution, but the militias have not disbanded; instead, they have fragmented into local vigilance committees that serve as the ready-made officer corps for the coming national conflict, holding weapons that will soon be turned eastward.
- **Thread — `republican-coalescence`:** The "Wide Awakes" have evolved from electoral marshals into a visible uniformed force marching in major Northern cities, signaling that the Republican Party is no longer just a political coalition but a mobilized civil society prepared to defend its victory by force if necessary, confirming Southern fears of a "Black Republican" invasion.
- **Thread — `industrial-warfare-preview`:** Private Northern foundries and rail networks have completed the logistical mapping for rapid troop deployment and arms mass-production; this shadow capacity now exceeds the official federal arsenal's output, meaning the North possesses the means to wage total war before the political decision to do so has even been made.
- **Thread — `epistemological-schism`:** The divergence in worldviews has hardened into mutually exclusive political ontologies; the South views the election not as a policy dispute but as an existential threat to a divine order, while the North increasingly views slavery as a barbaric anomaly incompatible with modern progress, rendering compromise logically impossible within either framework.
- **Thread — `global-capital-reorientation`:** The opening of Yokohama and the nearing completion of the Suez Canal have accelerated the shift of global finance toward London and Asia; British capital is quietly hedging against a potential disruption of American cotton, exploring alternative sources in India and Egypt, which subtly undermines the Southern belief in "King Cotton's" absolute diplomatic leverage.

## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — The Democratic Party Convention Splinters (April–June 1860)
- **What happened (fact):** The Democratic National Convention fractures in Charleston and Baltimore along sectional lines, nominating two separate candidates (Douglas and Breckinridge) and guaranteeing a Republican victory regardless of popular vote totals.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Douglas Democrats, this was the tragedy of sectional fanaticism overwhelming the Union-saving center; for Southern Democrats, it was the final proof that the party of Jackson had been captured by a Black Republican coalition willing to accept a president who spoke of slavery's ultimate extinction. Both sides claimed to be the true heirs of the Democracy, both insisted the fracture was forced upon them. Northern editors spoke of a "conspiracy" of fire-eaters who had planned the split; Southern papers returned the charge, that Douglas's popular-sovereignty sophistry was merely abolitionism with a longer fuse. The common thread: each side believed the other had broken a sacred vessel, and that the American experiment itself hung on who was believed.
- **The narrative *now*:** The split appears as structural inevitability, the final arithmetic of a party system that had papered over irreconcilable interests since 1820. Textbooks treat it as a case study in how institutions fail when sectionalism exceeds party loyalty. The pathos is drained; we see the result—Lincoln's victory—so clearly that we forget the genuine desperation of those who tried to hold the middle, and the genuine conviction of those who refused it.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the mechanism by which democratic process produced its own collapse. The fracture was not in the streets but in the rules, the ballots, the backrooms—a specifically American catastrophe, faith in procedure devouring itself.

#### US-2 — Election of Abraham Lincoln (November 6, 1860)
- **What happened (fact):** Abraham Lincoln wins the presidency with zero electoral votes from the South, triggering immediate secession conventions in seven Deep South states within six weeks.
- **The narrative *then*:** In the North, this was the vindication of free labor, of the farmer and mechanic against the Slave Power, of a republic that had nearly choked on the Compromise of 1850 and the Fugitive Slave Act. The Chicago Tribune spoke of "the peaceful revolution of the ballot"; Lincoln himself, in private, knew better, telling visitors that shadowed his face even as crowds cheered. In the South, it was something closer to apocalypse—a sectional party, openly committed to slavery's containment, had captured the federal apparatus without a single Southern voice. The Charleston Mercury had prepared the grammar for years: "The election of Lincoln is a declaration of war." For enslaved people, interpretation varied by proximity to hope; for free Black Americans, it was a door cracking that might yet be slammed.
- **The narrative *now*:** The Great Emancipator, the railsplitter, the last best hope. The story has been sanded so smooth that we risk forgetting it was ever contested, that Lincoln's own antislavery was hedged and evolutionary, that millions voted against him, that millions more were barred from voting at all. The myth of inevitable progress attaches here with particular tenacity; we inherit a Lincoln who grows toward our values rather than one who was, in 1860, a moderately ambitious politician navigating impossible constraints.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The most consequential American election. It demonstrates how a constitutional system can produce a president whom a third of the population considers illegitimate, and how that illegitimacy can be weaponized into dissolution.

#### US-3 — Formation of the Confederate States of America (December 20, 1860 – February 1861)
- **What happened (fact):** Following Lincoln's election, South Carolina leads six other states in passing ordinances of secession and drafting a provisional constitution explicitly protecting slavery as the cornerstone of the new nation.
- **The narrative *then*:** For secessionists, this was 1776 rehearsed—the same right of revolution, the same oppressive central power, the same sacred fire of self-determination. The ordinances borrowed the Declaration's cadences deliberately; the secession commissioners who fanned out to other Southern states carried this script. For Unionists, it was treason with a legal veneer, a coup by a slaveholding minority that had lost a fair election and would rather destroy the republic than accept majority rule. The provisional constitution's explicit protection of slavery was, to Confederates, mere honesty about what the old Constitution had always meant; to Unionists, it was the mask finally dropped. The enslaved heard the news differently—some with terror at what war would bring, others with the first flicker of what war might end.
- **The narrative *now*:** The "Lost Cause" narrative, dominant for a century, has been sufficiently dismantled in mainstream discourse that we risk the opposite error: treating the Confederacy as mere pathology, its founders as cartoon villains rather than men who believed themselves principled and whose beliefs mobilized millions. We inherit the Confederate flag as a symbol so overdetermined that its original meanings—however repugnant—are almost inaccessible. The "cornerstone" speech of Alexander Stephens, once obscure, is now weaponized in textbook wars; the actual complexity of Confederate ideology, its genuine republican borrowings, is flattened.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the American state's most serious crisis of legitimacy, and the moment when slavery's defenders chose to build an alternative nation rather than accept gradual decline. The story told about 1860-1861 still structures our politics: states' rights versus federal supremacy, minority protection versus majority rule, the meaning of "we the people."

#### W-1 — Unification of Italy Proclaimed (process peaked 1860; March 17, 1861)
- **What happened (fact):** Giuseppe Garibaldi's Expedition of the Thousand conquers Sicily and Naples in 1860, demonstrating how nationalist fervor and irregular warfare can dismantle established multi-ethnic empires.
- **The narrative *then*:** To European liberals, this was the springtime of peoples finally arriving, the proof that popular will could redraw maps that Metternich had thought fixed. Garibaldi in his red shirt was the romantic hero made flesh; his thousand volunteers, the demonstration that spirit could overcome numbers. To conservatives, it was anarchy licensed, the destruction of legitimate order by adventurers and carbonari. To Sicilian peasants, who had risen expecting land reform, it was a betrayal in progress—Garibaldi's alliance with Piedmont meant the preservation of property, the crushing of their own revolution. The Italian nationalist narrative required suppressing this double movement: the people had risen, and the people must be managed.
- **The narrative *now*:** Risorgimento as founding myth of modern Italy, Garibaldi as acceptable revolutionary—heroic enough for postage stamps, contained enough for state ceremony. The darker currents (the peasant revolts crushed, the South's subsequent impoverishment, the forced "Italianization" that followed unification) are acknowledged in academic circles but rarely penetrate the heroic frame. We use Italy 1860 as a template: nationalist movements succeed through charisma, foreign war, and the opportunism of existing states.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The American crisis was watched through this lens; Southern fire-eaters and Northern unionists alike read European papers. The demonstration that multi-ethnic empires could fragment along national lines was not lost on those contemplating the American map.

#### W-2 — Opening of the First Japanese Embassy to the US (February 1860)
- **What happened (fact):** The Tokugawa Shogunate sends its first official diplomatic mission to Washington aboard the *Kanrin Maru*, marking Japan's forced but rapid integration into the Western state system and global trade networks.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Americans, this was Manifest Destiny made maritime, Perry's "opening" of Japan validated and reciprocated. The embassy's arrival in San Francisco and procession to Washington were reported as the natural submission of the closed East to the commercial republic of the West. For the Japanese delegation, the narrative was more fractured: some saw necessary adaptation, others humiliation dressed in protocol, still others genuine curiosity about a nation that had itself rebelled against empire. The Shogunate's decision to send the mission was already controversial among those who would, within a decade, overthrow it; the embassy itself became a sign of the Shogunate's modernizing competence or its fatal compromise, depending on faction.
- **The narrative *now*:** We treat this as the beginning of Japan's modernization, a necessary if painful integration into the "international community"—the phrase itself carrying assumptions about what constitutes legitimate statehood. The violence of the opening (Perry's guns, the unequal treaties) is acknowledged but framed as growing pains. The Japanese perspective, once invisible, has been partially recovered, though often through a lens of inevitable Westernization versus authentic tradition that may not match actual 1860 experience.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the global context of 1860: the expansion of Western commercial and diplomatic norms at gunpoint, the creation of what would become the "standard of civilization" that structured imperialism for a century. The American Civil War would be fought while this system was being constructed; the postwar American empire would operate within its logic.

#### W-3 — Death of King Frederick VII of Denmark (November 15, 1860)
- **What happened (fact):** The death of the childless king triggers a succession crisis over the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein, setting the stage for a European war driven by competing claims of nationality versus dynastic right.
- **The narrative *then*:** For German nationalists, this was the moment to complete what 1848 had failed to achieve: the incorporation of German populations under German rule, the application of the nationality principle that liberalism had championed. For Danes, it was the defense of a multi-ethnic kingdom against Prussian aggression, dynastic right against ethnic chauvinism. The duchies' mixed populations meant that "self-determination" was not a solution but a weapon, usable by whichever power could mobilize the right census categories. Bismarck, already calculating, saw opportunity; the small German states saw a test of whether nationalism would serve Prussia or Germany.
- **The narrative *now*:** A minor prelude to 1871, if remembered at all. The Schleswig-Holstein question's very complexity—dynastic law, national identity, geographic mixedness—makes it resistant to heroic narrative. We have inherited the nationality principle as generally legitimate, which makes this case uncomfortable: here it served Prussian expansion, crushed Danish integrity, and would be overridden by Bismarck when convenient. The episode surfaces occasionally as a warning about ethnic nationalism's ambiguities.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the European system testing the same questions that would tear the American republic: what makes a people, what makes a state legitimate, whether existing political forms can survive the application of national principle. The timing—simultaneous with American secession—was noted by contemporaries who wondered whether the mid-century nation-state was emerging everywhere or collapsing everywhere.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected to expose the structural inevitability of the American rupture: the splitting of the Democrats reveals the collapse of the only national institution capable of mediating sectional conflict, while the formation of the Confederacy demonstrates that secession was not a bluff but an immediate institutional response to the election result. Globally, the Italian unification provides a contemporary mirror for how nationalist movements dismantle old orders through irregular force, paralleling the Kansas militias, while the Japanese embassy highlights the rigidifying boundaries of the sovereign state system just as the U.S. fractures. I deliberately omitted the Pony Express (April 1860) because, while iconic, it was a fleeting technological stopgap overtaken almost immediately by the telegraph; focusing on it would distract from the deeper infrastructural shifts in rail and industry that actually determined the war's logistics. Similarly, I excluded the specific details of the John Brown memorialization to avoid redundancy with the 1859 carry-forward, prioritizing instead the *consequences* of his act—the party split and secession—over the martyrdom itself.

## 2. Connections Within the Year

- **U.S. ↔ World:** The fracturing of the American Union and the unification of Italy are parallel manifestations of the mid-19th-century crisis of the multi-ethnic or sectional empire; both demonstrate that the old diplomatic balance (Vienna 1815) could no longer contain the kinetic energy of nationalist self-determination, whether expressed through Garibaldi's red shirts or the Southern Fire-Eaters' ordinances of secession.
- **Event ↔ Event:** The Democratic Convention's splintering (US-1) was the necessary structural precondition for Lincoln's election (US-2), which in turn acted as the immediate trigger for the Confederate formation (US-3); simultaneously, the Japanese Embassy's arrival (W-2) signals the global compression of time and space via steam and telegraph, ensuring that the American rupture would instantly become a geopolitical variable for London and Paris, just as the Danish succession crisis (W-3) reveals the fragility of dynastic legitimacy when confronted by the rising force of ethnic nationality.
- **Invisible / structural (shared hidden drivers):** The collision between static legal frameworks (the U.S. Constitution's silence on secession; the Danish dynastic laws; the Tokugawa isolation edicts) and dynamic material realities (the cotton economy's global leverage, the spread of print-capitalism fostering national consciousness, and the steam-powered acceleration of information) created a year where institutional inertia shattered under the weight of incompatible modernities.

1860 is the year the nineteenth century's central myth—progress through integration, the expansion of the liberal commercial order—hit the wall of its own contradictions and kept driving. Everywhere the stories people told about what they were doing outran what they could possibly deliver, and the gap between narrative and capacity became the space in which the catastrophes of the next decade would gestate.

Consider the American Democratic Party, which in 1860 still claimed to be the natural governing party of the republic, the heir to Jefferson and Jackson, the vessel of popular sovereignty itself. Its fracture was not simply a failure of political management, though it was that; it was the collapse of a story that had sustained American politics for forty years. The Democracy had promised that white men's freedom was compatible with Black enslavement, that Western expansion could absorb all sectional tensions, that the party could be simultaneously the party of the Northern working man and the Southern planter. By 1860 this narrative required such elaborate maintenance—popular sovereignty as a formula that meant opposite things in Chicago and Vicksburg—that its final rupture was almost a relief, a confession that the story could no longer be told with a straight face. What is remarkable is not that the party split but that so many continued to believe, even as it split, that some last-minute compromise could restore the tale. The narrative *then* was not of inevitable war but of temporary dysfunction, a fever that would break. We inherit the knowledge that the fever was terminal, and we must work to recover the genuine surprise of contemporaries who watched Charleston and Baltimore unfold.

#### 🜄 Heretic
The Herald is right to locate 1860 as a year of narrative collapse, but the story he tells is already too comfortable. He describes the Democratic Party’s fracture as a failure of storytelling, a contradiction that could no longer be maintained with a straight face. That framing treats the party as a vessel of ideas that simply broke under pressure. It omits the obvious: the Democratic Party did not fracture because its story failed. It fractured because the planter class, having dominated the party for decades through the three-fifths clause and the Senate’s parity rules, decided that even a nominally hostile president was an existential threat to the property regime that made them rich. The split was not a tragedy of ideas; it was a power play by men who had already decided that secession was preferable to any outcome in which their human chattel—four billion dollars of capital, the largest single asset class in the American economy—might be governed by a president they did not control. The Charleston walkout was choreographed by the same men who would, within months, sign ordinances of secession. The narrative of “sectional fracture” launders a coordinated exit strategy into a sad inevitability.

Steelman first: the received story of the Democratic Convention is that the party was torn apart by irreconcilable interpretations of popular sovereignty, that the Southern demand for a federal slave code in the territories was a principled constitutional position, and that the split was the organic outcome of deep ideological divergence. This is what the participants themselves said. It is not entirely false. But it is also a story that serves the interests of the men who walked out. It makes their action appear reactive, defensive, a last resort forced upon them by Northern aggression. It obscures the fact that the Southern delegates arrived in Charleston with instructions to bolt if they did not get their way—and that “their way” meant not merely a platform plank but a guarantee that slavery would expand, that the federal government would protect it, and that no future president could ever threaten it. The split was not a breakdown of the party; it was a pre-planned demonstration that the party was already dead as a national institution unless it surrendered entirely to planter interests. The “two candidates” outcome—Douglas, who would not give them the slave code, and Breckinridge, who would—was not an accident. It was the desired result of a faction that had already decided that losing the presidency was an acceptable price for forcing the constitutional crisis they wanted.

Now Lincoln. The Herald notes that the Republican Party was a coalition of former Whigs, Free-Soilers, and abolitionists who agreed on little beyond slavery’s non-extension. True. But what he omits is that this coalition won the presidency with only 39.8 percent of the popular vote—a minority presidency in a four-way race—and that this victory was made possible not by a groundswell of anti-slavery sentiment but by the demographic and electoral architecture that had been engineered over decades to favor Northern white voters. Lincoln received zero electoral votes from the South not because the South was monolithic but because in most Southern states, Lincoln’s name did not even appear on the ballot. The electoral system that delivered him the presidency was one in which Southern white men voted, but Southern Black people—four million of them—were counted as three-fifths of a person for apportionment, inflating Southern representation in Congress and the Electoral College while denying those same people any political voice. The irony is sharp and deliberate: the very constitutional provision that gave the slave states disproportionate power was now being used to argue that Lincoln’s election was illegitimate because he had no Southern mandate. The “minority president” complaint, wielded by secessionists, was a rhetorical weapon forged from the same constitutional compromise they had defended for seventy years. The gap between word and deed here is not subtle. Men who had built their political power on the suppression of Black votes now cried tyranny because a president was elected without their consent. The silence in the archive is the voice of the enslaved, whose non-votes were the foundation of the entire edifice.

The formation of the Confederacy is the event where the official story does the most work to obscure motive. The received narrative, even in critical tellings, often frames secession as a response to Lincoln’s election—a precipitate, panicked overreaction by a proud but doomed civilization. The Confederate constitution is sometimes noted for its protection of slavery, but the emphasis is usually on the act of secession itself, the drama of departure. What this framing omits is that the Confederacy’s founders were not panicked. They were prepared. The secession conventions that convened in December 1860 and January 1861 were not spontaneous outbursts of popular fury; they were the culmination of a decade of organized political work by the so-called “fire-eaters”—William Yancey, Robert Barnwell Rhett, Edmund Ruffin—who had been agitating for a Southern republic since the 1850s, who had walked out of the Charleston convention precisely to force this outcome, and who now moved with remarkable speed and coordination to seize federal arsenals, forts, and customs houses before Lincoln was even inaugurated. The Confederate constitution itself is a document of stunning candor if you read it without the filter of Lost Cause nostalgia. It does not merely protect slavery; it mandates it. Article I, Section 9, Clause 4: “No bill of attainder, ex post facto law, or law denying or impairing the right of property in negro slaves shall be passed.” The right to own human beings is made explicit and unamendable. Alexander Stephens, the Confederate vice president, declared in his “Cornerstone Speech” of March 1861 that the Confederacy’s “foundations are laid, its cornerstone rests, upon the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man; that slavery—subordination to the superior race—is his natural and normal condition.” This is not a side note. This is the founding principle, stated plainly by the second-highest official of the new government. The comfortable version—that the war was about states’ rights, or tariffs, or cultural difference—is a post-war invention, a narrative constructed by the losers to make their cause respectable. The men who built the Confederacy in 1860-61 knew exactly what they were doing, and they said so. The omission of their own words from the popular memory is itself a political act, one that served the interests of white reconciliation in the decades after the war and continues to serve them now.

Turn to Italy. The unification proclaimed in 1861, driven by Garibaldi’s Expedition of the Thousand in 1860, is the European event most often narrated as a triumph of liberal nationalism—the people rising against decaying dynasties, the romantic hero with his red shirts, the fulfillment of Mazzini’s vision. The Herald gestures at this when he speaks of “nationalist fervor and irregular warfare dismantling established multi-ethnic empires.” But cui bono? The unification of Italy was not a popular revolution. It was a conquest by the Kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia, engineered by Count Cavour through a combination of French military alliance, diplomatic manipulation, and the opportunistic co-option of Garibaldi’s southern campaign. When Garibaldi conquered Sicily and Naples, he did so with the tacit support of the British navy, which prevented Bourbon reinforcements from reaching the island, and with the active encouragement of Cavour, who then moved swiftly to annex the liberated territories into a Piedmontese-dominated monarchy before Garibaldi’s republican allies could establish an alternative government. The plebiscites that ratified unification were held under conditions of occupation, with no secret ballot, and produced suspiciously unanimous results—99.2 percent in favor in Naples, for instance. The “nationalist fervor” was real among a literate, urban minority, but the southern peasantry, the contadini, experienced unification not as liberation but as the imposition of Piedmontese taxes, conscription, and property laws that favored northern landowners. The brigandage that erupted in the Mezzogiorno in the 1860s—a bloody counter-insurgency that killed more Italians than all the wars of unification combined—is the event that the liberal narrative must omit, because it reveals that “Italy” was not a nation waiting to be born but a territory conquered by one region and imposed on others. The silence of the southern peasant is the absence that indicts the story. The unification of Italy was a power play by the Piedmontese elite, dressed in the language of Risorgimento, and its legacy is the structural underdevelopment of the Italian South that persists to this day.

The Japanese embassy to the United States in 1860 is the event where the Herald’s framing—integration into the Western state system—is most seductive and most misleading. The official story, then and now, is that Commodore Perry’s 1853-54 expedition “opened” Japan to the world, that the Tokugawa Shogunate recognized the inevitability of modernization, and that the 1860 embassy represented a dignified entry into the community of nations. The Japanese envoys were received in Washington with great ceremony; they toured New York and Philadelphia; they were feted as symbols of a new era. But the narrative omits the gun. Perry’s “opening” was an act of naval coercion—he sailed warships into Edo Bay, trained his cannons on the city, and refused to leave until the Shogunate signed a treaty granting the United States extraterritorial rights and favorable trade terms. The 1860 embassy was not a gesture of friendship; it was a mission undertaken under duress, required by the unequal treaty of 1858, which the United States had imposed after Britain and France had already extracted similar concessions. The Japanese delegates were not ambassadors of a sovereign nation freely choosing its place in the world; they were representatives of a government that had been forced to accept the humiliation of foreign gunboats in its harbors, foreign merchants in its treaty ports, and foreign legal jurisdiction over its own soil. The embassy was a performance of sovereignty designed to mask its erosion. The gap between the rhetoric of “opening” and the reality of coercion is the gap that the comfortable version fills with talk of cultural exchange and diplomatic firsts. What is omitted is the internal Japanese experience: the embassy deepened the crisis of the Tokugawa regime, emboldened the domains that opposed the Shogunate’s capitulation to the foreigners, and accelerated the chain of events that would lead to the Boshin War and the Meiji Restoration in 1868. The “integration into the Western state system” was, for Japan, a traumatic violation that destabilized its political order and ultimately produced a revolutionary transformation imposed from above—one that would, within a generation, make Japan itself an imperial power capable of the same gunboat diplomacy it had suffered. The embassy of 1860 is not a story of progress; it is the first act of a tragedy of compelled modernization whose costs were borne by the Japanese peasantry and whose benefits accrued to Western merchants and, eventually, to a new Japanese oligarchy.

#### 🜃 Cartographer
The year 1860 is not a collection of discrete shocks but a singular tectonic grinding of slow variables against rigid institutions. To understand why the United States fractured while Italy coalesced, one must look beneath the rhetoric of "rights" and "honor" to the geography of production and the topology of communication. The primary force at work is the exhaustion of the compromise form. For forty years, the American political system functioned as a pressure valve, delaying the confrontation between two distinct modes of production: the industrializing, wage-labor North and the expanding, slave-labor South. By 1860, the geographic container could no longer hold the economic contradiction. The Kansas-Nebraska Act had already dissolved the Missouri Compromise line, but the Democratic Convention in Charleston revealed the deeper truth: there was no longer a national party capable of straddling the sectional divide because the underlying economic interests had diverged irreconcilably. The split of the Democrats (US-1) was not a tactical error; it was the structural inevitability of a party trying to represent a continent that had ceased to be a single market in any meaningful social sense.

This internal fracture maps directly onto the global surge of nationalism seen in Italy (W-1). The "nation-state" was becoming the only viable unit of sovereignty in an industrializing world. In Europe, this meant shedding the multi-ethnic baggage of the Habsburgs or the patchwork of German principalities. In America, it meant the sheddings of the Union itself. The Confederacy (US-3) was not merely a rebellion; it was an attempt to forge a new nation-state on the principle of racialized agrarian capitalism, mirroring how Cavour and Garibaldi were forging Italy on principles of liberal nationalism. Both projects relied on the belief that a people defined by a specific cultural or economic identity possessed the right to sovereign statehood. The difference, fatal to the South, was that the Confederate definition of "the people" excluded a third of its population, creating a demographic fault line that would eventually collapse its military capacity.
