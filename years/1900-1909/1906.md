## 0. Context Carried Forward

- **Thread — `labor-arbitration`:** The fracture between the AFL's craft exclusivity and the IWW's industrial radicalism has moved from ideological debate to physical confrontation; entering 1906, the IWW initiates coordinated free-speech fights in Western mining towns, forcing local municipalities to choose between constitutional toleration and the extralegal violence required to break unlicensed union organizing.
- **Thread — `imperial-engineering`:** With the biological threat of yellow fever and malaria statistically neutralized, the Panama Canal Zone has transformed into a massive logistical machine; 1906 marks the pivot where the primary constraint shifts from worker mortality to the sheer volume of earth removal, demanding an unprecedented integration of steam shovel technology, rail logistics, and federal supply chains.
- **Thread — `philippine-governance`:** The military suppression of the Moro resistance has transitioned into a bureaucratic regime of segregation; 1906 sees the formal codification of "non-Christian" provinces, institutionalizing a legal dualism that separates the archipelago along religious and ethnic lines, effectively exporting the logic of American Jim Crow to the Pacific frontier.
- **Thread — `western-hydrology`:** The federal assertion of water rights under the Reclamation Service is no longer theoretical; as concrete dams rise in the West, the abstract concept of federal supremacy clashes with established riparian doctrines, igniting cross-border litigation with Mexico and interstate friction that challenges the very legality of the Newlands Act's central planning.
- **Thread — `global-alignments`:** The geopolitical vacuum left by Russia's collapse and Japan's ascent demands a new Pacific equilibrium; 1906 is the year of the tacit bargain, where the U.S. prepares to formally exchange recognition of Japanese suzerainty over Korea for the security of its own Philippine holdings, cementing a racialized sphere-of-influence doctrine.
- **Thread — `technocratic-conservation`:** Pinchot's "wise use" philosophy is expanding its territorial claim from timber to energy; 1906 brings the first aggressive federal withdrawals of water-power sites, provoking a fierce legislative backlash from Western developers who view the retention of hydraulic potential by the state as an unconstitutional seizure of local economic destiny.

## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — San Francisco Earthquake and Fire (April 18, 1906)
- **What happened (fact):** A magnitude 7.9 earthquake strikes Northern California, followed by fires that destroy over 80% of San Francisco, killing roughly 3,000 people and triggering a massive federal relief effort managed by the Army.
- **The narrative *then*:** A city of pioneers, risen from Gold Rush mud to continental glory, tested by nature's fury and answering with indomitable American will. The earthquake becomes secondary to the fire in popular telling—a crucial elision, since fire suggests human agency, insurance, and civic character rather than geologic inevitability. General Funston's Army management of relief is celebrated as martial efficiency saving republican enterprise; the burned district is already "the phoenix city" in editorials before the embers cool. The subjugated narrative: the Chinese quarter's destruction is greeted with relief by many, its refugees herded to segreg camps, the disaster an opportunity to complete what exclusion laws began.
- **The narrative *now*:** A foundational tale of urban resilience and emergency management, studied in disaster-preparedness courses as precedent for federal-local coordination. The fire's dominance over earthquake in memory persists—our "Great San Francisco Fire" instinct. The Chinese experience, the Army's rough justice, the insurance companies' evasions (blaming "earthquake" clauses to avoid fire payouts): these surface in revisionist scholarship but rarely penetrate the mythic frame. The city we now imagine rebuilt in Beaux-Arts splendor was built, in part, on seized Chinese property and rewritten policies.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The template for American disaster narrative: nature strikes, community unifies, federal power arrives as temporary tutor in self-government. Every hurricane, every 9/11, inherits this grammar. The Army's role prefigures permanent federal emergency capacity; the elided Chinese suffering prefigures whose devastation gets memorialized and whose gets forgotten.

#### US-2 — Passage of the Pure Food and Drug Act (June 30, 1906)
- **What happened (fact):** President Roosevelt signs legislation prohibiting the sale of misbranded or adulterated food and drugs, establishing the legal precedent for federal consumer protection and creating the precursor to the FDA.
- **The narrative *then*:** A moral crusade culminating, the triumph of exposed truth over corporate poison. Sinclair's *The Jungle* (published February 1906) is the sacred text—Roosevelt's breakfast-table disgust, the presidential eye-opener. Harvey Wiley's "Poison Squad" of government chemists becomes folk heroism. The story is Progress as conversion narrative: old laissez-faire man sees the filth, feels the revulsion, acts the state. The law's limits—no pre-market approval, weak enforcement, the meat inspection provisions actually serving large packers by raising barriers to smaller competitors—are invisible in the glow.
- **The narrative *now*:** The birth of the administrative state protecting the consumer, origin point for FDA, EPA, OSHA, the whole alphabet of federal guardianship. We teach it as inevitable response to industrialization's hazards, a milestone in government's expanding benignity. The Progressive synthesis: market failure, muckraking exposure, regulatory correction. The darker genealogy—how regulatory capture began here, how Wiley was eventually sidelined, how the act's passage actually consolidated certain corporate interests—belongs to specialist knowledge, not civic catechism.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The master-narrative of American regulatory legitimacy: not socialism but disgust, not class politics but moral hygiene. Every subsequent consumer protection debate invokes 1906's template. The FDA's modern authority, the supplement industry's persistent challenge to its founding premise, the "regulatory state" as political football—all germinate here.

#### US-3 — Hepburn Act Enactment (June 29, 1906)
- **What happened (fact):** Congress grants the Interstate Commerce Commission the power to set maximum railroad rates and extend its jurisdiction to pipelines and ferries, marking the apex of Progressive Era regulatory expansion over private capital.
- **The narrative *then*:** Roosevelt's "strenuous life" applied to economic power—the bully pulpit made substantive. The railroads as octopus, the Granger movement vindicated, the people's champion against the trusts. Contemporary telling emphasizes presidential will: Roosevelt's secret negotiations, his threat to disclose railroad rate manipulation, his mastery of public opinion to cow Senate opposition. The act as personal triumph, democratic control over the infrastructure of continental empire. The railroads' adaptation—learning to work the commission, the long game of regulatory capture—waits in shadows.
- **The narrative *now*:** A waystation to the modern administrative state, less remembered than the Sherman Act or New Deal agencies. When invoked, it's "Progressive Era reform," vague benevolence. The specific mechanism—rate-setting as price control, the ICC's eventual sclerosis and 1980 abolition—complicates heroic memory. Our present fights over net neutrality, airline regulation, platform monopolies: these echo Hepburn's questions without naming it. The railroads as problem solved, history done; we forget they were the platforms of their day, and rate-setting the algorithmic governance of its moment.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The experiment in technocratic economic management, its ambitions and its failures, directly inform present ambivalence about regulatory power. The ICC's arc—activist origins, industry capture, eventual deregulation—is the cautionary tale both sides of our political divide invoke without knowing.

#### W-1 — Algeciras Conference Concludes (April 7, 1906)
- **What happened (fact):** European powers and the U.S. agree to recognize Moroccan independence while granting France and Spain control over police and banking operations, exposing the fragility of the Concert of Europe and foreshadowing the alliance rigidities of WWI.
- **The narrative *then*:** A diplomatic triumph, the crisis surmounted, the old system working. Roosevelt's mediation earns him the Nobel Prize in retrospect (1906 award, though the negotiation was 1905); American self-congratulation at having "saved peace" while emerging as Pacific imperial power. European telling varies by alliance: Germans feel humiliated, their gunboat diplomacy checked; French see legitimate interest advanced; British breathe relief at Entente preserved. The fragility is felt only by pessimists—"a peace that is no peace" in some diplomatic memoranda, drowned out by official satisfaction.
- **The narrative *now*:** The moment the pre-war system showed its cracks, studied as origin of the 1914 catastrophe. We know the sequel: Agadir 1911, the blank check, the guns of August. The conference becomes, in retrospect, the last successful conference, the false dawn. Roosevelt's role shrinks in memory; the structural logic of alliance polarization expands. "Moroccan independence" reads as transparent fiction, colonialism's hypocrisy exposed. The U.S. presence as emerging great power, still believing in European-style diplomacy it would soon abandon for "associationalism" and then isolation.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The grammar of great-power crisis management we still use: international conferences, great-power guarantees, "independence" as fig leaf for spheres of influence. Every Minsk agreement, every Geneva communique, inherits Algeciras's structure and risks its fate. The American belief that mediation suffices, that presence equals influence, tested here and found wanting by history though not by contemporaries.

#### W-2 — British Launch of HMS Dreadnought (December 2, 1906)
- **What happened (fact):** The Royal Navy commissions the first all-big-gun battleship, rendering every existing warship in the world obsolete overnight and initiating a frantic, budget-breaking naval arms race with Germany.
- **The narrative *then*:** British ingenuity, the naval supremacy that built empire renewed and secured. Admiral Fisher's revolution, the "two-power standard" made modern. German reaction—Tirpitz's rage, the accelerated program—confirms the wisdom: we innovate, they scramble to catch up. The arms race as competitive advantage, not collective suicide. Some naval officers fret about cost, about the Mediterranean left uncovered, about the gamble of all eggs in one revolutionary basket. These doubts circulate in service memoranda, not public myth.
- **The narrative *now*:** The arms race that helped cause the war, technological escalation as structural cause. Dreadnought as synecdoche for pre-war irrationality, the ship that made itself and all rivals simultaneously necessary and useless. We see the waste: millions spent, vast fleets meeting at Jutland to indecision, ultimate surrender at Scapa Flow. The technological determinism—can you stop once started?—haunts nuclear age, AI race, hypersonic missiles. Fisher's gamble looks like strategic hubris, Tirpitz's response like inevitable emulation.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The template for technological arms racing: first-mover advantage, obsolescence spiral, domestic political economy of military-industrial complexes. Every "Sputnik moment," every hypersonic panic, every "missile gap" claim inherits Dreadnought's logic. The British choice to build—could they have not?—remains contested, as does any hegemon's decision to accelerate competition.

#### W-3 — First Russian State Duma Convenes (April 27, 1906)
- **What happened (fact):** Following the 1905 Revolution, Tsar Nicholas II reluctantly opens Russia's first elected parliament, which is dissolved within ten weeks, demonstrating the incompatibility of autocratic survival with genuine representative governance.
- **The narrative *then*:** A false dawn, recognized as such by many participants. The October Manifesto's promise, the electoral excitement of a newly politicized society, the Kadet constitutionalists' hope against hope. The dissolution felt as betrayal by liberals, as vindication by revolutionaries who said parliamentarism was sham. Western observers: some see Russian backwardness confirmed, others see promise deferred. The narrative fractures immediately by political position, no stable "then" existing—unlike the earthquake's unified myth-making.
- **The narrative *now*:** The revolution that failed, 1905 as rehearsal for 1917, the Duma as doomed institution. We read backward through Bolshevik victory: the Kadets become naive, the Octoberists become complicit, Nicholas's bad faith becomes obvious. The Duma's actual achievements—legislative drafting, public debate, the habit of opposition—are invisible in the shadow of what came after. "Russia was never ready for democracy" becomes the lesson, applied to 1990s shock therapy, to Putin's consolidation, to eternal Russian exceptionalism.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The myth of Russian democratic incapacity, constructed here and reinforced across century. Every Western analysis of post-Soviet politics that invokes "no democratic tradition" traces to this moment's interpretation. The alternative path—what if Nicholas had sustained the Duma, what if Stolypin's reforms had combined with parliamentary legitimacy—belongs to counterfactual history, not usable past.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected because they collectively map the transition from informal influence to formal structural control: the Hepburn Act and Pure Food and Drug Act represent the federal government finally claiming sovereignty over the domestic marketplace, while the Dreadnought and Algeciras Conference reveal the hardening of global imperial blocs that would soon fracture into total war. The San Francisco disaster is included not merely for its tragedy, but as the ultimate stress test for the emerging technocratic state, where military engineering and federal finance replaced local charity as the primary response mechanism. I deliberately omitted the Brownsville Affair (November 1906); while critical for the history of race and the military, its full structural impact on the black soldier's place in the empire is more potently analyzed in the subsequent years of discharge and eventual redemption, whereas 1906's legislative trio defines the *capacity* of the state itself.

## 2. Connections Within the Year

- **U.S. ↔ World:** The U.S. turns inward to regulate domestic capital (Hepburn, Pure Food) while European powers turn outward to manage imperial competition (Algeciras, Dreadnought); both are attempts to impose administrative order on chaotic industrial expansion, but the U.S. builds institutional capacity while Europe builds alliance rigidity.
- **Event ↔ Event:** The San Francisco Earthquake (US-1) demonstrates the physical vulnerability of concentrated capital, creating the political urgency for the Pure Food (US-2) and Hepburn (US-3) Acts; disaster proves that unchecked systems fail, legitimizing federal intervention in private markets.
- **Invisible / structural:** The underlying driver is `industrial_scale_risk`: whether in food supply chains, railroad pricing, urban density, or naval warfare, technology has created systems too complex for local or laissez-faire management, demanding centralized bureaucratic control.

The year 1906 is the year the modern world learned to tell stories about itself in a new register—not the grand narrative of civilization's march, but the managed narrative of progress through crisis, the alchemy of disaster into renewal, of exposure into reform, of potential war into diplomatic success that is itself a kind of war deferred.

Here was rate-setting, price control, the administrative state intervening in market mechanism, and doing so with constitutional blessing. The narrative then was democratic control over the sinews of continental expansion; the narrative now, when invoked at all, is "Progressive reform" vague enough to avoid specifying what was attempted and what failed. #### 🜄 Heretic
Let me begin by granting the Herald their due. Something did shift in 1906. The idea that government could be an instrument of public protection rather than merely a mailed fist for property—this was not nothing. The Pure Food and Drug Act, the Hepburn Act, even the federal response to San Francisco: these were moments when the state claimed a new relationship to its citizens, one of guardianship rather than mere discipline. The rhetoric was sincere, at least for some of its advocates. Harvey Wiley genuinely believed in pure food. Roosevelt genuinely believed that concentrated wealth required countervailing power. The Duma, however brief, represented millions of Russians who genuinely believed representation might come. To dismiss all this as mere theater would be to commit the error the Herald warns against—flattening belief into epiphenomenon.

Now let us look at who paid, who profited, and whose voice the archive swallowed.

**San Francisco, April 18.** The official story is civic resurrection. The Army restored order. The city rebuilt. What this story omits is a second disaster, administered not by nature but by men with badges. The Chinese population of San Francisco—perhaps 15,000 souls, concentrated in a Chinatown that occupied some of the most valuable real estate in the city—lost everything in the fire. And then they lost more. City officials, led by Mayor Eugene Schmitz and the graft-ridden Union Labor Party machine, saw opportunity in the ashes. Plans to relocate Chinatown to Hunter’s Point, to seize the land for commercial development, were drawn up within days. The Chinese consul general had to intervene. Chinese merchants, denied insurance payouts by companies that cited "act of God" clauses while paying white claimants under identical policies, had to finance their own reconstruction. The narrative of unified suffering was itself an instrument of dispossession: if "San Francisco" suffered, then "San Francisco" would rebuild, and "San Francisco" meant those who already owned it. The 3,000 dead were disproportionately poor, disproportionately immigrant, disproportionately the people whose deaths did not disturb property titles. The Army’s relief camps were segregated. The official death toll was almost certainly manipulated downward to protect real estate values and the city’s commercial reputation. *Cui bono* from the story of heroic recovery? The landowners who kept their land. The insurance companies that found reasons not to pay. The political machine that used the crisis to consolidate power. The Chinese who rebuilt Chinatown anyway did so against the grain of the narrative, not within it.

**The Pure Food and Drug Act, June 30.** The steelman: a triumph of muckraking journalism and scientific advocacy over industrial poisoners. Upton Sinclair exposed the stockyards; Roosevelt read *The Jungle*; the law passed. Here is what the comfortable version omits. The act was supported by the largest meatpackers—Armour, Swift, Morris—because federal inspection meant a government seal that would open European markets that had closed to American meat. The costs of compliance fell heaviest on small, local slaughterhouses that could not afford the inspection infrastructure. The act was, in part, a competitive weapon wielded by consolidated capital against its smaller rivals, dressed in the language of public health. The labeling provisions were written to be enforceable primarily against patent medicine manufacturers, not the pharmaceutical industry in its emerging corporate form. The law regulated *misbranding*, not *safety*—you could sell poison, provided you labeled it honestly. The Poison Squad’s drama was real, but the legislative outcome was shaped less by Wiley’s heroism than by the convergence of interests between reformist bureaucrats, large packers seeking market advantage, and a Republican Party eager to neutralize Democratic attacks on its corporate ties in an election year. The voices absent from the celebratory narrative: the small producers crushed by compliance costs, the Black farmers and butchers excluded from the regulated market, the consumers who got a label but not necessarily safety.

**The Hepburn Act, June 29.** The received story is the apex of Progressive regulation, the moment the Interstate Commerce Commission finally got teeth. Roosevelt stared down the Senate Old Guard. The railroads would no longer set rates by fiat. What this omits is that the railroads, by 1906, had already begun to see regulation as preferable to the alternative: rate wars that destroyed profit margins, state-level regulations that varied unpredictably, and the constant threat of more radical legislation. The Hepburn Act gave the ICC rate-setting power, but it also gave railroads a stable, predictable environment in which they could coordinate without technically colluding. The act’s judicial review provisions were a gift to the carriers—every rate decision could be tied up in court for years. The railroads did not lose in 1906; they traded the chaos of competition for the managed stability of a regulatory regime they could influence, litigate, and eventually capture. The real losers were the shippers and farmers who found that "maximum rates" quickly became the standard rates, and that the ICC’s ponderous procedures offered little relief. The act was a compromise dressed as a victory, and the dressing was the point.

**Algeciras, April 7.** The narrative: diplomacy preserved Moroccan independence and averted war. The reality: Morocco was partitioned in all but name, its "independence" a legal fiction under which French and Spanish police and bankers exercised actual control. Germany got a face-saving role in an international bank. The United States, under Roosevelt’s mediation, got the appearance of disinterested statesmanship while in fact backing the Anglo-French entente. The conference did not preserve the Concert of Europe; it revealed that the Concert was already dead, replaced by rigid alliance blocs that would make a Balkan crisis into a world war. The Moroccans themselves were not represented at the conference that decided their fate. Their voice is the silence at the center of the diplomatic record. The "independence" recognized at Algeciras was the independence of a corpse, kept nominally alive so that no European power had to admit it had killed it.

**HMS Dreadnought, December 2.** The story is technological revolution: Fisher’s vision, the all-big-gun ship, the obsolescence of every existing battleship overnight. The omitted story is the arms race as a structural necessity of industrial capitalism. The Dreadnought did not merely render German ships obsolete; it rendered British ships obsolete too, wiping out the Royal Navy’s numerical advantage and forcing Britain to compete on a new, level playing field. The ship was a gift to armaments manufacturers—Vickers, Armstrong Whitworth—who would profit from the ensuing race regardless of who "won." The naval scare was fed by a press that sold papers through invasion panic. The Dreadnought was not merely a ship; it was a fiscal commitment that locked Britain and Germany into a cycle of expenditure neither could escape without appearing to capitulate. The working classes of both nations paid for these ships in taxes and in the social spending that was deferred to finance them. The Dreadnought’s true cost was measured not in pounds sterling but in hospitals not built, schools not funded, pensions not paid. The silent voices are the taxpayers and the conscripts of 1914.

**The First Duma, April 27.** The narrative is tentative constitutionalism: the Tsar, forced by revolution, grants a parliament. The reality is that Nicholas II granted the Duma with the explicit intention of ignoring it. The Fundamental Laws, issued just before the Duma convened, reserved autocratic power over foreign policy, the military, and the appointment of ministers. The Duma’s demand for land reform was met with dissolution after ten weeks. The Kadets who dominated the First Duma believed they were building a constitutional order; the Tsar believed he was buying time. Both could not be right. The Duma was not a step toward representative government; it was a pressure valve, designed to release enough steam to prevent explosion while changing nothing. The peasants who had risen in 1905, the workers who had formed soviets, the national minorities who had demanded autonomy—none of them found their aspirations represented in a parliament that the autocracy treated as a decorative inconvenience. The dissolution of the First Duma was not a failure of liberal constitutionalism; it was the autocracy functioning as designed.

What unites these six events is the gap between the story told and the power exercised. In every case, the narrative of progress—recovery, reform, diplomacy, innovation, representation—served to legitimate outcomes that preserved existing hierarchies, enriched established interests, and silenced those who paid the real costs. The year 1906 was not a year of breakthroughs. It was a year of alibis, brilliantly constructed, still believed.

### Round 2 — Cross-Examination

**Heretic → Herald:**

I grant you this, Herald, and I grant it fully: the narrative machinery you describe is real and consequential. When you say that San Francisco taught America to narrate catastrophe as civic triumph, you are describing something that actually happened—the suppression of the earthquake in favor of the fire, the elevation of the soldier-hero, the alchemy of disaster into a story of renewal. And when you extend this to the Pure Food and Drug Act, you are right that something new entered American political culture: the idea that exposure—of adulterated meat, of patent medicines laced with morphine—could mobilize a public that would then demand reform. The muckrakers were not imaginary. *The Jungle* was not imaginary. The disgust it provoked was not manufactured out of nothing. There is a genuine democratic moment here, a moment when the governed used information to constrain the governors and the industrialists. I concede the sincerity of the belief, and I concede that the narrative you describe—progress through crisis—became a durable template for American reform.

Now let me press where the template cracks.

You say the rebuilding began in language before it began in brick. Yes. But *whose* language? The language of the Chamber of Commerce, of the banks that held mortgages on the destroyed district, of the insurance companies that faced ruin if the disaster were narrated as earthquake rather than fire. The decision to call it "The Fire" was not merely a cultural reflex toward heroism. It was a financial decision, because most insurance policies covered fire but excluded earthquake. The narrative you celebrate as civic alchemy was, at its foundation, a mechanism for protecting capital. The same insurance companies that invoked "act of God" clauses to deny Chinese merchants then lobbied the city to prevent Chinatown's relocation, because the land was too valuable to leave in the hands of its former occupants. The civic triumph you describe was built on a double expropriation: first the physical destruction, then the narrative one, then the legal one.

And the Pure Food and Drug Act—you are right that it was a conversion of exposure into reform. But let us ask who was exposed, and who was protected. The meatpacking industry was exposed, yes. But *The Jungle* was read by white, native-born consumers as a story about what immigrants were doing to *their* food. The disgust was directed at the bodies of the workers—Lithuanian, Polish, Slovak—as much as at the meat. The Act that followed regulated product, not workplace. It protected the consumer, not the laborer. The worker who fell into the rendering vat remained invisible to the law, because the law was written for the palate of the middle class, not the spine of the immigrant. The narrative of reform laundered a class project into a universal one.

So I ask you, Herald: when you trace the template from San Francisco to Katrina, you acknowledge the grammar of selective visibility. But is that selectivity a flaw in an otherwise noble narrative machinery, or is it the machinery's *function*? Does the story of crisis-and-renewal work *because* it excludes those whose suffering cannot be narrated as heroic—the Chinese whose displacement was opportunity, the worker whose death was an externality, the colonized subject whose land was the precondition for the very prosperity being rebuilt? If the template requires invisibility to operate, is it a template of progress, or a template of power managing its own contradictions?

**Herald → Heretic:**

You argue, Heretic, that the narrative machinery of 1906 was not a democratic achievement but a mechanism of expropriation—that the civic triumph was a cover for capital, that reform protected consumers at the expense of workers, that the grammar of selective visibility was not a bug but the operating system. You marshal evidence: the insurance companies, the Chinatown land grab, the immigrant body rendered invisible by the very law that promised purity. This is a powerful indictment, and I will not defend the indefensible. The treatment of San Francisco's Chinese population was a moral atrocity. The gap between the Pure Food and Drug Act's protection of the consumer and its indifference to the worker is real and damning.

But your argument, pushed to its limit, commits an error you yourself warned against. You flatten belief into epiphenomenon. You treat the narrative as *only* a weapon of the powerful, and in doing so, you erase the people who wielded it against power and won.

Consider who told the story of "The Fire." It was not only the Chamber of Commerce. It was also the residents of the Mission District who organized their own relief stations. It was the Chinese merchants who, using the narrative of civic membership, petitioned the consul and the courts to block relocation. It was the thousands of refugees who accepted aid from the Army and then demanded accountability for its distribution. The narrative of civic rebirth was not merely imposed from above; it was seized from below, because it offered a language in which the dispossessed could make claims. The Chinese community did not reject the story of San Francisco's renewal—they demanded inclusion in it. That they were betrayed does not mean the story was only a trap. It means it was a contested field, and the contest was real.

The same is true of pure food. You are right that the Act protected consumers and not workers. But *who were the consumers*? They were not only the middle class. They were working-class families who bought canned goods, immigrant mothers who purchased milk for their children, the poor who were most vulnerable to adulterated medicine because they could not afford doctors.
