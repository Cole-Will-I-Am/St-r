## 0. Context Carried Forward

- **Thread — `neutral entrapment`:** Entering 1810, the Non-Intercourse Act has collapsed into farce; with no navy to enforce it and British merchants openly flouting the ban, Madison faces a binary choice between accepting permanent vassalage to British maritime law or mobilizing for a war the nation's infrastructure cannot yet support.
- **Thread — `the indigenous firewall`:** Tecumseh's confederacy has hardened from a diplomatic coalition into a militarized state-within-a-state; following the winter councils, his forces now control the movement of goods and people across the Old Northwest, effectively vetoing U.S. sovereignty west of the Ohio River.
- **Thread — `the sectional arithmetic`:** The internal slave trade has accelerated as Upper South planters, facing soil exhaustion and low crop prices, increasingly rely on the sale of human capital to Deep South cotton barons, cementing a financial interdependence that makes the political defense of slavery a national, not merely regional, imperative.
- **Thread — `the imperial paradox`:** The insurgency in East Florida has matured into an open revolt backed by U.S. proxies; Madison now controls a rebel government he cannot officially recognize without triggering a wider war with Spain or Britain, exposing the fragility of executive power when operating outside constitutional declarations of war.
- **Thread — `the contested transfer`:** The judicial pushback in New England has evolved into a constitutional crisis; Federalist courts are not only blocking enforcement but actively legitimizing smuggling as a defense of property rights, fracturing the federal monopoly on violence and commerce regulation.

## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — Macon's Bill No. 2 (May 1, 1810)
- **What happened (fact):** Congress repealed the Non-Intercourse Act and offered to restore trade with whichever belligerent—Britain or France—first dropped restrictions on American neutral shipping. Napoleon, through his minister the Duc de Cadore, responded with a deceptive promise that France would comply, which Madison accepted despite British warnings of fraud.
- **The narrative *then*:** This was pragmatic flexibility, a republican commercial state using its economic leverage as a weapon of peace. Jeffersonians believed they had found the formula: play the two tyrannies against each other, let commerce be the arbiter of European morality. The Cadore Letter of August 1810 was read as vindication—Napoleon had blinked. Federalists warned of humiliation; Republicans heard only the squealing of Anglophile merchants.
- **The narrative *now*:** A case study in structural naivety, the moment the young republic's commercial diplomacy met the master manipulator of the age. Textbooks render it as the path to inevitable war: Napoleon played Madison, the embargo's logic collapsed, and coercion of Britain became the only remaining option. The "choice" between belligerents was illusory.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The hinge between Jefferson's commercial warfare and Madison's eventual declaration of war. It reveals how American self-conception as a neutral trading power collided with a European system that had no place for neutrals.

#### US-2 — Census of 1810 (August 6, 1810)
- **What happened (fact):** The third federal census counted 7,239,881 Americans, with Ohio's population quintupling since 1800, Indiana and Illinois territories showing explosive growth, and enslaved persons comprising over 17% of the total, heavily concentrated in the Deep South and Chesapeake tobacco counties.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Republicans, proof of the agrarian empire's vitality—Yeo-men spreading across the West, the numerical triumph of free labor. For Federalists in New England, a warning of diminishing political weight. For southern planters, a census of property that confirmed their political calculus: three-fifths of the enslaved counted toward representation, an arithmetic of power they had fought to embed in 1787 and now saw bearing fruit.
- **The narrative *now*:** The demographic infrastructure of civil war made visible. The census is read as a prophecy—the western surge that would demand internal improvements and bank controversies; the enslaved concentration that would make cotton king and secession thinkable. The three-fifths clause operates here as original sin quantified.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The first census to capture the full geographic momentum of post-Louisiana expansion, and the starkest numerical portrait of the slavery compromise's political economy before the Missouri crisis makes it explosive.

#### US-3 — Battle of Tippecanoe (November 7, 1810)
- **What happened (fact):** [Fact correction: The major battle occurs November 7, 1811; 1810 saw Harrison's military buildup at Vincennes, his September council with Tecumseh at Grouseland that ended in threats, and escalating frontier skirmishes that made armed confrontation probable.] Harrison mobilized Indiana militia and regulars, interpreting Tecumseh's confederacy as British-sponsored aggression requiring preemptive action.
- **The narrative *then*:** On the frontier, 1810 was the year of gathering storm—Harrison as vigilant defender, Tecumseh as the orator of a terrifying unity that threatened to reverse the Northwest's "settlement." Eastern newspapers caught fragments: a charismatic "prophet," warriors gathering at Prophetstown, the specter of 1780s border warfare revived. Harrison's reports shaped the story before the battle; he was already constructing the narrative of necessary aggression.
- **The narrative *now*:** Tippecanoe is remembered as the 1811 battle that made Harrison president, the slogan "Tippecanoe and Tyler Too." The prehistory in 1810 is largely elided, yet it was here that Harrison committed to military solution over negotiation, that Tecumseh's diplomatic journey south left his brother vulnerable, that the frontier's logic of preemptive violence was set. The 1810 buildup is the decision point obscured by the 1811 spectacle.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The temporal compression matters. Harrison's 1810 mobilization and the Grouseland confrontation were narrated then as defensive preparation, but represent the active choice for war that 1811 merely executed. The myth requires the elision.

#### W-1 — French Annexation of Holland (July 9, 1810)
- **What happened (fact):** Napoleon formally deposed his brother Louis Bonaparte as King of Holland and incorporated the kingdom into the French Empire, extending the Continental System's prohibitions to Dutch ports and intensifying the seizure of American vessels attempting neutral trade.
- **The narrative *then*:** For American merchants and diplomats, one more brutal demonstration that Europe recognized no neutral rights. The annexation was read through the lens of Macon's Bill: if France absorbed Holland, which belligerent was even capable of restoring trade? Some Republicans clung to the Cadore promise as distinguishing French behavior; others saw the Continent closing entirely.
- **The narrative *now*:** A minor entry in Napoleonic consolidation, significant mainly for showing the Continental System's totalizing logic. For American history, it illustrates the narrowing of neutral commercial space that made the War of 1812 economically overdetermined—though the standard narrative prefers to emphasize British impressment as the sufficient cause.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The material context that makes Macon's Bill's diplomacy farcical. Napoleon was not a potential partner for neutral trade; he was dismantling the very commercial infrastructure Americans hoped to use.

#### W-2 — Mexican War of Independence Begins (September 16, 1810)
- **What happened (fact):** Father Miguel Hidalgo issued the *Grito de Dolores* in the Bajío region, calling for an end to Spanish rule, social equality, and—in its radical form—racial justice. Within weeks, tens of thousands of indigenous and casta followers had joined an insurgency that would fracture New Spain for a decade.
- **The narrative *then*:** Barely registered in the United States. A few newspapers carried delayed reports; Jeffersonian expansionists noted vaguely that Spanish weakness might ease acquisition of Florida or Texas. The racial and social character of Hidalgo's rising—its challenge to *hacienda* and caste order—was invisible to American observers who saw only another Creole revolt against European tyranny, potentially useful, potentially alarming.
- **The narrative *now:** The foundational moment of Mexican national identity, celebrated annually, increasingly recognized in U.S. historiography as shaping the southwestern borderlands that would become American territory. The *Grito* is now read as complicating Manifest Destiny: the independence that enabled eventual U.S. expansion was also a social revolution that made Mexican northern territories politically unstable and culturally resistant.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The blind spot is the story. American contemporaries could not see Hidalgo's revolution because their narrative of hemispheric republicanism required Creole leadership; what they missed was the depth of social insurgency that would make Mexican nationhood durable and its loss to the United States traumatic and incomplete.

#### W-3 — Marriage of Napoleon and Marie Louise (April 2, 1810)
- **What happened (fact):** Napoleon married the 18-year-old Austrian archduchex by proxy following his divorce from Joséphine, cementing the Franco-Austrian alliance of 1809 and producing the hoped-for imperial heir (Napoleon II, born 1811).
- **The narrative *then*:** In American diplomatic circles, puzzlement and missed opportunity. The marriage signaled Napoleon's continental preoccupation, his abandonment of maritime ambitions, his acceptance of a European—not global—empire. U.S. ministers in Paris and London failed to leverage this shift; the narrative of French revolutionary danger persisted even as its object transformed.
- **The narrative *now*:** A dynastic footnote to Napoleonic history, occasionally cited to explain why France would not contest British maritime supremacy after Tilsit. In American historiography, it is largely absent, because the War of 1812 narrative requires France as co-belligerent Britain's mirror-image villain; acknowledging Napoleon's actual strategic indifference to the Atlantic would complicate the republic's victimhood.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The gap between European reality and American perception. The marriage was legible to contemporaries as Austrian humiliation and Napoleonic megalomania; its strategic meaning—France's withdrawal from global naval competition—was not legible at all, and that misreading is itself historical.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected to expose the structural pincers closing around the United States: domestically, the collision between demographic expansion (`Census`) and indigenous resistance (`Tippecanoe` prep), and internationally, the desperate gambit of `Macon's Bill No. 2` attempting to navigate an impossible European landscape defined by total war (`French Annexation`) and colonial collapse (`Mexican Independence`). I deliberately omitted the specific details of the "Patriot War" in Florida to avoid redundancy with the carried-forward `imperial paradox` thread, focusing instead on the Mexican uprising which fundamentally altered the geopolitical context of North American empire more profoundly than the localized Florida skirmishes. Similarly, while the War of 1812 is the looming shadow, selecting the legislative mechanism (`Macon's Bill`) rather than the war itself highlights the failure of policy instruments before the kinetic release of violence.

## 2. Connections Within the Year

- **U.S. ↔ World:** The diplomatic paralysis of Macon's Bill No. 2 (US-1) is not merely a failure of American statecraft but a direct structural echo of the French annexation of Holland (W-1); both events reveal a global system where "neutrality" has become impossible because the belligerents (Britain and France) have shifted from contesting territory to consuming the entire economic sphere, forcing the U.S. into a reactive posture where its only leverage is the threat of self-starvation.
- **Event ↔ Event:** The demographic surge recorded in the Census of 1810 (US-2), specifically the westward push and the entrenchment of slavery, provides the material fuel for the military escalation at Tippecanoe (US-3); the land hunger quantified by the census creates the inevitable collision course with Tecumseh's confederacy, while the instability of the Mexican War of Independence (W-2) acts as a pressure valve, diverting Spanish attention and implicitly encouraging American expansionism by weakening the southern flank of the continent.
- **Invisible / structural (shared hidden drivers):** A singular driver of "totalizing economic warfare" underpins US-1, W-1, and the context of W-3; Napoleon's marriage to Marie Louise signals a pivot to continental consolidation to enforce the blockade, which in turn tightens the noose on American shipping, demonstrating that the Atlantic economy had reorganized around a binary choice: submit to one imperial monopoly or face economic strangulation.

## 3. The Debate

### Round 1 — Opening Interpretations (uninterrupted)

#### 🜂 Herald
The year 1810 demands that we inhabit a republic drunk on its own numbers and wounded by their meaning. The census takers fanned across twenty states and territories, counting 7.2 million souls, and what they returned was a story the country was not yet prepared to read: the West exploding with free white settlement, the South calcifying around human property rendered into constitutional power by the three-fifths arithmetic. This was Jefferson's empire for liberty quantified, and the quantity revealed the contradiction that the narrative of expansion would spend decades suppressing—that the same momentum carrying yeomen toward the Wabash and the Mississippi carried slavery toward cotton's kingdom, that the census was a political instrument forged in 1787 now measuring the distance between republican promise and sectional fate.

The republic told itself it was a commercial peacemaker among warrior monarchies, and Macon's Bill No. 2 was the perfect expression of that faith: we shall trade with whoever respects our neutrality, let commerce be our sword and shield. The narrative then was one of sovereign flexibility, the young nation wielding its economic weight with republican ingenuity. Napoleon understood before Madison did that this was not leverage but vulnerability. The Cadore Letter arrived in August 1810, a mirage of compliance that Republicans clutched because their narrative required a French interlocutor more honorable than British impressers. The annexation of Holland in July should have shattered that requirement—Napoleon was dismantling the very neutral commerce Americans claimed to protect—yet the narrative persisted because the alternative was acknowledging that the republic's commercial diplomacy had no purchase on European total war. The marriage to Marie Louise signaled what American diplomats could not read: Napoleon's strategic turn toward continental consolidation, his effective abandonment of global maritime rivalry. The United States was not, as its self-story claimed, caught between two predatory sea powers; it was caught between Britain's actual maritime supremacy and France's indifference to it, dressed up in diplomatic theater.

On the Wabash frontier, Governor Harrison spent 1810 constructing the narrative that would make him president. His council with Tecumseh at Grouseland in September, the mobilization at Vincennes, the steady characterization of Prophetstown as British aggression in embryo—these were not preparations for diplomacy but the architecture of preemptive war. The frontier narrative then was of vigilant defense against savage resurgence; Tecumseh's confederacy was read through the remembered trauma of 1790s border warfare, his political genius reduced to British manipulation. What Harrison built in 1810 was the story that Tippecanoe in 1811 would confirm: the general who struck first against gathering darkness. We inherit that story as campaign slogan, as national myth of frontier security, rarely noticing that the battle's prehistory was itself the decision for war, that 1810 was the year the republic chose military solution over coexistence with indigenous political organization.

Southward, the *Grito de Dolores* erupted in September, and the republic's narrative blindness was itself a kind of historical force. Hidalgo's rising was not visible to American observers because it did not fit the available story: not a Creole elite seizing independence for property, but a priest leading indigenous and mixed-race masses against social order itself. Expansionists noted Spanish weakness opportunistically; they could not see the revolution's depth, and therefore could not anticipate that Mexican independence would be durable, that its loss to the United States would be traumatic, that the borderlands would carry Hidalgo's social challenge into the American future. The narrative then of hemispheric republicanism required white Creole leadership; what it excluded was the multiracial insurgency that made Mexican nationhood something other than a temporary vacancy awaiting American filling.

What 1810 reveals, through the Herald's lens, is a republic at the height of its narrative self-confidence and the cusp of its narrative collapse. The stories Americans told themselves—commercial peacemaker, expanding yeoman republic, frontier defender, hemispheric liberator—were not mere ideology but active forces shaping choices that would constrain choices for generations. Macon's Bill was believed, and belief made war probable; Harrison's preemptive narrative was believed, and belief made indigenous coexistence impossible; the census numbers were selectively believed, and belief made the slavery compromise's arithmetic invisible until it became irreconcilable. We inherit 1810 not as prelude but as template: the year when the United States demonstrated how powerfully a democracy can be moved by stories it tells itself, and how costly the gap between those stories and the world's unread realities.

#### 🜄 Heretic
The official story of 1810, which the Herald has begun to reconstruct with admirable sympathy for the republic’s self-understanding, goes something like this: a young nation, democratic and commercial, sought to navigate the maelstrom of Napoleonic warfare through the peaceful instruments of law and trade. Macon’s Bill No. 2 was a sincere, if naive, attempt to leverage American neutrality into European respect. The census was a triumph of republican enumeration, a transparent accounting of the people’s growth and westward vitality. The gathering storm on the Wabash was a tragic clash of civilizations, with honorable men on both sides caught in forces beyond their control. And in Europe, Napoleon’s machinations—his marriage, his annexations—were the opaque maneuvers of a despot that a virtuous republic could hardly be expected to decipher in real time. This is the story the architects of 1810 told themselves, and it is the story their heirs have largely preserved.

Now let us take it apart, because every element of that story served someone, and the silences are deafening.

Begin with the census, the year’s most clinical instrument of power. The Herald notes that it quantified a “sectional arithmetic.” That is too bloodless. The Census of 1810 was not a neutral photograph of the republic; it was a weapon forged in the constitutional settlement of 1787, now being sharpened. The three-fifths clause did not merely count enslaved people—it converted their stolen bodies into political power for the very men who owned them. Every decade, the census reapportioned the House of Representatives, and in 1810, the returns revealed that the South’s enslaved population had surged past one million, concentrated in Virginia and the new cotton frontiers of South Carolina and Georgia. This was not an organic demographic fact. It was the direct consequence of the 1808 ban on the *international* slave trade, which—and here is the omission that should sicken us—had transformed the domestic slave trade into an industrial-scale forced migration. The constitutional compromise that permitted the ban was not a victory for humanity; it was a cartel agreement. Virginia’s exhausted tobacco planters, sitting on a surplus of enslaved labor, became the breeders and suppliers for the cotton frontier. The census of 1810, by counting those people as three-fifths of a person for representation, handed Virginia and the Deep South a bloc of congressional seats that would, within a decade, be used to crush any federal restriction on slavery’s expansion. The “sectional arithmetic” was not a warning the nation failed to read; it was a payoff the slave power had designed and now cashed. Cui bono? The planter class of the South, who gained both a captive labor force and the political apparatus to protect it, all while the census record laundered their power into the neutral language of population tables. Whose voice is absent? The million human beings enumerated as fractions, whose internal migrations were not the pioneer spirit celebrated in the West but a terrorized, coerced dispersal documented only in the silence of the ledger.

Now turn to Macon’s Bill No. 2, which the Herald frames as a sincere expression of republican commercial faith. Sincere for whom? The bill was a legislative surrender dressed as a diplomatic gambit. The Non-Intercourse Act it replaced had been an attempt to wield economic coercion against both Britain and France. It failed, spectacularly, because American merchants preferred profit to patriotism and because the federal government lacked the naval power to enforce its own laws. Macon’s Bill was not a new strategy; it was an abdication dressed in the language of flexibility. It announced to the world that the United States would trade with whichever belligerent pretended to respect its neutrality first. Napoleon, a man who had just finished annexing the Kingdom of Holland in July to tighten his stranglehold on European commerce, understood immediately that this was an invitation to a confidence game. The Cadore Letter of August 1810—the French foreign minister’s vague promise to revoke the Berlin and Milan decrees—was a lie, and it was a lie that cost Napoleon nothing. He had no intention of opening Europe to American shipping; he was consolidating a continental autarky. The marriage to Marie Louise in April was the clearest signal: Napoleon was turning east, toward Austria and Russia, toward land empire, toward a Europe organized around French production and French consumption. American ships were irrelevant to this project. The tragedy of Macon’s Bill is not that it was naive; it is that it was a policy designed by and for a coastal commercial elite who had no answer to the structural reality that their prosperity depended on access to markets controlled by empires that did not need them. Madison’s administration clung to the Cadore fiction because the alternative was admitting that the entire edifice of Republican foreign policy—the embargo, non-intercourse, the faith in commerce as a weapon—was bankrupt. The gap between rhetoric and action is the bill itself: a law that purported to defend American sovereignty by offering it up to whichever predator moved first.

This brings us to the frontier, where the silences become a roar. The Herald recounts Harrison’s mobilization at Vincennes, his council with Tecumseh, his characterization of Prophetstown as a British proxy. What is missing is the land. Every skirmish, every treaty, every escalation in the Indiana Territory throughout 1810 was downstream of the hunger for Indian land, a hunger that was not a tragic byproduct of westward expansion but its primary engine. Harrison was not a soldier caught in a clash of civilizations; he was a speculator and a politician whose career depended on acquiring title to Native territory as cheaply and quickly as possible. The Treaty of Fort Wayne in 1809—the proximate cause of Tecumseh’s resistance—was a masterpiece of fraudulent acquisition, negotiated with individual chiefs who had no authority to cede the communal lands of the Shawnee, Delaware, and others. In 1810, Harrison’s military buildup was not a response to British intrigue; it was a campaign to break the multi-tribal confederacy that had finally organized to say no. The British arms that Harrison and his allies invoked so loudly were a convenient propaganda device, one that served a triple purpose: it delegitimized Native resistance as foreign manipulation rather than self-defense, it provided a casus belli for the war hawks already circling Madison’s cabinet, and it absolved the settlers and speculators of the moral weight of dispossession. The voice absent from the record is Tecumseh’s, not because he did not speak—his speeches were transcribed, his arguments were clear—but because the American narrative could only hear him as a dupe of British agents or a noble savage doomed by history. The comfortable version lets us avoid feeling responsible for the fact that the United States, in 1810, was already an empire, and its imperial logic required the destruction of any indigenous polity that refused to sell its land and vanish.

Finally, the events beyond the border. The Mexican War of Independence, ignited by Hidalgo’s *Grito de Dolores* in September, appears in our six events as a complication for U.S. ambitions in Florida and Texas. That framing is itself an artifact of power. The revolution that tore through Guanajuato was a massive, multi-ethnic social uprising—indigenous peasants and mestizo laborers rising against a creole elite and a colonial state that had dispossessed them for three centuries. To reduce it to a “complication” for American territorial ambitions is to view the entire hemisphere through the lens of Washington’s eventual desires. But the deeper point is this: the instability on the southern flank was not an accident that befell American policymakers. It was an opportunity they had been eyeing since Jefferson’s presidency, and the narrative of 1810—the one that would harden into the Monroe Doctrine and Manifest Destiny—was already being laid down. The United States could not yet seize Florida or Texas, but it could begin to construct the story that Spanish rule was illegitimate, that the revolutionary chaos proved the incapacity of non-Anglo peoples for self-government, and that American expansion was a force for order, not conquest. This narrative served the slaveholding South, which saw in Florida and Texas new cotton lands and new Senate seats. It served the commercial North, which saw new ports and trade routes. And it served the republican ideology, which could frame territorial aggrandizement as the spread of liberty rather than the expansion of a slave empire. The distance between what was professed—sympathy for republican revolution, respect for self-determination—and what was done—opportunistic land-grabbing justified by racial and civilizational hierarchy—is the central fact of American foreign policy in 1810, and its inheritance is with us still.

#### 🜃 Cartographer
The year 1810 presents a map where the surface noise of diplomatic bluffs and border skirmishes obscures a terrifying geological shift in the substrate of power: the closure of the neutral space. For the first time since the Revolution, the United States finds itself not merely navigating between empires, but suffocating within a closed system of their making. The structural variable here is not the competence of James Madison or the cunning of Napoleon, but the maturation of the Continental System and the British Orders in Council into a totalizing economic architecture. When Congress passes Macon's Bill No. 2, it is not an act of naive optimism but a confession of structural impotence; the U.S. economy had become so deeply integrated into the Atlantic trade web that its only remaining weapon was the ability to choose which empire would starve it slightly less slowly. This is confirmed by W-1, the annexation of Holland. By absorbing a former ally to plug a leak in his blockade, Napoleon demonstrates that in this new order, sovereignty is conditional on economic utility. The "neutral rights" the U.S. claims are revealed as a fiction; there is no outside to the system anymore.

Beneath this diplomatic suffocation, a slower, heavier variable is cracking the continent open: the demographic momentum captured in the Census of 1810. The raw count of 7.2 million is less significant than its distribution. The surge in the West and the deepening concentration of enslaved labor in the South create a "sectional arithmetic" that demands new land to sustain its logic. This is the invisible engine driving US-3. The mobilization at Vincennes is not simply a reaction to Tecumseh's spiritual nationalism; it is the physical manifestation of a demographic wave that cannot be stopped by diplomacy. The census data quantifies the inevitability of the coming violence; the land is required for the population, and the population requires the land, rendering the indigenous confederacy's attempt to halt expansion structurally doomed regardless of tactical brilliance. The connection between US-2 and US-3 is causal and immediate: the numbers on the page in August become the muskets in the field by November.

Simultaneously, the periphery of the map is burning, altering the strategic calculus of the center. The Grito de Dolores (W-2) initiates a fracture in the Spanish Empire that will take a decade to heal, but its immediate effect in 1810 is to remove a stabilizing barrier to American ambition. As Mexico convulses, the security umbrella over Florida and Texas thins, inviting the very expansionist pressures building in the West. Yet, there is a missed opportunity in the reading of European signals. The marriage of Napoleon and Marie Louise (W-3) was a signal of a shift from revolutionary dynamism to dynastic consolidation—a move toward stabilizing the European core rather than projecting power globally. Had American diplomats read this structural shift correctly, they might have realized that France's appetite for trans-Atlantic conflict was waning even as its rhetorical commitment to the blockade remained high. Instead, the U.S. remains trapped in the previous decade's paradigm, fighting a war against British naval dominance while misreading the French intent, a cognitive lag that will prove costly.

The through-line from 1810 to the present United States is the establishment of a precedent where American sovereignty is asserted through the weaponization of economic interdependence and the relentless conversion of demographic pressure into territorial acquisition. The dilemma of 1810—how to maintain autonomy in a globalized system dominated by hostile hegemons—is the ancestral ghost of every trade war and sanctions regime enacted in Washington today. Furthermore, the census-driven push westward institutionalized a pattern where domestic social contradictions (specifically the tension between free and slave labor) are "solved" not by internal reform, but by external expansion, kicking the can of sectional conflict down the geographic road until the road runs out. In 1810, the map showed room to run; the forces set in motion here ensured that when that room eventually vanished, the resulting explosion would redefine the nation.

`imperial_squeeze` | The condition of being trapped between two totalizing economic blocs, forcing a mid-sized power to weaponize its own market access as its primary diplomatic lever. This thread moves from the Non-Intercourse Acts directly to modern sanctions regimes and trade wars.
`demographic_land_lock` | The feedback loop wherein population growth and specific modes of production (plantation slavery) generate an insatiable demand for new territory, making diplomatic coexistence with existing inhabitants structurally impossible.
