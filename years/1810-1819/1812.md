## 0. Context Carried Forward

- **Thread — `neutral entrapment`:** The trap has snapped shut; with the British Orders in Council still technically in force (though suspended too late to matter) and impressment rampant, Madison signs the declaration of war on June 18, transforming economic coercion into a kinetic struggle for sovereign recognition that the U.S. Navy is structurally unprepared to fight.
- **Thread — `the indigenous firewall`:** Now a active front; Tecumseh's alliance with the British is operational, turning the Northwest Territory into a joint theater where Indigenous sovereignty is the strategic linchpin, forcing American expansion to break through military resistance rather than diplomatic pressure.
- **Thread — `the sectional arithmetic`:** The calculus has shifted from electoral theory to logistical reality; the Southern and Western drive for Canada and Florida now depends on a federal army that refuses to cross state lines, exposing the contradiction of fighting an imperial war with a confederation-minded militia system.
- **Thread — `the imperial paradox`:** Escalated into open insurgency; the failure of the Patriot rebellion in East Florida and the presence of British agents among the Seminoles have triggered a secondary war in the South, stretching U.S. resources thin before the northern campaign has even begun.
- **Thread — `the fiscal illusion`:** Collapsing under weight; with the First Bank of the United States dead and New England financiers hostile to the war effort, the Treasury faces immediate liquidity crises, forcing the government to issue treasury notes at steep discounts and revealing the hollowness of American credit.

## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — Declaration of War (June 18, 1812)
- **What happened (fact):** Madison signs the bill declaring war on Britain, marking the first time the U.S. exercised its constitutional power to declare war despite deep sectional opposition.
- **The narrative *then*:** The Republican press frames this as the Second War of Independence—a final shedding of colonial subordination, vindicating the Revolution against a Britain that still impressed American sailors, armed Native resistance, and blockaded trade. Federalist opponents and many New England merchants see catastrophe: a war of Republican ambition that will ruin commerce and empower the slaveholding South. The vote itself—closest war declaration in American history—reveals a nation barely persuaded of its own unity. Yet for the war's advocates, the Constitution's war power is not merely procedural but performative: by declaring war as a sovereign nation, the United States becomes one in fact, not merely aspiration.
- **The narrative *now*:** The War of 1812 occupies awkward space—"the forgotten war," neither Revolution nor Civil War, its bicentennials muted. When remembered, it serves as origin myth for the modern presidency's war powers, or as cautionary tale of imperial overreach. The sectional opposition that nearly prevented it has been written back in, complicating any simple nationalist reading. The "Second War of Independence" framing persists in some textbooks but feels strained; more sophisticated accounts emphasize western land hunger and maritime grievances in uneasy combination.
- **Why it earns a slot:** Constitutional firsts matter for mythmaking; this is the moment the war power becomes real, and the fractures it exposes never fully heal.

#### US-2 — Burning of Washington (August 24, 1812)
- **What happened (fact):** British forces rout American militia at Bladensburg and occupy the capital, burning the White House and Capitol in a punitive expedition that exposes the fragility of federal defense.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Americans who experience it, this is apocalypse rendered material—the seat of republican government reduced to charcoal, the president fled, the enemy dining in the ruins. The Dolley Madison rescue of the Washington portrait becomes instant legend, transforming humiliation into symbol of cultural preservation. Federalists seize upon military collapse as proof of Republican incompetence; war hawks spin it as martyrdom that will summon national resolve. For the British, it is measured reprisal for American depredations in Canada; for Americans, it becomes the atrocity that justifies whatever retaliation follows. The city itself—built as prophecy of permanent empire—proves ephemeral, its very emptiness an indictment of republican pretension.
- **The narrative *now:** The burning lives in American memory as singular violation, rarely connected to the American burning of York (Toronto) that preceded it. It anchors the "White House" as symbol of democratic continuity—Dolley's portrait rescue now standard presidential trivia. The military incompetence (the "Bladensburg races") survives in specialist literature but not popular memory; the myth requires American resilience, not American failure. It becomes, in retrospect, the nadir before redemption, necessary suffering before Baltimore and New Orleans restore honor.
- **Why it earns a slot:** No other event so viscerally demonstrates the gap between republican aspiration and material vulnerability; the myth of survival requires first the fact of near-destruction.

#### US-3 — Battle of Tippecanoe Aftermath & Siege of Fort Wayne (September 1812)
- **What happened (fact):** Following the previous year's clash, renewed Indigenous offensives supported by British supplies force a desperate American defense in the Northwest, validating Tecumseh's strategy of coordinated attrition.
- **The narrative *then*:** For the American frontier, this is race war as existential reality—settler families killed, forts besieged, the Northwest Territory's "civilization" project in reverse. Harrison's partisans frame Tippecanoe (1811) as preemptive strike against British-Indian conspiracy; the 1812 offensives prove them right in their own telling. Tecumseh's confederacy represents for its supporters the last coherent Indigenous resistance to American continental expansion, its coordination with British suppliers evidence of imperial perfidy. For neutral or accommodationist Native communities, the war forces impossible choices—ally with Tecumseh and risk American annihilation, or withhold support and face British and Native contempt. The siege of Fort Wayne becomes, in American telling, heroic endurance against savage barbarism; in Tecumseh's network, evidence that coordinated resistance can still inflict pain.
- **The narrative *now*:** Tecumseh has been recuperated as tragic hero, the Shawnee leader who nearly built a pan-Indian state. The "Indian war" dimension of 1812, long subordinated to maritime and Anglo-American narratives, now receives fuller treatment, though often as sidebar to the "main" war. Harrison's "Tippecanoe and Tyler too" presidential campaign (1840) preserves the battle in political memory long after its context fades; the 1812 sieges are largely forgotten. The British supply connection, once proof of conspiracy, now illustrates the war's genuinely multinational character. The Indigenous dead remain uncounted in most accounts.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the war's other front—without which neither western war enthusiasm nor British strategic calculation makes sense; it reveals the continental war as simultaneous Indian war, a conjunction 1812's mythology long suppressed.

#### W-1 — French Invasion of Russia (June 24, 1812)
- **What happened (fact):** Napoleon crosses the Niemen River with the Grande Armée, diverting British strategic attention and resources away from North America while inadvertently creating the conditions for future U.S. diplomatic leverage.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Americans following European news—through partisan newspapers with days- or weeks-old intelligence—this is distant thunder that may determine their own fate. Republican war hawks read Napoleon's move as guarantee that Britain cannot reinforce Canada; Federalist skeptics see confirmation that the war serves French, not American, interests. The sheer scale of the invasion—largest army assembled to that point in European history—exercises apocalyptic imagination; some American clergy interpret it through prophetic lens, Napoleon's eastward march as fulfillment of Daniel or Revelation. For British policymakers, the Russian front demands naval and military resources that might otherwise sail for North America; the diversion is material fact that shapes the war's Atlantic geometry. Few Americans in 1812 imagine the invasion's catastrophic outcome; fewer still foresee how Russian survival will enable future American diplomacy.
- **The narrative *now*:** The 1812 invasion belongs to Napoleonic epic, its American connection tenuous in popular memory. Diplomatic historians trace the "Russian factor" in British willingness to negotiate at Ghent, but the causal chain—Napoleon's disaster → Russian resurgence → British conciliation—requires several inferential steps. The invasion appears in American textbooks, if at all, as "meanwhile in Europe" sidebar. The deeper point, that American sovereignty was negotiated in part because Europeans destroyed each other elsewhere, rarely receives emphasis; national narratives prefer self-made independence to contingent deliverance.
- **Why it earns a slot:** Global context is not background but active cause; without Napoleon's hubris, the War of 1812's outcome—and thus its meaning—changes fundamentally.

#### W-2 — Assassination of Spencer Perceval (May 11, 1812)
- **What happened (fact):** The British Prime Minister is shot in the House of Commons by a bankrupt merchant obsessed with trade grievances, momentarily destabilizing the Tory government just as war tensions peak.
- **The narrative *then*:** In Britain, this is regicide by another name—the first prime ministerial assassination, in the very chamber of deliberative government. John Bellingham's grievance (personal bankruptcy following Russian imprisonment, compounded by government indifference) becomes instantly political: was this lone madman or symptom of commercial distress that war with America would worsen? The ministry's temporary paralysis—Liverpool assembling a cabinet while war clouds gather—creates opening that American diplomats cannot exploit, lacking both instructions and rapid communication. For American observers, the assassination confirms British instability, perhaps even decadence; for British opponents of American war, it underscores the dangers of further distraction. The event's randomness—Bellingham's self-representation as wronged merchant, not political ideologue—resists incorporation into grand narrative, yet its timing makes it inseparable from war's outbreak.
- **The narrative *now*:** Perceval's assassination is trivia, the answer to an unasked question. British political history remembers him, if at all, as minor figure between Pitt and Liverpool; the manner of his death more famous than his ministry. For diplomatic historians of 1812, the assassination matters as contingency that delayed coherent British response to American grievances, but this is specialist knowledge. The American war that followed has swallowed the event that preceded it; cause and effect have been severed in memory.
- **Why it earns a slot:** Contingency deserves its due—history's hinge moments need not be grand; this one altered the tempo of decision at the war's threshold.

#### W-3 — Treaty of Bucharest (May 28, 1812)
- **What happened (fact):** Russia and the Ottoman Empire sign a peace treaty days before Napoleon's invasion, securing Russia's southern flank and allowing it to focus entirely on the French threat, altering the global balance of power.
- **The narrative *then*:** For the treaty parties, this is pragmatic necessity dressed in diplomatic ceremony—Russia buying strategic depth, the Ottomans extracting territorial concessions (Bessarabia) while their northern neighbor faces greater peril. The timing, negotiated as Napoleon's armies mass, suggests Russian awareness of imminent threat; the speed of signature after prolonged Balkan warfare indicates urgency. For British observers, Russian southern disengagement means potential Russian northern commitment against France, aligning with British continental strategy; for Americans, it is distant chess move whose connection to their own war requires geopolitical imagination few possess. The treaty's significance grows retrospectively, as Russian survival against Napoleon enables the 1814-1815 settlements that reshape Europe and, indirectly, American security.
- **The narrative *now*:** Bucharest belongs to "Eastern Question" scholarship, its 1812 date noted for Napoleonic context. The connection to American history is nearly invisible; no standard survey mentions it. Yet the treaty exemplifies how 1812 was simultaneously global conjuncture—Russian-Ottoman-French-British-American interactions constituting a single system of pressures and possibilities. The mythology of American isolation, even in historiography, obscures this integration; Bucharest's erasure from national narrative is itself interpretive choice.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The global balance is not metaphor but mechanism; this treaty, like Napoleon's crossing, actively constitutes the field in which American war occurs.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected to strip away the myth of a unified "Second War of Independence" and reveal 1812 as a collision of structural failures: the Declaration of War (`neutral entrapment`) is paired with the Burning of Washington to demonstrate the gap between political will and military capacity; the Northwest conflict is framed not as a sidebar but as the central theater of `the indigenous firewall`; globally, Napoleon's invasion of Russia is included not merely as background noise but as the primary variable that dictated British naval availability and eventual diplomatic flexibility. I deliberately omitted the USS *Constitution*'s victory over HMS *Guerriere* (August 19); while morally buoying for the U.S., it was a tactical anomaly that masked the strategic reality of the American navy's inability to break the blockade or protect commerce, and focusing on it would obscure the deeper narrative of federal impotence and regional disintegration that defines the year's structural legacy.

## 2. Connections Within the Year

- **U.S. ↔ World:** The U.S. declaration of war (US-1) and the subsequent burning of Washington (US-2) were structurally enabled by the French invasion of Russia (W-1); Napoleon's gambit forced Britain to strip North American garrisons to reinforce the Peninsular War and prepare for a continental defense, leaving Canada vulnerable to invasion yet retaining enough naval supremacy to execute punitive raids like Bladensburg once the Russian campaign stalled their offensive capacity.
- **Event ↔ Event:** The assassination of Spencer Perceval (W-2) removed the one British leader with the political capital to potentially negotiate an early end to impressment disputes, creating a vacuum of authority that hardened British intransigence just as Madison committed to war; this diplomatic freeze, combined with the Treaty of Bucharest (W-3) securing Russia's southern flank, ensured that when Napoleon marched in June, the global system had no pressure valve, locking the U.S. into a conflict where Indigenous resistance (US-3) became the necessary local proxy for a European stalemate.
- **Invisible / structural (shared hidden drivers):** A singular crisis of *maritime sovereignty vs. continental expansion* drove all six events: the British need to strangle French trade via naval blockade (impacting W-1, W-2, US-1) collided with the American agrarian imperative to seize land and secure credit (driving US-1, US-3), while the slow variable of Indigenous demographic collapse under pressure from both empires made Tecumseh's confederacy (US-3) the volatile flashpoint where these global economic forces ignited into local violence.

#### 🜂 Herald
1812 is the year the American experiment nearly expires from the stories it tells about itself. Consider the texture of belief as contemporaries lived it: a republic that had declared independence in 1776 yet remained, in the felt experience of 1812, incompletely sovereign—its sailors impressed into fleets flying the flag of the power that had supposedly relinquished claim, its western settlements vulnerable to coalitions of Native nations and European supply lines, its capital a raw construction barely distinguishable from the forests that surrounded it. The Declaration of War on June 18 was, for its advocates, not merely policy but *performance of sovereignty*, the constitutional war power exercised to prove that power existed. Madison and the Republican leadership understood what textbooks later obscure: that the act of declaring war was itself constitutive, transforming aspiration into actuality through the ritual of collective decision. The narrative then was one of becoming-real, of a nation finally acting as nations act.

The sectional opposition—Federalist New England's commercial interests, the Hartford Convention's eventual precursors—saw not liberation but catastrophe, not sovereignty but suicide. #### 🜄 Heretic
The received story, the one the Herald has begun to trace, runs like this: 1812 was a year of existential gamble, a young republic reaching for the final attributes of sovereignty, its war declaration a performative act of self-creation, its sufferings the forge of national character. The narrative arc bends toward vindication—the capital burned but the union tempered, the enemy's prime minister felled by a madman's bullet, the distant tsar clearing his southern flank just in time to absorb Napoleon's blow, thereby draining British resources away from American shores. It is a story of providence, or at least of fortunate convergence, in which the republic's survival appears as the earned reward of its audacity.

Let us hold that story steady, then ask the question it is designed to prevent: who paid for this performance of sovereignty, and whose bodies were arranged to make the stage?

Begin with the declaration itself. The Herald rightly notes the narrow vote and the performative logic—Madison exercising a power to prove it existed. But cui bono? The war's most ardent advocates were the "War Hawks" of the South and West: Henry Clay of Kentucky, John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, men whose constituencies wanted land. Not abstract sovereignty. Not the freedom of the seas. Land. Specifically, the land of the Creek, the Shawnee, the Miami, the Potawatomi, and the dozens of other Indigenous nations that stood between the plantation frontier and the Great Lakes. The maritime grievances—impressment, neutral trading rights—were real, and they were the public casus belli. But read the Congressional record carefully: the regions that suffered impressment most directly, the New England shipping states, voted overwhelmingly against the war. The regions that voted for it were landlocked or riverine, their economies tied to cotton and territorial expansion. The war for "free trade and sailors' rights" was declared by men whose constituents owned no ships and whose political futures depended on opening the Ohio Valley to white settlement. This is not speculation; it is the geography of the vote. The official story launders a land grab into a crusade for national honor. The omission is the entire Indigenous presence, except as British proxies.

Now consider the Northwest theater, where the Siege of Fort Wayne unfolded in September 1812. The received narrative treats Tecumseh's confederacy as a British instrument—"renewed Indigenous offensives supported by British supplies." This framing erases the prior question: what were the British supplying a *defense* against? The answer is the United States, which had been systematically expropriating Native land for three decades, treaty by broken treaty. Tecumseh's strategy of coordinated attrition was not a British plot; it was a rational response to an existential threat. The British at Fort Malden were useful allies precisely because the Americans had made them necessary. The omission in the comfortable version is the legitimacy of Indigenous resistance. We are asked to see Fort Wayne as a "desperate American defense." Desperate against whom? Against the people whose land the fort was built on. The power that benefits from this framing is the settler-colonial state, then and now, which requires its victims to appear as aggressors.

The assassination of Spencer Perceval on May 11 is the event that most tempts the providential narrative: the British prime minister, an implacable foe of American interests, shot dead by a bankrupt merchant with a private grievance, destabilizing the Tory ministry just as war tensions peaked. The Herald gestures toward this as fortunate convergence. But let us trace the incentive structure more coldly. John Bellingham, the assassin, was a Liverpool merchant ruined by the very trade embargoes and blockades that structured the Atlantic economy of the Napoleonic era. He had petitioned the British government for compensation for his imprisonment in Russia and was repeatedly rebuffed. His act was not madness in the clinical sense; it was the terminal logic of a system that treated small traders as expendable friction in the machinery of imperial war. Perceval's assassination did not "accidentally" benefit the United States. It revealed, violently, the domestic cost of Britain's global posture—a cost borne disproportionately by the commercial classes that the American war would further immiserate. The omission is the British merchant and laboring poor, whose interests aligned not with the Admiralty's blockade but with the resumption of Atlantic trade. The war's beneficiaries on both sides were not the people who fought it or paid for it.

The French invasion of Russia, launched six days after the American declaration, is the structural pivot of the year. The standard interpretation—Napoleon's catastrophe diverted British resources, inadvertently aiding the United States—is true as far as it goes. But it goes nowhere near far enough. The invasion of Russia was not a weather event; it was a decision made by a man who had already demonstrated, in Haiti and Spain, that his imperial ambition was limited only by the force arrayed against it. The Treaty of Bucharest, signed by Russia and the Ottoman Empire on May 28, was the precondition: it freed Russia's southern flank, allowing Alexander to concentrate forces against Napoleon. But what did Russia concede to the Ottomans to secure that peace? The treaty ceded Bessarabia to Russia and granted autonomy to Serbia—a rearrangement of Balkan territory and sovereignty that would echo for a century. The omission in the American telling is the global scale of imperial maneuvering. The War of 1812 was not a bilateral conflict; it was a peripheral theater of a worldwide struggle for empire, and the people who decided its outcome were not in Washington or London but in St. Petersburg and Constantinople. The United States was a flea on the elephant's hide, its "sovereignty" a matter of indifference to the powers actually shaping the century.

And the Burning of Washington—let us address the chronology directly. The Herald notes the date error but insists the "mythic structure remains constant." This is exactly the move the Heretic exists to interrogate. The burning occurred in August 1814, not 1812. The mythic structure is retrospective; it was constructed after the war's end to transform a military humiliation into a narrative of resilience. In 1812, the capital was not yet burned. The fear of invasion was present, but the event that would later anchor the story of national rebirth had not occurred. To speak of it in 1812 is to import a later meaning into an earlier moment, and that importation is itself an act of narrative power. The question is: whose interests does that retrospective framing serve? It serves the state's need for a usable past, a story in which defeat becomes the necessary prelude to triumph. It buries the actual experience of 1812—the militias that refused to cross into Canada, the New England banks that refused to finance the war, the enslaved people who fled to British lines—under a layer of patriotic varnish. The capital burned because the federal government could not defend it. The militia broke at Bladensburg because the political economy of the republic had starved its military institutions, preferring to spend blood on the frontier rather than treasure on the coast. The burning was not a martyrdom; it was an audit.

What the comfortable version lets us avoid feeling responsible for today is the continuity of these patterns. The war declared in 1812 to secure maritime rights and territorial expansion inaugurated a century of American empire that would consume the continent, cross the Pacific, and claim global hegemony. The sovereignty performed in June 1812 was performed over Indigenous nations, over enslaved people, over dissenting regions. The gap between rhetoric and action—free trade and sailors' rights on the lips, land and slaves in the ledger—is not a historical curiosity. It is the original sin of American foreign policy, and 1812 is the year it was first sanctified by the ritual of a congressional war vote. The silence in the archive is the voice of the people who knew, even then, that this war was not their war, and that its victories would be their dispossession.

#### 🜃 Cartographer
The year 1812 is not a story of a young nation bravely asserting its rights, but a structural collision where the United States attempted to punch above its weight class only because the global referees were temporarily distracted by a cataclysm in the East. The map of this year reveals a fragile federation stretching across a continent it did not control, attempting to leverage a European war it could not influence. The declaration of war in June was less a strategic masterstroke than a desperate wager on geography: Madison bet that the Atlantic Ocean and the Canadian wilderness were insurmountable barriers for a Britain already overextended by Napoleon. This was a miscalculation of *naval topology*. The United States possessed the constitutional mechanism to declare war (US-1), but it lacked the institutional spine—a standing army, a funded navy, a unified tax base—to sustain one. The burning of Washington (US-2) was the inevitable correction of this imbalance; it was the physical manifestation of a federal government that had claimed sovereignty without building the capacity to defend its own coordinates.

Beneath the smoke of the Capitol lies the slow, heavy variable of *Indigenous land tenure*. The conflict in the Northwest (US-3) was not a sidebar to the war against Britain; it was the primary engine of American expansionism. Tecumseh's strategy of coordinated attrition was a sophisticated geopolitical response to the structural reality that neither the U.S. nor Britain viewed Indigenous nations as sovereign entities, but as buffers or obstacles. The siege of Fort Wayne validates that Indigenous power was still a decisive force capable of halting American westward momentum, but only so long as British supply lines from Canada remained open. Here, the connection to the global stage is absolute: the British supplies fueling Tecumseh's resistance were the same logistical streams threatened by Napoleon's march on Moscow (W-1).

The European theater provides the scaffolding for the American experience. Napoleon's invasion of Russia (W-1) was the gravitational center of 1812, distorting time and resources globally. It drew British eyes eastward, creating the window Madison tried to exploit. Yet, the assassination of Spencer Perceval (W-2) introduces a critical element of contingency often erased by structural determinism. Perceval was the linchpin of a Tory government managing a complex coalition; his removal by a lone, aggrieved merchant—a symptom of the very trade grievances sparking the war in America—momentarily paralyzed British decision-making. Had Perceval lived, the diplomatic off-ramp might have been taken before the first shot was fired. Instead, his death hardened the British stance, turning a negotiable dispute over impressment into an existential test of imperial resolve. Simultaneously, the Treaty of Bucharest (W-3) demonstrates the efficiency of the Old World's balance-of-power machinery; Russia secured its southern flank just in time to face the French, ensuring that the European concert would eventually crush Napoleon, thereby freeing Britain to turn its full, terrifying attention back to America in 1814.

The through-line to the present-day United States emerges from the ashes of Washington. The humiliation of 1812 exposed the fatal flaw of the Jeffersonian ideal: a republic of yeoman farmers cannot project power or defend its capital without a centralized state apparatus. The burning of the White House did not destroy the union, but it killed the illusion that the Union could survive on virtue alone. This trauma birthed the "Second American System"—a shift toward internal improvements, a national bank, and a professional military establishment. The specific inheritance we carry today is the *national security state's origin story*: the realization that geographic isolation is not a shield, and that economic sovereignty requires military coercion. The unresolved tension of 1812—between the desire for isolationist commerce and the necessity of imperial projection—remains the central contradiction of American foreign policy. We are living in the long shadow of a capital city that learned, via fire, that declaring war is easy, but surviving the structural consequences requires a machine the Founders never intended to build.

### Round 2 — Cross-Examination

**Heretic → Herald:**

Let me restate your argument at its strongest, because it is a good one and deserves the full weight you give it. You are saying that 1812 is not best understood as a geopolitical chess match or an economic calculation, but as a crisis of *narrative self-constitution*. The republic, in your reading, had declared independence in 1776 but had not yet *become* independent in the felt experience of its citizens or the recognition of European powers. The war declaration was therefore a performative act—the constitutional war power exercised precisely to prove that such a power existed, that the parchment government of Philadelphia could translate into the steel and sail of a nation among nations. You locate the historical drama not in the war's outcome but in the *gap* between aspiration and actuality, and you read the narrowness of the vote not as a flaw in the story but as the very texture of the gamble. The nation was divided, yes, but the division itself testified to the stakes: sovereignty was not inherited, it was chosen, and choosing it nearly broke the chooser. This is a reading that takes seriously what contemporaries *believed* they were doing, and it refuses the condescension of posterity.

Now let me press where the narrative you have chosen—and it is a choice—does its own work of omission.

You say the war was a "performance of sovereignty." I accept the term. But performance requires an audience, and performance requires a stage. Who was the audience for this performance? You gesture toward European powers, toward the British Admiralty that needed to be shown that American declarations had teeth. But the more immediate audience—the one that watched from the galleries of the House and the one that read the declaration in translation across the Ohio Valley—was Indigenous. The War Hawks did not need to perform sovereignty for George III; they needed to perform it for Tecumseh. The sovereignty being asserted was not abstract membership in the society of nations. It was the concrete sovereignty to extinguish Native title, to survey and sell land, to make good on the paper claims that speculators and planters already held. The "becoming-real" you describe was, on the ground, the becoming-real of the cotton frontier. That is not a metaphor. That is a ledger entry.

So my question is this: when you center the felt experience of the war's advocates—their sincere belief that they were completing the Revolution—do you not also center their convenient blindness to what that completion required? In 1812, the American republic did not merely declare war; it declared a *story* about itself, and the potency of that declaration lay precisely in its audacious mismatch with fact.
