## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — Wagner Act Signed (July 5, 1935)
- **What happened (fact):** Roosevelt signs the National Labor Relations Act, establishing the right to unionize and creating the NLRB to enforce collective bargaining.
- **The narrative *then*:** Industrial democracy finally arrives. The factory ceases to be the boss's private kingdom; the worker becomes a citizen even at the point of production. Labor leaders call it the Magna Carta of American workers, while business spokesmen denounce it as revolution by another name. The National Labor Relations Board will be the honest broker, the neutral referee in what had been a street brawl with one combatant bound and gagged. For millions, this is the New Deal's soul—not relief, but rights.
- **The narrative *now*:** The last monument of a vanished labor republic. Invoked ritualistically by Democrats, dismantled in practice by decades of appointments and rulings that hollowed out "protected activity." The Wagner Act survives as text while its animating assumptions—collective bargaining as public good, worker power as stabilizing force—have become nearly unintelligible in an economy of gig contracts and "right to work." It is remembered as origin myth by a movement that has lost the factory floor.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The statutory creation of industrial citizenship, and the template for how American liberalism would try to solve class conflict through rights-based institutionalism rather than structural transformation.

#### US-2 — Social Security Act Enacted (August 14, 1935)
- **What happened (fact):** Congress passes the cornerstone of the American welfare state, creating old-age pensions and unemployment insurance while excluding agricultural and domestic workers.
- **The narrative *then*:** The end of the poorhouse, the beginning of security. Roosevelt frames it as "protection against the hazards and vicissitudes of life," a compact between generations. The excluded workers are noted in passing, if at all—practical politics, the price of Southern votes, the administrative difficulty of covering field hands and maids. The dominant feeling is forward motion: at last, a nation that does not abandon its elderly to destitution. The payroll tax is sold as earned right, not charity, preserving dignity through contributory design.
- **The narrative *now:** The American welfare state's original sin and enduring foundation. The racial exclusions are central to the story, not footnotes—demonstrating how white democracy built its social compact on black exclusion, how Social Security's very architecture of "earned" benefits encoded occupational segregation. Yet it is also the program that lifted the elderly from poverty, the third rail of politics, the proof that American exceptionalism need not mean American cruelty. It lives as both indictment and achievement.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The most consequential domestic legislation of the twentieth century, establishing the institutional form and moral vocabulary of American social provision while inscribing racial hierarchy into its structure.

#### US-3 — Supreme Court Strikes Down AAA (January 6, 1935)
- **What happened (fact):** In *United States v. Butler*, the Court rules the Agricultural Adjustment Act unconstitutional, invalidating the federal tax used to subsidize crop reduction.
- **The narrative *then*:** The nine old men stand athwart history. For New Dealers, *Butler* is judicial sabotage of recovery itself—farmers already receiving checks told the program was illegal, the Constitution wielded as a veto on democratic economic experiment. For conservatives, it is the last stand of enumerated powers against administrative overreach, the line between republic and bureaucracy. The decision feeds a mounting crisis: will the New Deal be ruled out of bounds before it can be tested? The court-packing scheme gestates in this moment.
- **The narrative *now*:** A constitutional curiosity, overshadowed by the switch-in-time and the post-1937 settlement. *Butler* is taught as Commerce Clause archaeology, the dead hand of *Hammer v. Dagenhart* logic applied to agriculture. The substantive result—farmers did get their subsidies, eventually, through different constitutional doors—makes it seem like theater. What endures is the jurisprudential question it posed and deferred: what economic activities are "local" and what "national," a question that would resurface in *Lopez* and *Sebelius* but with the ideological poles reversed.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The moment when constitutional conservatism most directly threatened New Deal governance, and the crisis that forced Roosevelt toward his most consequential political miscalculation (court-packing) and his most consequential victory (the constitutional revolution of 1937).

#### W-1 — German Conscription Decreed (March 16, 1935)
- **What happened (fact):** Hitler formally announces the reintroduction of mandatory military service and the expansion of the Wehrmacht, openly violating the Treaty of Versailles.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Germans, the shackles fall. The Versailles "dictate" is repudiated not by protest but by deed; Germany rejoins the community of sovereign nations, no longer a second-class state. The Rhineland militarization will follow. For France and Britain, a dilemma: enforce the treaty and risk war, or accommodate and encourage further demands. The League notes the violation; the Stresa Front forms and immediately frays. For American newspapers, another European crisis, distant, complicated, probably manageable. The narrative of German grievance competes with the narrative of Hitler's unpredictability.
- **The narrative *now*:** The first unmistakable step beyond revisionism into militarization, the moment when "Hitler only wants" apologetics should have died. Conscription is read backward through the full Holocaust and world war as the obvious prelude to catastrophe. Yet the *then*-narrative of legitimate grievance has not disappeared—it mutates into "Versailles was too harsh," a historical consolation that preserves German victimhood even while condemning Nazi crimes. The date functions as a test: who saw, who flinched, who collaborated in the not-seeing.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The open demolition of the interwar security architecture, and the moment when Hitler's foreign policy graduated from bluff to committed rearmament.

#### W-2 — Nuremberg Laws Promulgated (September 15, 1935)
- **What happened (fact):** The Nazi regime codifies racial antisemitism into law, stripping German Jews of citizenship and prohibiting marriage or sexual relations between Jews and non-Jews.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Germans, the restoration of "healthy" racial order through legal means—modern, scientific, hygienic. The laws are announced at a Nazi Party rally, theatrical and triumphant. For foreign observers, disturbing but perhaps containable: Germany has its "Jewish problem," every nation has its minorities, at least this is legal, not pogrom. Some American Jewish organizations protest; the State Department notes and files. The *Reich Citizenship Law* and *Law for the Protection of German Blood* are reported as internal German matters, the latest in a series of discriminatory measures.
- **The narrative *now*:** The bridge from prejudice to genocide made visible in statutory text. The Nuremberg Laws are taught as the moment when the state committed itself to racial purification through legal instrumentalities, demonstrating that modern bureaucracy could serve barbarism with precision. The "at least it's legal" response of contemporaries is held up as moral failure, a warning against proceduralism detached from substantive justice. The laws are also the origin point for tracing German complicity: after September 1935, racial antisemitism was not mob violence but state policy, knowable and known.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The legal crystallization of Nazi racial ideology, transforming antisemitism from populist rhetoric into bureaucratic program, and the clearest pre-war signal of the regime's ultimate intentions.

#### W-3 — Italy Invades Ethiopia (October 3, 1935)
- **What happened (fact):** Mussolini orders Italian forces from Eritrea and Somaliland to invade Abyssinia, initiating a colonial war of conquest that exposes the League of Nations' inability to enforce collective security.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Italy, the completion of Risorgimento empire, revenge for Adwa, the civilizing mission finally fulfilled. For Ethiopia, the defense of African independence against European aggression—the Emperor Haile Selassie will plead before the League, a black monarch claiming the rights of sovereign equality that the League's covenant promised. For Britain and France, an excruciating choice: principles or interests, the Stresa Front or the League, Abyssinia or appeasing Mussolini to contain Hitler. The Hoare-Laval plan betrays the choice. For African Americans and colonized peoples globally, Ethiopia becomes a symbol—fund-raising, volunteer schemes, the black international's cause célèbre.
- **The narrative *now*:** The death certificate of collective security, the demonstration that the interwar order could not enforce its own rules against a determined aggressor with great-power patrons. Ethiopia's resistance is recovered as anti-colonial precursor, Selassie's League speech as founding text of postcolonial international law. The invasion is also remembered as the event that drove Mussolini toward Hitler—sanctions pushing Italy into German arms, the Rome-Berlin Axis forged in Abyssinian dust. The Hoare-Laval affair remains a byword for cynical betrayal dressed in diplomatic language.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The terminal crisis of the League system, the demonstration that international law without enforcement was rhetoric, and the event that rearranged European alignments toward fascist alliance.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected because they represent the crystallization of structural forces rather than mere incidents: the Wagner and Social Security Acts institutionalize the New Deal's shift from emergency relief to permanent state capacity, directly answering the `industrial-frontier` and `dawes-capital-loop` threads; the *Butler* decision forces the `broadcast-presidency` into open conflict with the judiciary; globally, German conscription and the Nuremberg Laws mark the transition of the `totalitarian-drift` from internal purge to external aggression and ideological codification; and the invasion of Ethiopia demonstrates the collapse of the interwar diplomatic order. I deliberately omitted the "Wealth Tax" (Revenue Act of 1935) despite its rhetorical fame, because its material impact was negligible compared to the structural rewiring accomplished by Social Security and labor law; similarly, I excluded the Lindbergh kidnapping trial resolution, as it was a cultural spectacle that did not alter the underlying geopolitical or economic tectonic plates of the year.

## 2. Connections Within the Year

- **U.S. ↔ World:** The collapse of liberal international order abroad (W-3) and the rise of autarkic militarism in Europe (W-1) forced the U.S. to turn inward, accelerating the construction of a closed-loop domestic stability pact (US-1, US-2) that prioritized internal consumption and labor peace over global engagement, effectively decoupling American recovery from European security.
- **Event ↔ Event:** The judicial dismantling of the agrarian subsidy state (US-3) created an immediate legislative vacuum that necessitated the rapid, compensatory enactment of the Wagner and Social Security Acts (US-1, US-2); simultaneously, the legal codification of racial hierarchy in Germany (W-2) mirrored the structural exclusions baked into the U.S. welfare state (US-2), where "universal" benefits were engineered to preserve a low-wage, racially segmented labor force in the South.
- **Invisible / structural (shared hidden drivers):** The global crisis of overproduction and underconsumption drove both the fascist drive for territorial expansion to secure resources (W-1, W-3) and the New Deal's attempt to manage surplus through state-mediated demand (US-1, US-2); beneath both lies the rigidification of the nation-state as the primary unit of economic survival, replacing the fluidity of the pre-1914 gold standard era with bordered, managed economies.

#### 🜂 Herald
1935 is the year of *the story that failed*—the interwar narrative of collective security, of social progress through institutional reform, of fascism as a phase or a national peculiarity. What contemporaries believed, and what we inherit, diverge most sharply here: they were still inside the story of possible rescue, while we know it as the last act before the catastrophe.

Consider the *then*-narrative of American liberalism in this year. The Wagner Act and Social Security arrive not as terminal monuments but as beginnings—the first steps toward a reconstituted republic. The National Labor Relations Board will civilize industrial conflict; old-age pensions will end the terror of dependency. The exclusions are visible to the excluded, invisible or excusable to the architects. Roosevelt's signature generates a specific hope: that capitalism can be democratized without revolution, that the state can be simultaneously activist and constitutional. The *now*-narrative preserves the achievements while mourning the truncation—the Wagner Act's promise of workplace democracy reduced to bureaucratic procedure, Social Security's racial exclusions only partially repaired, the New Deal's class politics submerged into a technocratic liberalism that would lose the language of power. We tell the story as prelude to the Great Compression, the thirty glorious years that followed 1945; but we also tell it as the moment when American liberalism chose insurance over redistribution, stability over transformation, and so prepared its own decline.

The Supreme Court's *Butler* decision sits within this drama as the conservative counter-narrative at full strength. In January 1935, the Court speaks for a constitutional order that believes itself permanent: enumerated powers, dual federalism, the agricultural economy as fundamentally local. The New Dealers experience this not as jurisprudence but as sabotage—the deliberate disabling of democratic capacity in the name of abstractions. The *then*-narrative of constitutional crisis is urgent, existential: will the New Deal be permitted to exist? The *now*-narrative has softened this into institutional conflict, the "switch in time" as pragmatic adjustment, *Butler* as road not taken. But the deeper story is the story of constitutional faith itself—the belief that a text could and should limit democratic majorities, and the rival belief that emergency required emergency measures. Both faiths persist in American politics; 1935 is when they last fought with the stakes fully visible.

Across the Atlantic, 1935 destroys the remaining narratives of containment and normalcy. German conscription in March is reported, debated, accommodated. The *then*-narrative includes genuine uncertainty: Is Hitler a madman or a statesman? Is rearmament defensive or offensive? The British Foreign Office, the French general staff, the American isolationist press—all participate in a collective narrative of manageable risk. Versailles was unjust; Germany seeks equality; the Stresa Front will restrain; Hitler can be dealt with. This is not stupidity; it is the operation of a story that the Great War had been the war to end war, that modern nations do not willfully choose catastrophe. The *now*-narrative cannot afford this generosity. We read March 1935 as the first unambiguous step toward total war, and we judge the contemporaries harshly for their failure to see what we see. But the Herald's obligation is to recover the *plausibility* of their blindness—the genuine desire to believe that the twentieth century had learned something, that the machinery of diplomacy still functioned, that Hitler was a phenomenon that could be bounded by traditional statecraft. The narrative work of 1935 was the work of hope against evidence, and hope is not contemptible even when it is wrong.

The Nuremberg Laws destroy a different kind of hope: the hope that Nazi antisemitism was rhetorical, opportunistic, containable. In September 1935, the regime moves Jew-hatred from propaganda to statute, from mob to bureaucracy. The *then*-narrative is fractured. For Germans attending the rally, or reading the *Völkischer Beobachter*, this is the restoration of natural order through modern means. For German Jews, it is the legal crystallization of exclusion, the moment when emancipation's promise is formally revoked. For foreign observers, it is variously disturbing, distant, or deniable—"internal German affairs," "not our concern," "at least it's legal." The *now*-narrative has made the Nuremberg Laws central to the Holocaust's prehistory, the bridge from discrimination to destruction. We read them as the regime's announcement of ultimate intention, and we are right to do so. But the Herald must also track the narrative labor of 1935: the work of making racial hierarchy appear as progress, as science, as law. The Nuremberg Laws were not chaotic violence but ordered procedure; their very formality is what made them useful to the regime and what made them legible to a world trained to respect legal process. The story they told was the story of purification, of health restored, of the nation healing itself. This story was believed, enacted, and defended by millions who would later claim ignorance or coercion. The Nuremberg Laws are where we must study belief as historical force—not as sincere interior state but as public performance that generates its own sincerity.

The *then*-narrative is excruciating for its participants. #### 🜄 Heretic
The received story of 1935 is seductive precisely because it lets us feel tragic rather than implicated. It goes like this: democratic governments, however imperfectly, reached toward their citizens with new protections—the Wagner Act giving workers a seat at the table, Social Security promising dignity in old age. Meanwhile, the dictators revealed their true nature: Hitler tore up Versailles and codified antisemitism into law; Mussolini launched a colonial war of conquest. The League of Nations proved hollow. The democracies tried to build a decent world; the fascists built a death machine. The tragedy is that the decent world wasn’t ready in time. We inherit the unfinished business of the New Deal and the memory of what happens when collective security fails.

This is a story that has been carefully washed. Let us follow the money, the silence, and the gun.

Begin with the Wagner Act. The official narrative—then and now—is that the state finally intervened to correct the power imbalance between capital and labor. Workers could organize without retaliation; employers had to bargain in good faith. Cui bono? The answer is not simply “workers.” The answer is the industrial union movement, yes, but also the Democratic Party, which secured a multi-generational voting bloc, and—more quietly—the large corporate employers who preferred regulated, predictable, contract-bound unions to the wildcat strikes, the sit-downs, the communist organizers who answered to no NLRB procedure. The Wagner Act did not simply *grant* rights; it *channeled* militancy into a legal framework administered by the state. The year 1934 had seen general strikes in San Francisco, Minneapolis, and Toledo—insurrections in the streets, workers seizing control of production and distribution. The Wagner Act, signed in July 1935, was in part a response to that insurgency, and its effect was to demobilize it. The NLRB would hear your grievance. You would not need the general strike. The omission in the standard history is the radical alternative that was foreclosed: industrial unionism as a revolutionary force, not a junior partner in the Democratic coalition. The comfortable version lets us avoid feeling responsible for the fact that American labor was domesticated, not liberated, and that the domesticated union movement would, within a generation, become a conservative force—defending seniority over solidarity, white job lines over class unity, and eventually its own institutional survival over the unorganized.

Now Social Security. The exclusions are not a footnote; they are the architecture. Agricultural workers and domestic workers—the two largest categories of Black labor in 1935, and a substantial portion of Latino and Asian labor—were written out of the bill to secure the votes of Southern Democrats. The Southern committee chairmen would not permit a program that gave federal benefits to Black workers on terms approaching equality with whites, because that would disrupt the racial wage hierarchy and the political economy of the plantation. Roosevelt and the bill’s architects knew this. They chose to build a welfare state with a color line running through it. The *then*-narrative among reformers was that this was a necessary compromise, a foot in the door. The *now*-narrative treats it as a blemish later corrected. The Heretic’s lens sees something else: the creation of a two-tier citizenship that would compound for decades. Social Security became a program in which white workers accrued benefits while Black workers, excluded from covered employment, were pushed into the stigmatized, means-tested, locally administered programs of the welfare state—the ones that would later be racialized as “welfare” itself. The omission is the voice of the Black sharecropper, the Latina domestic, the Filipino farmworker, for whom 1935 was not the year the safety net was woven but the year they were formally classified as outside the circle of desert. The gap between rhetoric and action is not a compromise; it is a founding crime of the American welfare state, and its consequences are alive in the racial wealth gap today.

The Supreme Court’s *Butler* decision is usually narrated as a clash of constitutional philosophies—the Four Horsemen versus the New Deal, federal power versus states’ rights. Cui bono from the story told about it? The story makes the Court’s opposition abstract, principled, legal. But the AAA was struck down because it taxed processors to pay farmers to reduce production—at a moment when Americans were starving. The plaintiffs in *Butler* were the Hoosac Mills Corporation, a textile firm that didn’t want to pay the processing tax. The beneficiaries of the decision were the processors who kept the money. The omitted perspective is that of the tenant farmers and sharecroppers who were being *evicted* under the AAA’s crop-reduction program, because landlords took the federal payments and then pushed tenants off the land—plowing under cotton and plowing under people simultaneously. The AAA was a subsidy to landowners, and the constitutional challenge came from other capitalists who didn’t want to fund it. The poor—Black and white—were ground between them. The *Butler* decision is not a tragedy of constitutional obstruction; it is a fight among the powerful over who would extract from the weak, dressed in the language of enumerated powers.

Now cross the Atlantic. The Nuremberg Laws, promulgated in September, are the event that makes the comfortable narrative possible: here is the evil, unmistakable, against which the democracies would eventually define themselves. But what is omitted? The silence of the democracies in 1935. The Nuremberg Laws were public, explicit, and widely reported. They stripped citizenship from German Jews and criminalized intimacy across a racial line the Nazis had just invented. The United States did not break diplomatic relations. The British did not break diplomatic relations. The League of Nations did not convene an emergency session on the statelessness of German Jews. The *then*-narrative in the democracies was that this was an internal German matter—unpleasant, extreme, but not a casus belli. The *now*-narrative treats the Holocaust as the rupture that revealed the depths. But in 1935, the depth was already visible to anyone who chose to look, and the choice was to look away. The omission is the voice of the German Jew in September 1935, who could read the laws and see the future, and who could also read the newspapers from London and Washington and see that no one was coming. The comfortable version lets us mourn the victims later without asking why the democracies granted the Nazi regime the sovereign right to classify and degrade its own citizens without external consequence.

German conscription, announced in March, is the companion piece. Hitler violated Versailles openly, and the Western powers responded with—what? A protest note. The Stresa Front, formed in April between Britain, France, and Italy, condemned German rearmament and then did nothing. The omission here is the strategic calculation that is rarely named plainly: British and French elites were more afraid of a revived German *left* than of a rearmed German *right*. Hitler had crushed the German Communist Party and the Social Democrats; he had abolished the trade unions. To the conservative establishments of London and Paris, this was not entirely unwelcome. A rearmed Nazi Germany was a problem for the future; a communist Germany would have been a problem for the present. The gap between the rhetoric of collective security and the action of accommodation is not weakness; it is preference. The comfortable story says the democracies were unprepared, naive, slow to awaken. The Heretic’s lens says they were awake enough, and their nightmare was not fascism but revolution.

And then Italy invades Ethiopia. October 1935. This is the event that exposes the League of Nations, yes—but the exposure is not a discovery; it is a confirmation of what the colonized world already knew. The League was a club of imperial powers. Ethiopia was one of the only African states not under European colonial rule, and its crime, in the eyes of Mussolini, was precisely its independence. The League debated sanctions. Britain and France, the League’s great powers, had their own empires to protect; they would not set a precedent of punishing a European power for conquering African territory. The Suez Canal remained open to Italian supply ships. Oil sanctions were never imposed. The omission is the voice of the Ethiopian peasant facing Italian mustard gas—gas dropped from planes, gas that the Geneva Protocol of 1925 had banned, gas that the civilized powers had solemnly forsworn. Mussolini used it anyway, and the League’s response was to debate whether sanctions on oil would be too provocative. The *then*-narrative in the West was that this was a distant colonial quarrel, regrettable but not central. The *now*-narrative treats it as a prelude to World War II. But for the colonized world—for the Africans, the Asians, the subjects of empire watching from every corner of the globe—1935 was the year the mask fell completely. The League would not protect a Black nation from a white aggressor. International law was for Europeans. The gap between the rhetoric of collective security and the action of complicity is not a failure of the League; it is the League functioning exactly as designed.

What does the comfortable version of 1935 let us avoid feeling responsible for today? It lets us believe that the democracies were fundamentally decent but tragically slow, that the New Deal was fundamentally inclusive but pragmatically compromised, that the fascist threat was fundamentally external and exceptional. The Heretic’s lens sees a different inheritance: an American labor movement channeled into legalism and racial hierarchy; a welfare state built on the exclusion of Black and brown workers; a constitutional order that protected property against democracy; and a Western international order that, when confronted with fascist aggression and racialized persecution, chose to protect its own imperial prerogatives and class interests over the lives of Jews, Ethiopians, and the stateless. The year 1935 is not the year the story failed. It is the year the story worked—for those it was always meant to serve.

#### 🜃 Cartographer
The year 1935 is not a collection of disparate headlines but a singular tectonic shift in the architecture of the modern state. We are witnessing the death of the nineteenth-century liberal experiment—the belief that markets self-correct and that peace is the default condition of trade—and its replacement by the managerial state, a entity defined by its capacity to organize production, discipline labor, and define the boundaries of belonging. The six events of this year are the structural bolts being driven into this new framework, locking in trajectories that will define the United States for the next century.

Beneath the surface of the Wagner Act and the Social Security Act lies a desperate recalibration of the American mode of production. The Great Depression was not merely a cyclical downturn; it was a systemic failure of demand caused by the maldistribution of income inherent in unregulated industrial capitalism. The Wagner Act (US-1) is the Cartographer's primary evidence of the state intervening to artificially sustain purchasing power. By legalizing collective bargaining, the federal government transformed labor from a commodity subject to pure market volatility into a political institution with a guaranteed share of profits. This was not altruism; it was a structural necessity to prevent the total collapse of the consumer base upon which mass production depended. However, this new social contract had a geographic and racial fault line. The Social Security Act (US-2), while creating the skeleton of the American welfare state, explicitly excluded agricultural and domestic workers. This was not an oversight; it was a design feature required to secure the votes of the Southern Democratic bloc. The result was a dual-track economy: a protected, unionized, predominantly white industrial core in the North and Midwest, and an exposed, low-wage, predominantly Black labor reserve in the South. This structural bifurcation entrenched racial inequality into the very mechanics of American economic security, a distortion that echoes in the wealth gap and regional disparities of the present-day United States.

The Supreme Court's destruction of the Agricultural Adjustment Act in *United States v. Butler* (US-3) acts as the catalyst that hardened these legislative responses. The Court's invocation of "states' rights" to strike down federal crop controls threatened to unravel the entire New Deal coalition. The response was not retreat, but a pivot to more constitutionally durable mechanisms of control: regulating labor relations (interstate commerce) and insurance (taxing power). The speed with which Congress passed US-1 and US-2 after the January ruling in US-3 reveals the fragility of the recovery; the administration understood that without these specific structural supports, the industrial engine would seize. The "invisible" connection here is the transition from voluntary association to mandatory administration. The state was no longer a referee; it was the architect of the economic floor.

Globally, the map is redrawing itself along similar lines of statist consolidation, but through violence rather than legislation. Hitler's decree of conscription (W-1) and Mussolini's invasion of Ethiopia (W-3) are manifestations of the same crisis of overproduction and resource scarcity that plagued the democracies, but resolved through imperial expansion rather than internal redistribution. Germany and Italy, lacking the vast internal markets and resources of the United States or the British Empire, turned outward. They sought to solve the equation of limited space and population pressure by seizing territory. The League of Nations' failure to stop Italy in Ethiopia signaled the end of collective security and the return of the law of the jungle, where geography is destiny and might makes right. This forced the United States into a defensive crouch. The construction of the domestic welfare state (US-1, US-2) was, in part, a substitution for global engagement. As the world fractured into armed camps, America built a fortress of internal stability, choosing to manage its own contradictions rather than police the globe.

There is a chilling structural resonance between the Nuremberg Laws (W-2) and the exclusions in the Social Security Act (US-2). Both regimes were engaged in defining the "national community" (*Volksgemeinschaft* in Germany, the "deserving citizen" in America) by legally excluding specific groups from the protections of the state. In Germany, the exclusion was biological and total, stripping Jews of citizenship and human rights. In the United States, the exclusion was economic and occupational, designed to maintain a racial caste system under the guise of fiscal federalism. While the moral chasm between the Holocaust and the Southern plantation economy is vast, the *mechanism*—using state power to codify hierarchy and restrict the distribution of resources based on identity—is a shared tool of the 1930s state. The Cartographer notes that while the Herald may celebrate the birth of American social insurance and the Heretic may condemn the moral cowardice of excluding Black workers, the structural reality is that the American welfare state was built on a foundation of segmented citizenship that preserved the existing racial order even as it revolutionized class relations for whites.
