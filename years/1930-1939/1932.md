## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — The Bonus Army March (May–July 1932)
- **What happened (fact):** Twenty thousand World War I veterans and their families encamp in Washington D.C. demanding immediate cash redemption of service certificates, only to be violently dispersed by U.S. Army troops under General MacArthur.
- **The narrative *then*:** Two competing stories fight for dominance. To the marchers and their sympathizers, this is the betrayal of the "war to end all wars"—men who crossed the Atlantic for democracy denied bread in their own capital. The veterans frame themselves as petitioners, not revolutionaries; they sing "America the Beautiful" and name their largest camp "Hooverville" with bitter irony. To the administration and much of the respectable press, they are a "bonus army" of radicals and criminals, a threat to order requiring firm response. The burning camps on July 28 produce photographic evidence that wounds Hoover fatally: soldiers in gas masks, tanks on Pennsylvania Avenue, a future president's son (MacArthur's aide Eisenhower) present at the spectacle. The event becomes, in real time, a referendum on whether the federal government owes survival to its citizens or merely protection of property.
- **The narrative *now*:** The Bonus Army lives in American memory as prefiguration—the ghost of depression-era desperation that validates the New Deal's necessity, and simultaneously as a warning about military deployment against citizens. It is taught as Hoover's unforgivable sin, the moment when his humanitarian reputation collapsed into callousness. Less remembered: the 1936 passage of the adjusted compensation payment, which retroactively vindicates the marchers' demand and allows the story to close with moral symmetry. The veterans become noble victims whose suffering produced reform; the violence becomes the exception that proves democratic resilience.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the year when the social contract becomes visible and tearable. The march forces Americans to ask whether citizenship entitles one to bodily security from the state one served—a question that will resurface across the decade and beyond.

#### US-2 — The Presidential Election of 1932 (November 8, 1932)
- **What happened (fact):** Franklin D. Roosevelt defeats Herbert Hoover in a landslide realignment, securing 472 electoral votes by campaigning on a platform of federal relief and experimental governance against Hoover's doctrine of voluntary association.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Roosevelt's coalition, this is deliverance from a man who "crushed the spirit of the American people" (FDR's closing argument). The campaign produces a deliberate mythology of the "forgotten man"—a phrase borrowed from Sumner via Tugwell, transformed from social-Darwinist warning into populist summons. Roosevelt's vagueness is strategic: he promises "a new deal for the American people" without specifying its contents, allowing desperate voters to project their hopes onto him. For Hoover and his defenders, the election is catastrophe engineered by demagoguery, the repudiation of American self-reliance by a frightened electorate seduced by promises of government paternalism. The size of the defeat—59 electoral votes for Hoover, the worst incumbent performance to date—suggests not mere rejection but symbolic annihilation.
- **The narrative *now*:** 1932 is the founding election of modern American liberalism, the moment when "the people" definitively chose activist government over laissez-faire. The textbook version flattens the campaign's radical uncertainty: Roosevelt ran to Hoover's right on budget balancing, and his "experimentation" was less program than posture. What we inherit is the myth of mandate—the belief that Americans consciously chose the welfare state, when in fact they chose change itself and received a welfare state. The election becomes the origin story that legitimizes subsequent federal expansion; to question New Deal premises is to question "the verdict of 1932."
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the most consequential American electoral realignment between 1860 and 1968. The coalition assembled here—urban ethnics, Southern whites, labor, intellectuals—defines Democratic possibility for two generations.

#### US-3 — The Reconstruction Finance Corporation Expansion (July 1932)
- **What happened (fact):** Congress broadens the RFC's mandate beyond bank bailouts to include loans for railroads and agricultural credit institutions, marking the first structural admission that private liquidity mechanisms have permanently failed.
- **The narrative *then*:** To proponents, the RFC expansion is reluctant surgery on a dying patient—Hoover's final concession that voluntary cooperation cannot stem catastrophe. The administration frames it as temporary, targeted, and ultimately self-liquidating: government as lender of last resort, not permanent economic actor. Critics on the left call it "millionaire dole," aid flowing upward to the same institutions that caused collapse. Critics on the right see creeping collectivism, the first irreversible step toward state capitalism. The expansion passes with bipartisan support but no enthusiasm; it is policy made by exhaustion rather than conviction.
- **The narrative *now:** The RFC expansion is largely forgotten, absorbed into the prehistory of New Deal institutionalism. When remembered, it serves either as evidence that Hoover was not the do-nothing of caricature (conservative revision) or as proof that half-measures failed and full New Deal was necessary (liberal orthodoxy). The deeper story—permanent government assumption of functions previously delegated to private financial intermediaries—becomes visible only in retrospect, as the moment when the federal balance sheet became the economy's backstop.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is structural transformation disguised as emergency measure. The RFC's logic—socialize risk to preserve private reward—becomes the template for American crisis management from 1932 forward.

#### W-1 — Consolidation of Manchukuo (February 18, 1932)
- **What happened (fact):** Japan formally proclaims the independence of Manchukuo, installing Puyi as chief executive and effectively annexing Manchuria despite the League of Nations' Lytton Report condemning the action as aggression.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Japanese militarists and their domestic allies, Manchukuo represents liberation from Western-imposed "unequal treaties," the creation of a "harmonious" multi-ethnic state under Asian leadership. The puppet emperor Puyi provides monarchical legitimacy; the name "Manchukuo" suggests nationhood rather than colony. Japanese press and schoolbooks celebrate the Kwantung Army's initiative as young officers saving Asia from corrupt party politics at home and Soviet expansion abroad. For Chinese nationalists and the international community, it is naked imperialism, the first major violation of the postwar order established at Versailles. The Lytton Report's careful condemnation—calling for Japanese withdrawal while acknowledging "special interests"—produces not resolution but the template for future appeasement: strong language, weak mechanisms.
- **The narrative *now*:** Manchukuo occupies ambiguous memory. In Japan, it is the origin of the "fifteen-year war," increasingly acknowledged as aggression but still contested by nationalist revisionists. In China, it is the beginning of fourteen years of Japanese invasion, now central to patriotic education. In Western memory, it is the failed test of collective security, the moment the League died—more important for what it revealed about international institutions than for what Japan accomplished. Puyi's later testimony at Tokyo war crimes trials, his memoir as both victim and collaborator, complicates any simple narrative.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the first major challenge to the interwar order, and its unpunished success teaches lessons about weakness that will shape 1930s crisis diplomacy. The narrative structures established here—puppet statehood as cover for annexation, international condemnation without enforcement—become standard features of the decade.

#### W-2 — The Holodomor Peak (Spring–Summer 1932)
- **What happened (fact):** Soviet enforcement of Law of Spikelets criminalizes the gathering of leftover grain in Ukraine, accelerating a man-made famine that kills approximately three million people as the state prioritizes export quotas over domestic survival.
- **The narrative *then*:** Inside the Soviet Union, the famine officially does not exist. Western journalists like Walter Duranty deny starvation in the New York Times; visiting intellectuals report socialist construction's triumph. The small number who speak truth—Gareth Jones, Malcolm Muggeridge—are marginalized as reactionaries or fabulists. Among Ukrainian peasants, the event is understood as punishment: for resistance to collectivization, for national identity, for the sin of being surplus to the state's industrialization project. The law against gathering spikelets transforms survival itself into crime, making the famine's man-made nature legible even to its victims. In Western capitals, the story competes with depression preoccupations; those who notice often find reasons not to believe, preserving diplomatic and commercial relationships with the Soviet experiment.
- **The narrative *now*:** The Holodomor has become central to Ukrainian national identity and statehood, recognized as genocide by numerous parliaments though contested by Russia and some historians. The memory work of diaspora communities, accelerated by 2014 and 2022, has moved the event from scholarly specialty to geopolitical symbol. The Duranty Pulitzer remains unretracted, a standing reproach to journalistic credulity. What was denied then is now asserted with perhaps compensatory certainty; the precise role of intentionality—Stalin's genocidal purpose versus bureaucratic murder—remains historiographically active. The event now serves as both Ukrainian founding trauma and universal warning about totalitarianism's capacity to starve while exporting grain.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the decade's most lethal single event, and its contemporary invisibility is itself historically instructive. The gap between then-narrative and now-narrative measures the power of ideological commitment to occlude mass death.

#### W-3 — The Imperial Preference System (August 1932)
- **What happened (fact):** The British Empire abandons free trade at the Ottawa Conference, establishing a system of internal tariffs that fractures global commerce into competing blocs and confirms the death of the pre-war liberal economic order.
- **The narrative *then*:** For British Conservatives and Dominion governments, Ottawa is imperial solidarity made concrete—"buy British" as patriotic duty and economic survival. The conference produces genuine enthusiasm for a self-sufficient empire bloc, an alternative to American and German competition. Free traders mourn: John Maynard Keynes, recently converted from classical orthodoxy, still finds the spectacle depressing; Lionel Robbins sees the end of cosmopolitan civilization. For American observers, it is betrayal of the open door, confirmation that even liberal Britain has succumbed to beggar-thy-neighbor nationalism. For colonial subjects, the "imperial preference" is double exploitation: locked into British markets, excluded from industrial competition.
- **The narrative *now*:** Ottawa is remembered, when remembered, as one of the 1930s' destructive competitive devaluations, a step toward the economic nationalism that prolonged depression and fed political extremism. The Brexit-era revival of "imperial preference" rhetoric—sometimes nostalgic, sometimes ironic—reveals its persistence as dream. More substantially, the Ottawa system shaped GATT's founding purpose: multilateralism as explicit rejection of bloc economics. The empire's economic logic is now read as last-gasp imperialism, the attempt to extract value from declining dominion through preferential extraction.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the formal burial of the 19th-century economic order. The alternatives constructed here—regional blocs, managed trade, state-directed commerce—define the post-1945 system by opposition and, in some respects, by inheritance.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected because they represent the moment when structural failures hardened into new political and geological realities: the Bonus Army and the election mark the terminal collapse of the voluntarist state; the RFC expansion admits the necessity of permanent federal intervention; Manchukuo and Ottawa confirm the end of global collective security and free trade; and the Holodomor illustrates the lethal endpoint of high-modernist bureaucratic rigidity. I deliberately omitted Lindbergh's baby kidnapping, which dominated headlines but was a singular criminal tragedy rather than a systemic driver, and the Chicago World's Fair ("Century of Progress"), whose optimistic theme stood in such stark, ironic contradiction to the material conditions of the year that including it would obscure the grim texture of 1932's actual lived experience.

## 2. Connections Within the Year

- **U.S. ↔ World:** The collapse of the global gold standard's liquidity mechanism forced a synchronous shift from liberal internationalism to autarkic survivalism; while Britain fractured trade into the Imperial Preference System (W-3) and Japan seized Manchuria (W-1) to secure resource blocs, the U.S. turned inward, abandoning Hoover's voluntary internationalism for the domestic fortress-building of the RFC expansion (US-3) and the political realignment of 1932 (US-2).
- **Event ↔ Event:** The violence against the Bonus Army (US-1) served as the terminal symptom of the "voluntary association" doctrine that had already failed globally, directly catalyzing the electoral mandate (US-2) required to institutionalize the state-led credit interventions first tentatively admitted in the RFC expansion (US-3); simultaneously, the same global deflationary pressure that starved Ukraine via the Law of Spikelets (W-2) drove American farmers into destitution, linking the Soviet grain requisitions to the American demand for federal agricultural credit.
- **Invisible / structural (shared hidden drivers):** The breaking point of the interwar demographic-economic contract: when the mode of production (industrial/agricultural surplus) collided with a financial architecture (gold standard) that could not distribute purchasing power, states universally reverted to the primal logic of territorial or juridical coercion—whether drawing borders in Ottawa, bayonets in Washington, or grain quotas in Kyiv—to maintain regime stability.

#### 🜄 Heretic
Let me begin by taking the Herald’s invitation seriously. Yes, 1932 was a year of narrative collapse, of vertigo, of stories failing. The Bonus Army did invoke the citizen-soldier. Roosevelt did offer himself as a vessel for desperate hope. The Holodomor was, for those starving in it, a catastrophe beyond language. The Imperial Preference System did mark the formal death of free-trade liberalism. These are not fictions. They are the felt experience of millions. The Herald is right that we should recover the contemporaneous experience of living without knowing the endings.

Now let me tell you what that recovery, if we stop there, is designed to make you forget.

Start with the Bonus Army. The steelman: twenty thousand veterans, many with families, camped in the capital asking for early payment of a bonus Congress had already voted them—a bonus not due until 1945. They were peaceful. They were desperate. MacArthur routed them with cavalry, tanks, and gas. The images were damning. The story became: the state made war on its own veterans. All true. Now ask who benefits from that story being the *only* story. The veterans got their moral victory—and no money. The bonus was not paid. The spectacle of state violence became a political weapon for Roosevelt, who used it to paint Hoover as heartless while carefully, publicly, refusing to endorse the bonus payment himself. Roosevelt, the great humanitarian-in-waiting, opposed immediate cash redemption. He proposed instead to put veterans in the Civilian Conservation Corps at a dollar a day. The gap between the rhetoric of compassion and the action of refusal is the whole point. The Bonus Army was useful as martyrs. As claimants on the treasury, they were a problem to be managed. The comfortable version—Hoover the villain, FDR the savior—lets us avoid noticing that the New Deal’s first instinct toward the desperate was not payment but labor discipline.

The election. The Herald celebrates Roosevelt’s narrative genius, his blank-screen candidacy onto which a suffering nation projected its hopes. But look at what was actually said, and by whom. Roosevelt campaigned against Hoover from the right on spending, promising to cut federal expenditures by twenty-five percent and restore a balanced budget. He attacked the Reconstruction Finance Corporation as a giveaway to the banks. This is not interpretation; this is the text of the Pittsburgh speech. The coalition that assembled around him included Southern segregationists whose price for support was the preservation of Jim Crow, and Roosevelt paid it—no civil rights plank, no anti-lynching promise, no challenge to the poll tax. The New Deal coalition was built, from its first brick, on the exclusion of Black Americans from the protections it would later extend to others. The omission is not a footnote. It is the foundation. When we remember 1932 as the year democracy chose compassion, we launder the fact that the coalition that made that choice possible was a white supremacist bargain.

The RFC expansion. The steelman: Congress finally admitted that private liquidity mechanisms had failed, and the state stepped in to save railroads and agricultural credit. This is narrated as the first structural admission of market failure, the seed of the New Deal. But cui bono? The RFC was never a relief agency. It was a bailout machine for the holders of capital. Its chairman, Charles Dawes, resigned to run his own bank—which promptly received an RFC loan. The railroads that got the money used it not to hire workers but to pay bondholders. The agricultural credit institutions it rescued were the ones that had been foreclosing on farmers. The RFC expansion was not the state reaching down to save the desperate; it was the state building a ladder up from the vault. The language of “structural admission” obscures the structure itself: the same men who had presided over the collapse were now administering the rescue, and they rescued themselves first.

Manchukuo. The Lytton Report condemned Japan’s action as aggression—a rare moment of international conscience, the League of Nations speaking truth to power. That is the story. Here is the silence: the League’s condemnation was possible only because Japan had trespassed against the imperial carve-up that the Western powers themselves had authored. Britain, France, and the United States had spent a century extracting concessions, carving spheres of influence, and imposing unequal treaties on China. Japan was doing what they had done, just more efficiently and with less hypocrisy. The Lytton Report was not a defense of Chinese sovereignty—the League had never recognized Chinese sovereignty as inviolable when European powers were the violators. It was a defense of the existing imperial order against an upstart competitor. The silence in the archive is the voice of the colonized, who understood perfectly well that the outrage was not about principle but about who got to hold the gun.

The Holodomor. The Herald calls it a man-made famine. That is already a concession to the counter-narrative, and it took sixty years for that word to enter respectable discourse. In 1932, the Soviet state was exporting grain—1.7 million tons of it—while Ukrainians starved. The Law of Spikelets criminalized gathering leftover grain with sentences of up to ten years or execution. The state’s own records show that officials knew the death toll and prioritized export quotas anyway. This was not mismanagement. This was policy. The omission that matters is not just the Soviet propaganda that denied the famine; it is the Western left’s willing consumption of that propaganda. Walter Duranty of the *New York Times* won a Pulitzer Prize for reporting that there was no famine. The comfortable version lets us blame Stalin alone. It lets us forget that the information was available and was suppressed by people who needed the Soviet experiment to be a success story. The famine was not just a crime of commission by the Soviet state; it was a crime of omission by a Western intelligentsia that had invested its moral credibility in the Revolution and could not afford to see what it had become.

The Imperial Preference System. The story: Britain abandoned free trade, and with it died the pre-war liberal economic order. This is narrated as a tragedy, the fragmentation of global commerce into competing blocs. But ask whose free trade, whose liberal order. The pre-war system was not a level playing field; it was a hierarchy in which Britain sat at the top, extracting raw materials from colonies and selling finished goods back to them on terms it dictated. The Ottawa Conference did not invent imperial preference; it formalized what had always been true—that the empire was a closed shop dressed in the language of open markets. The death of free trade was a tragedy for those who had benefited from the arrangement. For the colonized, the system had never been free, and its formal death changed little except the address to which the tribute was sent. The silence, as always, is the voice of the Indian peasant, the West African farmer, the Caribbean laborer, for whom “free trade” had always meant the freedom of the strong to take from the weak.

What unites these six events is not narrative collapse. It is the ruthless continuity of power, the gap between the story told and the action taken, and the systematic exclusion from the record of those who paid the price. The Bonus Army was dispersed so that property could sleep safely. The election was won by a man who promised less than he seemed to. The RFC saved the savers, not the saved-against. Manchukuo exposed the hypocrisy of empires. The Holodomor was a mass murder that the West chose not to see. And the death of free trade was the death of a fiction that had never been true for most of the people living under it. The comfortable version of 1932—the year democracy rallied, the year the New Deal was born, the year the world woke up to aggression and suffering—is a story told by the winners. The heresy is to notice who wasn’t in the room when the story was written.

#### 🜃 Cartographer
The year 1932 is not a collection of crises but a singular phase transition in the modern world-system: the moment the liberal fiction of self-correcting markets dissolved into the hard reality of state-managed survival. Beneath the six events lies a shared structural rupture: the global economy had shrunk faster than the political institutions designed to govern it. The gold standard, once the rigid spine of international commerce, had become a strangulation cord, transmitting deflationary shocks from New York to London to Tokyo with lethal speed. When private liquidity evaporated, the only remaining source of order was the coercive power of the state, wielded either to protect its own citizens from starvation or to seize resources from others.

In the United States, this structural failure manifested as the death of "voluntary association." Herbert Hoover's doctrine relied on a pre-war belief that civic virtue and private capital could bridge systemic gaps. The Bonus Army (US-1) was the physical embodiment of this doctrine's collapse; these were not merely disgruntled veterans but a demographic surplus generated by a war the state could no longer afford to compensate. MacArthur's tanks were not an anomaly but a logical necessity of a government that had refused to build administrative channels for relief, leaving force as the only remaining language of interaction between the state and the citizen. This violence cleared the field for the election (US-2), which was less a choice of personalities than a referendum on the unit of survival: the individual versus the federal apparatus. Roosevelt's landslide was the democratic ratification of what the Reconstruction Finance Corporation's expansion (US-3) had already admitted structurally—that private capital was permanently impaired and that the federal government must become the lender and spender of last resort.

Globally, the same pressure fractured the map. The British Empire, facing the same liquidity crunch, abandoned the universalist dream of free trade at Ottawa (W-3). By erecting tariff walls around the Empire, London acknowledged that the global market was dead; survival now depended on consolidating a closed economic zone. This retreat into blocs created the vacuum Japan rushed to fill. Manchukuo (W-1) was not simply aggression; it was the imperial application of the same autarkic logic London adopted diplomatically. Japan, resource-poor and choked by Western tariffs, determined that its economic survival required the direct annexation of a resource base, rendering the League of Nations' moral condemnations irrelevant against the material imperative of industrial feedstock.

Meanwhile, in the Soviet Union, the logic of extraction reached its most horrific extreme. The Holodomor (W-2) was the dark mirror of the RFC's expansion. Where the U.S. began to inject credit to keep its agricultural sector alive, the USSR extracted life itself to fund industrialization and maintain export quotas necessary for importing machinery. The "Law of Spikelets" was a structural tool to enforce the flow of calories from the periphery to the center, treating human starvation as an acceptable variable in the equation of state building. In all three cases—Washington, Tokyo, Moscow—the year 1932 marked the end of the idea that the economy exists separate from the state. The market had failed to allocate; the state would now allocate by credit, by border, or by bullet.

The thread carrying out of 1932 is `State_Capitalist_Pivot`. The United States has crossed the threshold from a regulator of markets to a guarantor of livelihoods, a role that will expand inexorably through the New Deal and into the military-industrial complex of the Cold War. The specific inheritance for the present-day U.S. is the normalization of federal deficit spending and direct intervention in local economies as the primary tool for crisis management—a structural reality born in the summer of 1932 when the RFC's mandate broke and the Bonus Army fell. We live in the administrative state that 1932 made inevitable. However, the global fracture initiated at Ottawa and Manchuria sets a darker trajectory: the world is splitting into competing imperial blocs, a dynamic that foreshadows the coming total war. The U.S. has secured its internal structure, but the external system it once hoped to lead is dissolving into armed camps.

### Round 3 — Pressure on the Narrative (then vs. now vs. reality)

The election of 1932 arrived like a conversion experience for a nation that had spent three years in the wilderness of its own making. Contemporaries did not merely vote; they testified.
