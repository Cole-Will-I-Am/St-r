## 0. Context Carried Forward

- **Thread — `total-war-industrial`:** The deflationary spiral has bottomed out but not reversed; with commodity prices collapsed, the "welfare capitalism" peace is fracturing as employers slash wages to match shrinking margins, turning the fragile 1920 truce into open conflict over who bears the cost of the transition from war production.
- **Thread — `red-scare-feedback`:** The retreat from federal raids has calcified into a legislative strategy; with the Department of Justice dormant, the drive to exclude radicalism shifts entirely to the border, where quota systems are being drafted to surgically remove the demographic sources of dissent rather than prosecuting individuals.
- **Thread — `imperial-fracture`:** The British mandate in Iraq has ignited a full-scale revolt that threatens to bankrupt the Exchequer; London is forced to choose between deploying massive conventional forces or adopting a cheaper, technologically mediated form of control (air power) to police the fractured Ottoman territories.
- **Thread — `racial-caste-reinforcement`:** The geographic enclosure of Black populations in northern cities is now generating political friction; as the demographic footprint solidifies, the "color line" moves from a housing issue to a voting bloc issue, triggering a violent white backlash aimed at freezing Black political agency before it can mature.
- **Thread — `unfinished-armistice`:** The dissolution of official *Freikorps* units has successfully driven the vanguard of German nationalism into the shadows; these underground cells are now coalescing around a single party structure, transforming from disparate paramilitaries into a cohesive political weapon waiting for a catalyst.
- **Thread — `soviet-containment`:** With the western border secured by Poland, the Kremlin's pivot to the New Economic Policy (NEP) is underway, signaling a temporary ceasefire with global capitalism; this creates a paradoxical window where the USSR seeks trade recognition even as the Comintern quietly funds subversion abroad.

## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — Emergency Quota Act Signed (May 19, 1921)
- **What happened (fact):** President Harding signs the first federal law establishing numerical limits on immigration based on national origin, slashing entry from Southern and Eastern Europe by roughly 50%.
- **The narrative *then*:** America was healing itself. The law fulfilled a wartime promise to "Americanize" the nation, protecting native workers from the "alien tide" that had flooded industrial cities. Restrictionists spoke of "scientific" immigration policy—eugenic language permeated congressional debate. The Johnson Quota Act (its companion still pending) would perfect the system. This was progressive reform in service of national integrity, not prejudice.
- **The narrative *now*:** The 1924 Johnson-Reed Act receives the dark spotlight; 1921 appears as its rehearsal, a "first step" toward the racialized immigration regime that would exclude Jews fleeing Hitler. The Emergency Quota Act enters textbooks as an early expression of American nativism, sometimes with the eugenic rhetoric noted, more often as bureaucratic prelude. The "narrative of inevitability"—that restriction was coming, that the numbers demanded it—still haunts mainstream accounts.
- **Why it earns a slot:** It is the moment federal immigration policy becomes explicitly racial arithmetic, and the moment "American" redefines itself by blood fraction rather than territorial presence. The mythology of the melting pot cracks audibly.

#### US-2 — Tulsa Race Massacre (May 31–June 1, 1921)
- **What happened (fact):** A white mob, deputized by city officials, destroys the prosperous Black district of Greenwood using private aircraft and fire, killing hundreds and erasing $2M in Black wealth without federal intervention.
- **The narrative *then*:** In Tulsa, a "race riot"—the term deployed immediately—occurred when "Negro agitators" threatened white citizens. The local Tribune's sensationalized account of a Black teenager's alleged assault on a white elevator operator provided the spark; the conflagration that followed was regrettable, perhaps, but the restoration of order justified the means. Greenwood's prosperity had been "uppity"; its destruction, a warning. Black Tulsans who spoke of massacre were ignored or threatened. The story vanished from Oklahoma schoolbooks within a decade.
- **The narrative *now*:** "Tulsa" has become a proper noun of national reckoning, the single most cited event in conversations about Black economic destruction and white supremacist violence. The 1921 massacre anchors arguments for reparations, for the constructed nature of the racial wealth gap, for the complicity of municipal governance in pogrom. The 2021 centennial—presidential visit, HBO dramatization, curriculum mandates—marks its transformation from suppressed memory to obligatory reference. The "riot" framing survives only in scare quotes.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The most dramatic instance of narrative burial and exhumation in American history. The story of Greenwood's erasure and its return demonstrates how completely a society can un-know what it has done, and how political necessity can restore memory as weapon or balm.

#### US-3 — Conference on Unemployment Convened (September 26, 1921)
- **What happened (fact):** Secretary of Commerce Hoover gathers industry leaders to coordinate voluntary wage maintenance and public works, institutionalizing the idea that economic stability is a federal concern despite laissez-faire rhetoric.
- **The narrative *then*:** Herbert Hoover, the "Great Engineer," was demonstrating American genius—voluntarism over European statism. Businessmen meeting in Washington to pledge wage maintenance and planned hiring: this was the associative state, the marriage of efficiency and philanthropy. Harding's address to the conference condemned government dole as "the dope of economic debauchery." The story was of capitalism saving itself through enlightened self-interest, with Hoover as its prophet.
- **The narrative *now*:** The 1921 conference appears as prelude to Hoover's 1929-1932 failures, evidence that voluntarism could not scale. Historians of the administrative state note it as an early federal intervention, a way-station between Wilsonian War Industries Board and New Deal planning. The dominant narrative, however, subordinates 1921 to 1929; the conference itself rarely earns independent mention. When it does, it illustrates Hoover's tragically persistent faith in private coordination.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The gap between then-heroism and now-irony is precisely the Herald's terrain. Hoover's self-conception as modernizer, and its subsequent demolition, reveals how quickly functional myths become cautionary tales when the material world refuses them.

#### W-1 — Iraqi Revolt Suppressed via Air Power (August 1921)
- **What happened (fact):** Britain ends the costly ground campaign in Iraq by installing Faisal I as king and relying primarily on Royal Air Force bombing campaigns to pacify tribal insurgencies, setting a precedent for colonial policing.
- **The narrative *then*:** Air power had arrived as civilization's gift to empire. Trenchard's RAF could police vast territories from above, replacing costly infantry with "moral effect"—the threat of destruction from an unreachable enemy. Faisal's installation satisfied Arab nationalist sentiment (he was, after all, a Hashemite, nearly one of them). The revolt was tribal backwardness; air control, modernity's firm but fair hand. Churchill, as Colonial Secretary, spoke of "experimental" governance. The British public heard of pacification, not bombardment of villages.
- **The narrative *now*:** The Iraq precedent enters discourse primarily through two channels: military historians tracing drone warfare's genealogy, and post-2003 commentators noting grim historical rhyme. The "45-minute claim" and "shock and awe" inevitably invoke 1921. The narrative of "air policing" as humane innovation has been thoroughly dismantled; what survives is the revelation that imperial powers always find technologies to reduce the human cost of domination—to themselves. Faisal's puppet monarchy is understood as stage-setting for 1958's revolution.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The most direct line from 1921 to American present: the technological fantasy of clean imperial violence, and its repeated disenchantment. The myth of air power as substitute for politics regenerates across a century.

#### W-2 — New Economic Policy Decreed (March 21, 1921)
- **What happened (fact):** Lenin replaces War Communism with the NEP at the 10th Party Congress, legalizing small-scale private trade and agriculture to prevent total economic collapse and peasant uprising in the Soviet Union.
- **The narrative *then*:** Two stories competed. In Moscow, the NEP was "strategic retreat"—Lenin's own phrase, weaponized by factional opponents. To the party faithful, it smelled of betrayal, of kulak accommodation, of the revolution consuming its children. To Western observers, it was confirmation: Bolshevism had failed, was reverting to capitalism, would surely collapse. The New York Times reported "Russia Returns to Private Enterprise." Both narratives agreed on one point: this was aberration, not evolution. The true revolution awaited its resumption.
- **The narrative *now*:** The NEP occupies ambiguous space. In Cold War historiography, it was the road not taken—Bukharin's "socialism at a snail's pace," crushed by Stalin's forced collectivization. Post-1991, some Russian liberals mourned it as missed opportunity. Contemporary left discourse treats it cautiously: a necessary compromise, perhaps, but one that seeded the NEPmen class and Stalin's later justification for terror. The dominant Western narrative, however, simply forgets it; 1921 means Tulsa, or perhaps the Washington Naval Conference (not even in our six), not Lenin's concession to the peasant.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The NEP reveals the fragility of revolutionary narrative: the moment when the story of inevitable proletarian triumph required open revision, and when the revolution's survival depended on abandoning its advertised principles. The myth of consistency—so necessary to both Soviet and anti-Soviet accounts—cracks here.

#### W-3 — Anglo-Irish Treaty Signed (December 6, 1921)
- **What happened (fact):** After months of guerrilla warfare, Britain grants dominion status to 26 Irish counties while retaining Ulster, formally fracturing the island and proving the brittleness of imperial cohesion in the face of sustained asymmetric war.
- **The narrative *then*:** In London, the treaty was "settlement," the end of a tiresome Irish nuisance, proof that empire could reform without dissolving. The dominion status offered—equal to Canada's, in theory—was generous, almost reckless. In Dublin, the narrative split instantly: Collins and the delegates had won "the freedom to achieve freedom"; de Valera and the republican hardliners saw poison, partition, the betrayal of 1916's martyrs. The Dáil debate of December 1921-January 1922 was a battle over which story would prevail: negotiated liberation or mutilated republic.
- **The narrative *now*:** The treaty is the origin point of two Irelands, with all subsequent violence—Civil War, Troubles, Good Friday Agreement's uneasy equilibrium—traceable to December 6. In British memory, it figures marginally: empire's reluctant contraction, one of many. In Irish memory, it remains fiercely contested, the wound that Collins's assassination prevented from healing. American Irish nationalism, long sustained by 1916's martyrology, has only recently begun engaging the treaty's complexity. The "troubles" narrative of eternal sectarian hatred obscures the treaty's specific colonial arithmetic: the six counties retained for industrial and Protestant interest.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The treaty's signing demonstrates how peace conferences generate not closure but competing mythologies, and how the "solution" to asymmetric war—partition—stores violence for future generations. The story of 1921 as ending is itself the myth that enables the next round.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected because they collectively map the transition from the chaotic violence of the immediate post-war years to the structured, systemic constraints of the interwar period: the Quota Act and Tulsa Massacre reveal how the U.S. replaced judicial repression with legislative exclusion and extralegal terror to manage demographic anxiety; Hoover's conference marks the birth of technocratic management attempting to smooth the rough edges of industrial capitalism without altering its base. Globally, the use of air power in Iraq and the Anglo-Irish Treaty demonstrate empire adapting to solvency crises by outsourcing violence or conceding territory, while the NEP shows the Soviet state surviving only by temporarily embracing the market it sought to destroy. I deliberately omitted the Washington Naval Conference (late 1921/early 1922) because its effects are diplomatic and lagged; the selected events show the *immediate* structural hardening of borders, race relations, and economic management that defined the year's lived reality.

## 2. Connections Within the Year

- **U.S. ↔ World:** The logic of exclusion in the Emergency Quota Act mirrors the logic of imperial fragmentation in the Anglo-Irish Treaty and the tactical containment of the Iraqi Revolt; as global empires fracture under the weight of asymmetric resistance and demographic complexity, the United States preemptively seals its borders to prevent the "contagion" of these destabilizing populations from entering its own industrial core.
- **Event ↔ Event:** The destruction of Black wealth in Tulsa (US-2) and the suppression of the Iraqi Revolt via air power (W-1) share a operational methodology: the use of overwhelming, technologically superior force (deputized mobs/RAF bombers) to liquidate localized concentrations of autonomy that threaten the established racial-imperial order, proving that "order" in 1921 is maintained not by law, but by the capacity for rapid, total annihilation of dissenting spaces.
- **Invisible / structural (shared hidden drivers):** The crisis of post-war demobilization drives both the NEP's retreat from pure ideology (W-2) and Hoover's Conference on Unemployment (US-3); in both cases, the rigid structures of war-time command or laissez-faire dogma collapse under the pressure of mass surplus labor and production gluts, forcing a pragmatic, hybrid stabilization where the state quietly manages capitalism to prevent revolutionary overthrow.

## 3. The Debate

### Round 1 — Opening Interpretations (uninterrupted)

#### 🜂 Herald
1921 is the year the modern world learns to tell stories about its own violence that permit the violence to continue. Not denial, precisely—something more sophisticated. The quota act is "scientific"; the Tulsa destruction is a "riot"; air control is "pacification"; the NEP is "retreat"; the Irish treaty is "settlement." Each narrative performs the same operation: it names a wound as healing, a rupture as continuity, a defeat as deferred victory. This is the Herald's terrain at its most exposed.

Consider the American case first, for here the mythology of 1921 most directly nourishes our present. The Emergency Quota Act arrives in a nation already narrating itself through the Great War's supposed clarifications. The "100% Americanism" of 1917-1918 had required enemies: German language, Bolshevik sympathy, hyphenated identity. By 1921, these enemies are being institutionalized into numerical formulas—3% of the 1910 foreign-born, the census as destiny. The narrative then: this is measurement, not prejudice; the "national origins" system (perfected in 1924) will restore demographic balance. The eugenicists who testified before Congress—Harry Laughlin, Madison Grant—spoke as experts, not bigots. The belief was genuinely held, or genuinely performed, by millions who understood themselves as protecting a republican experiment from degenerative overload.

What we inherit is the quota system's afterlife in the 1965 Immigration and Nationality Act's supposed repudiation, and in the ongoing battles over "merit-based" immigration that reproduce national-origin logic in skills-based language. The myth of 1921 as aberration—pre-Hitlerian excess, corrected by postwar liberalism—obscures the deeper continuity: the American nation as an ethnos requiring protection, a story that 1921 fortified and that persists in "replacement" rhetoric a century later. The Herald's obligation: to feel the sincerity of the restrictionists' narrative without letting that sincerity excuse its function. They believed they were saving America. They were constructing an America that required saving from its own pluralism.

Tulsa operates differently—there the narrative was not maintained but buried, which is itself a narrative technology. The Greenwood massacre's erasure from Oklahoma textbooks, from national memory, from even Black American familial transmission in many cases, required active institutional work: police records destroyed, newspaper archives purged, survivors intimidated into silence. The "riot" framing served immediate legal purposes (insurance companies denied claims to "riot"-damaged properties) but also a longer mythological one: American race relations as tragic misunderstanding rather than systematic expropriation. The 2021 resurrection of Tulsa as national trauma—Biden's visit, "Watchmen" and "Lovecraft Country"—represents not simple truth-recovery but narrative competition. The new story of Tulsa serves specific political functions: reparations claims, critical race theory curricula, Democratic coalition management. The Herald must ask: what does this society need from Tulsa's memory now, and what does that need conceal? The erasure of 1921-2000 and the hypervisibility of 2021 are symmetrical operations, both serving present power.

Hoover's unemployment conference reveals the American liberal tradition's most durable myth: the volunatrist fix, the meeting that substitutes for structure, the "public-private partnership" as perpetual deferral of redistribution. Hoover in 1921 was already the Great Engineer, the man who fed Belgium, who would make capitalism work without capitalism's cruelty. The narrative of administrative competence—later applied to Robert McNamara, to the Clinton policy wonks, to the Obama technocrats—finds its template here. The 1921 conference's actual results were negligible; the mythology of enlightened business leadership, immense. We live still in the gap between Hoover's promise and his 1932 desolation, repeatedly refilling it with new "data-driven" leadership that will finally make voluntarism work.

Turning to the world events, the Iraqi air policing precedent matters most for American inheritance because it established the technological fantasy that persists in our drone warfare doctrine. Trenchard's RAF, Churchill's enthusiasm for "experimental" control from above—these created the narrative template: imperial violence can be made precise, moral, even humanitarian. The British public of 1921 received stories of pacified tribes, not of bombed villages; the "moral effect" of air power was its threat, its use supposedly rare. We recognize this structure: "surgical strikes," "collateral damage" as administrative category, the drone pilot's commute from suburban Nevada. The 2003 Iraq invasion's rhetorical dependence on "shock and awe"—the fantasy of rapid technological dominance replacing political engagement—descends directly from 1921's narrative innovation. The Herald's task: to trace how imperial powers consistently discover that their new technologies finally solve the problem of restive populations, and how this discovery consistently fails, and how the narrative nonetheless persists because it serves the structural need to maintain empire without conscript armies.

Lenin's NEP presents the revolutionary myth in crisis. The Bolshevik story of 1917—inevitable proletarian triumph, scientific socialism's vindication—required War Communism as its authentic expression. The peasant revolts of 1920-21, the Kronstadt rebellion, the economic collapse: these threatened not merely policy but narrative coherence. The NEP's legalization of private trade was, in this sense, a more profound crisis than any military defeat. The party congress debates of March 1921 reveal Bolsheviks arguing over whether the revolution could survive its own survival. The narrative then: retreat in service of advance, dialectical wisdom. The narrative now, in our post-Soviet perspective: the moment when revolutionary violence's inability to produce its promised world became undeniable, requiring either honest abandonment (impossible for the regime) or intensified terror (Stalin's solution). The NEP's memory in contemporary American left discourse is cautionary: the revolution that compromises with peasants and traders may survive, but only by becoming something other than itself. The myth of "actually existing socialism" as betrayal of authentic Marxism—a myth that served both Cold War liberals and Trotskyist sects—finds its origin here.

The Anglo-Irish Treaty generates competing myths that still structure Irish-American identity and, through it, American understandings of anticolonial struggle. Collins's "freedom to achieve freedom" versus de Valera's "external association": these were not merely policy

#### 🜄 Heretic
The Herald has done something characteristic and useful: it has named the operation. Each event arrives wrapped in a story that converts violence into procedure, rupture into adjustment. But naming the operation is not the same as following the money, the guns, the land titles, and the silences to their terminus. The Herald’s account risks becoming a story about stories—a hall of narrative mirrors—when 1921 demands we look at who was left holding the deed and who was left in the ground.

Steelman the official version, as required. The Emergency Quota Act was a democratic response to native-born labor’s anxiety and a legitimate exercise of sovereignty; nations have always controlled their borders. The Tulsa massacre was a tragic eruption of mob violence that decent people deplored and that the state of Oklahoma, constrained by federalism, could not have anticipated. Hoover’s unemployment conference was a genuine attempt to stabilize a volatile economy through enlightened voluntarism. Britain’s air policing in Iraq was a cost-saving measure that minimized British casualties and brought order to restive tribes. The NEP was a tactical retreat that saved the revolution from its own excesses. The Anglo-Irish Treaty was a hard-won compromise that gave Ireland what it had demanded for decades: self-government. These are not lies. They are the stories that permitted the winners to sleep.

Now press.

**Cui bono—who gained from the event AND from the story told about it?**

The Quota Act’s beneficiaries were not abstract “American workers.” The Act was drafted with the explicit counsel of the American Federation of Labor, whose president Samuel Gompers had spent two decades arguing that unrestricted immigration depressed wages and radicalized the workforce. But the AFL’s leadership was itself engaged in a project of racial gatekeeping: the same unions that demanded immigration restriction were, in 1921, systematically excluding Black workers from skilled trades and using the “threat” of cheap immigrant labor to justify color bars. The story told—that restriction protected the American workingman—obscured which workingmen counted as American. The Act’s numerical formula was not neutral. It was designed by eugenicist Harry Laughlin, whose testimony before the House Committee on Immigration and Naturalization explicitly ranked nationalities by “social adequacy.” The 3% cap on 1910 foreign-born populations was chosen because the 1910 census predated the heaviest waves of Italian, Jewish, and Slavic migration. This was not measurement. This was the weaponization of the census to freeze an ethnic hierarchy into law. The gainers: northern European ethnic elites who saw their demographic dominance slipping, and the industrialists who had already extracted what they needed from the great migration of 1880-1914 and now preferred a docile, restricted labor pool to a polyglot one prone to organizing. The story that this was “science” laundered racial panic into administrative procedure. We inherit the technique, not just the policy: the use of quantitative thresholds to achieve racial ends without racial language.

Tulsa demands a harder look at the word “riot.” The term implies spontaneous, disorganized violence. What occurred in Greenwood was a military operation. The white mob was deputized by the sheriff’s office. Weapons were distributed by the National Guard. Private aircraft—a detail that should never cease to shock—were used to drop incendiary devices on Black homes and businesses. The police arrested Black men en masse, not for their protection but to disarm the community. The official story, when it was told at all, blamed a Black teenager named Dick Rowland for assaulting a white elevator operator, a charge she later recanted. But the precipitating incident is almost beside the point. Greenwood was destroyed because Greenwood existed: a prosperous, self-contained Black economy of over 10,000 people, with banks, theaters, hotels, and a level of wealth that constituted a standing refutation of Jim Crow’s premise. The massacre was not a failure of law. It was an enforcement of the racial order by means the law could not officially sanction but did officially enable. Cui bono? White Tulsa’s business class, which absorbed the commercial traffic that Greenwood had captured. The city’s political machine, which faced no federal inquiry, no prosecutions, and no restitution. The insurance companies that denied every claim under “riot exclusion” clauses, a legal maneuver that converted mass murder into an uninsurable act of God. And the story of the “riot”—chaotic, tragic, exceptional—benefited every institution that needed to deny that the United States was capable of organizing the aerial bombardment of its own citizens. That silence was not passive forgetting. It was active suppression: police records vanished, newspaper archives were scrubbed, survivors were threatened with prosecution if they spoke. The gap between word and deed here is not a gap. It is a grave.

**Hoover’s Conference on Unemployment.** The received story: a public-spirited commerce secretary convenes industry to voluntarily maintain wages and accelerate public works, demonstrating that laissez-faire could be tempered by enlightened cooperation. The reality: Hoover was managing a political crisis. The postwar depression of 1920-21 was the sharpest deflationary collapse in American history before the Great Depression. Unemployment reached perhaps 12%. The Bolshevik revolution was five years old; the Seattle general strike, the Boston police strike, the steel strike of 1919 were fresh memories. American elites were terrified of labor radicalism. Hoover’s conference was not an act of charity. It was a containment strategy. The “voluntary” wage maintenance he extracted from industry leaders was accompanied by a ferocious open-shop campaign that crushed union membership from 5 million to 3.6 million over the decade. The public works he championed were minimal, localized, and designed to preempt demands for a federal dole. The story told—that private coordination solved a public crisis—became the template for the next decade’s response to suffering: voluntarism as a substitute for redistribution. The gainers: industrialists who got credit for magnanimity while breaking unions, and a Republican Party that could claim to have solved unemployment without expanding the state. The omitted: the unemployed themselves, who had no seat at the table, and the labor movement, which was being dismantled while Hoover burnished his progressive credentials.

**Air control in Iraq.** The British narrative was one of humane efficiency. Ground campaigns in Mesopotamia had cost 40,000 lives and tens of millions of pounds. The Royal Air Force, newly independent and desperate to justify its budget, proposed a cheaper method: bombing villages that resisted colonial rule. Wing Commander Arthur Harris—later “Bomber Harris” of Dresden infamy—refined the technique: drop leaflets warning of bombardment, then destroy the village’s livestock, crops, and homes. “The Arab and Kurd now know what real bombing means,” Harris wrote. “Within 45 minutes a full-sized village can be practically wiped out and a third of its inhabitants killed or injured.” This was called “pacification.” The gainers: the RAF, which secured its institutional survival by proving it could police an empire on the cheap; the Hashemite monarchy installed as a client regime; and British oil interests, which needed a quiescent Mesopotamia. The omitted: the Iraqi dead, uncounted and uncountable, whose villages were reduced to rubble so that British pilots could practice the techniques they would later apply to Hamburg and Tokyo. The story—air power as a humane, precise instrument of order—is the direct ancestor of every drone strike rationale a century later. The colonial laboratory produced not just a policy but a doctrine: violence from the air is clean, proportionate, and does not require the consent of those beneath it.

**The New Economic Policy.** The Herald is right to call this a “retreat,” but the word is too gentle. War Communism had produced famine that killed perhaps five million people. The Kronstadt sailors, the Bolsheviks’ former revolutionary vanguard, had risen in revolt demanding “soviets without Bolsheviks” and were crushed in March 1921, the same month the NEP was announced. The NEP was not a tactical adjustment. It was a surrender to reality by a regime that had just machine-gunned its own base. The story Lenin told—that this was a temporary “breathing space,” a strategic withdrawal before the next advance—was a narrative of control imposed on a situation of collapse. Cui bono? The peasantry, briefly, who could now sell their grain on a market instead of having it requisitioned at gunpoint. The “Nepmen,” the private traders who accumulated wealth and became the scapegoats for the next decade’s purges. And the Bolshevik party itself, which survived by abandoning every economic principle it had fought a civil war to impose. The omitted: the Kronstadt dead, whose demand for free soviets and peasant markets was stolen and repackaged as Leninist wisdom after they were slaughtered. The gap between word and deed is the distance between “all power to the soviets” and the mass grave on Kotlin Island.

**The Anglo-Irish Treaty.** The British story: a magnanimous grant of self-government to a restive province, preserving the integrity of the Empire through flexibility. The Irish story, at least the pro-Treaty story: a stepping-stone to full independence, the freedom to achieve freedom. Both narratives require omitting Ulster. The six counties were not retained because of some organic Unionist sentiment that no settlement could override. They were retained because the British Conservative Party, and its Ulster Unionist allies, threatened to bring down Lloyd George’s government if the industrial north was surrendered. The boundary commission promised in the Treaty was a deliberate deception, designed to suggest that partition might be revised when both sides knew it would not. The result: a truncated Irish Free State, a gerrymandered Protestant parliament in Belfast, and the seeds of a civil war that would kill more Irish people than the War of Independence. Cui bono? The British military, which extricated itself from an unwinnable guerrilla war while retaining strategic ports. The Catholic middle class in the south, which got a state it could run. The Ulster Unionist elite, which got a permanent majority in a sectarian statelet. The omitted: the northern Catholic minority, abandoned to a regime that would define itself by their exclusion. And the Irish republican dead, who had not fought for a partitioned island with an oath of allegiance to the Crown. The Treaty debate in the Dáil was not about whether the settlement was good. It was about whether the IRA had the capacity to keep fighting. Michael Collins signed because he knew they did not. The story of the Treaty as a triumph of negotiation over violence is a story told by those who did not have to live under the regime it created.

What unites these six events is not just the Herald’s “naming operation.” It is the distribution of consequences. In every case, those who made the decisions—the restrictionist legislators, the white Tulsans, the corporate voluntarists, the RAF planners, the Bolshevik leadership, the British negotiators—did not bear the costs of their decisions. The costs fell on the excluded, the bombed, the unemployed, the colonized, the starved, the partitioned. The stories told afterward ensured that this distribution would appear natural, inevitable, or benevolent. That is the function of the official narrative: to make the suffering of the omitted seem like the price of order rather than the point of the exercise. 1921 is not the year the modern world learned to tell stories about its violence. It is the year it perfected the technique of making the victims pay for the story.

#### 🜃 Cartographer
The year 1921 is not a collection of disparate crises but a singular, global recalibration of the boundaries of belonging and control following the cataclysm of the Great War. The map of this year reveals a world contracting inward, where the primary structural imperative is the containment of entropy. The slow, heavy variables at play are the demographic shocks of migration, the technological asymmetry of violence, and the fragility of industrial capitalism when stripped of wartime demand. These forces do not merely influence events; they dictate the narrow corridor of available outcomes.

Consider first the architecture of exclusion. The Emergency Quota Act (US-1) is often narrated as a resurgence of nativism, but structurally, it is a defensive fortification of the American labor market against the collapsing peripheries of Europe. The quota system is a bureaucratic dam built against the flow of human capital displaced by the very imperial fractures seen in W-3 and W-1. As the British Empire realizes it cannot hold Ireland without partition or Iraq without aerial bombardment, the United States recognizes that it cannot absorb the human fallout of these disintegrating zones without fracturing its own racial and economic hierarchy. The Quota Act and the Anglo-Irish Treaty are twin responses to the same structural pressure: the impossibility of maintaining universalist imperial or national myths in the face of distinct, unassimilable identities demanding self-determination. The U.S. chooses to wall off; Britain chooses to slice apart. Both are admissions that the old model of seamless expansion is dead.

Beneath the surface of these political maneuvers lies a darker, more immediate mechanism of control: the weaponization of technology to enforce spatial segregation. There is a chilling resonance between the burning of Greenwood (US-2) and the bombing of Iraqi tribes (W-1). In Tulsa, the "deputized" mob utilized private aircraft to drop turpentine bombs on Black homes; in Iraq, the Royal Air Force used public aircraft to drop ordnance on tribal villages. In both instances, the state (whether local municipal authority or imperial crown) outsourced or directly deployed air power to erase a geography of autonomy. Greenwood was a node of Black economic independence that threatened the white supremacist structure of Oklahoma; the Iraqi tribes were nodes of anti-colonial resistance that threatened British oil interests. The structural lesson of 1921 is that when legal frameworks fail to suppress dissent, technology provides a shortcut to obliteration. The airplane, a symbol of modern progress, becomes the primary tool for enforcing the color line and the imperial border. This is not coincidence; it is the convergence of available technology with the imperative to maintain hierarchy.

Simultaneously, the economic engine of the world is sputtering, forcing a pragmatic retreat from ideological purity in both Washington and Moscow. The Conference on Unemployment (US-3) and the New Economic Policy (W-2) appear diametrically opposed—one within a capitalist framework, the other within a communist one. Yet, structurally, they are identical survival mechanisms. Both Lenin and Hoover confront the same reality: the post-war transition has created a surplus of labor and a collapse of demand that threatens total systemic failure.
