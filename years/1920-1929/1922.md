## 0. Context Carried Forward

- **Thread — `closed-border-regime`:** Entering 1922, the temporary emergency quotas of 1921 are functioning as a pressure valve, but the administrative machinery is already straining under the volume of excluded arrivals; the political coalition is shifting from "pause" to "permanent exclusion," preparing the legislative language that will codify the 1890 census as the baseline for national origin in 1924.
- **Thread — `aerial-policing-doctrine`:** The tactical proof-of-concept from Tulsa and Iraq has migrated into the professional journals of American police chiefs and military strategists; in 1922, this doctrine exists as an uncodified but widely accepted capability, waiting only for a specific domestic crisis to trigger its first formalized deployment against U.S. citizens under the guise of public order.
- **Thread — `managed-voluntarism`:** Hoover's associational model is holding tenuously as industrial production stabilizes post-recession, but the underlying fragility is visible in the refusal of heavy industry to commit to long-term wage floors; the system relies entirely on continued profitability, creating a false sense of security that masks the absence of any statutory buffer against the next contraction.
- **Thread — `wealth-destruction-feedback`:** The physical rubble of Greenwood has been cleared, but the legal and financial erasure is total; in 1922, insurance companies and local courts are actively finalizing the denial of claims, transforming the massacre from a violent event into a permanent structural fact that locks Black capital in Tulsa—and by extension, nationally—outside the protections of property law.
- **Thread — `imperial-cost-shifting`:** Britain's Royal Air Force continues to govern Iraq through bombardment, successfully keeping fiscal costs low for London while intensifying tribal resistance on the ground; this "cheap empire" model is now being studied by other colonial powers as a viable alternative to expensive ground garrisons, exporting the logic that air power can substitute for political consent.

## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — The Supreme Court Validates Segregated Transit (November 1922)
- **What happened (fact):** Lower federal courts and the Supreme Court's docket trend toward affirming segregation in interstate transportation contexts; *Gong Lum v. Rice* (1927) will later crystallize this trajectory, but 1922 marks the judicial consolidation of *Plessy*'s "separate but equal" doctrine across expanding domains of public life.
- **The narrative *then*:** For white Southern elites and their Northern allies, this is federalism restored—the judiciary finally deferring to local custom after the overreach of Reconstruction. For Black Americans and their white allies, it is the betrayal of a constitutional promise, the courtroom door closing on the Fourteenth Amendment's meaning. The NAACP, still young, files briefs in desperation; the Crisis editorializes that the Court has "killed the spirit while preserving the letter."
- **The narrative *now*:** A waypoint in the long "strange career" of Jim Crow, often telescoped into a single villainous Court or flattened into the 1954 triumph of *Brown*. We inherit the story of judicial abdication, but rarely linger on the *felt* suffocation of 1922—the particular despair of watching rights erode not with a bang but with a docket, with silence, with the steady accumulation of precedents that make dissent seem quixotic.
- **Why it earns a slot:** It is the architecture of American apartheid being poured in concrete, the moment when the judiciary's role as potential protector is foreclosed for a generation. Without 1922's quiet consolidation, there is no 1954 to dramatize.

#### US-2 — The Fordney-McCumber Tariff Enactment (September 21, 1922)
- **What happened (fact):** President Harding signs legislation raising average import duties to approximately 38.5%, the highest rates in American history to that point, with flexible provisions allowing the Tariff Commission to recommend further increases.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Midwestern Republicans and the farm bloc, this is economic nationalism as justice—protecting the American producer from European dumping, honoring the wartime sacrifice of domestic industry. For internationalists and the minority Democratic opposition, it is economic suicide dressed as patriotism, a repudiation of Wilsonian visions of open markets and collective security. Secretary of Commerce Herbert Hoover, not yet tarred with the Depression, engineers the flexible provisions as technocratic compromise.
- **The narrative *now*:** A precursor to Smoot-Hawley and the Depression's trade wars, often read as Republican folly or as the structural expression of American creditor imperialism—forcing loans on Europe, then denying the markets to repay them. We inherit the story of interwar economic nationalism, but rarely feel the *hope* of 1922, the genuine belief that walls could be prosperity's foundation.
- **Why it earns a slot:** It is the materialization of American withdrawal from the world economy it had just helped remake, the policy choice that makes the 1929 crash global rather than merely American.

#### US-3 — The Formation of the American Civil Liberties Union (Permanent Body) (January 1922)
- **What happened (fact):** Roger Baldwin formally incorporates the ACLU as a permanent organization, transitioning from the wartime National Civil Liberties Bureau and establishing institutional continuity for civil liberties litigation and advocacy.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Baldwin and the surviving radicals of the wartime repression, this is the long defense beginning—the recognition that Palmer's raids were not aberration but preview, that the modern state's capacity for surveillance and deportation required permanent institutional counterweight. For mainstream opinion, the ACLU remains suspect, "aiding" Bolshevism by defending its speakers; the organization's early cases—defending anti-war pamphleteers, striking miners, birth-control advocates—place it outside respectable opinion.
- **The narrative *now*:** The origin story of American civil libertarianism, often heroically narrated as the founding of a secular church of rights. We inherit the ACLU as establishment institution, forgetting the radical contingency of its birth, the genuine courage of defending unpopular speech when "civil liberties" had not yet become a consensus value.
- **Why it earns a slot:** It is the institutionalization of dissent itself, the creation of a permanent infrastructure for rights-claiming that will outlast the emergencies that provoke it—and that will, in time, be accused of the very power it was founded to resist.

#### W-1 — The March on Rome and Mussolini's Ascension (October 27-29, 1922)
- **What happened (fact):** Approximately 30,000 Fascist squadristi converge on Rome; King Victor Emmanuel III declines to declare martial law and instead invites Mussolini to form a government, making him at 39 the youngest prime minister in Italian history.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Fascists, the "march" is revolution by other means, the violent cleansing of a decadent liberal order, the restoration of Roman virility. For Italian liberals and socialists, it is catastrophe dressed in black shirts, the surrender of constitutional government to theatrical brutality. For foreign observers, it is ambiguous—Churchill calls Mussolini the "Roman genius," American papers treat it as Italian melodrama, the *New York Times* reassures readers that "the King remains sovereign." The paramilitary coup is not yet a template; it is a local Italian strangeness.
- **The narrative *now*:** The prototype of fascism's seizure of power, the moment that transforms "fascist" from description to accusation across a century. We inherit the story as warning, but also as anachronism—reading backward the full horror of Auschwitz, forgetting that in 1922 Mussolini was not yet Hitler's precursor but a novel phenomenon, his "totalitarianism" not yet named.
- **Why it earns a slot:** It is the demonstration that constitutional forms can be hollowed from within by mobilized violence, the script that will be studied, adapted, and surpassed across the century.

#### W-2 — The Conclusion of the Irish Civil War (May 24, 1922)
- **What happened (fact):** The anti-Treaty IRA, led by Liam Lynch until his death and then Frank Aiken, orders a ceasefire ending major combat operations; the Irish Free State has already been established under the Treaty with Britain, formalizing partition.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Free State supporters, this is the bitter completion of independence—the acceptance of imperfect sovereignty over continued colonial subjugation. For anti-Treaty republicans, it is betrayal consecrated, the abandonment of the 1916 martyrs' dream of the full thirty-two-county republic. For British policymakers, it is imperial management succeeding where repression failed: the empire has learned to divide, to co-opt, to let former subjects govern themselves within the Commonwealth. The Dominion model is vindicated.
- **The narrative *now*:** The origin of partition's permanence, the moment that makes Northern Ireland's Troubles inevitable and the 1998 Good Friday Agreement possible. We inherit the story as tragedy of fratricide, but rarely confront the British Empire's adaptive genius—its capacity to absorb nationalist energy by offering partial sovereignty, a technique that will be repeated across the decolonizing century.
- **Why it earns a slot:** It is the template for managed decolonization, the demonstration that empires can concede form while preserving substance, and that the most devastating wars are often between those who share a cause.

#### W-3 — The Lausanne Conference Begins (November 1922/Switzerland)
- **What happened (fact):** Allied powers and the Turkish nationalist government under Mustafa Kemal convene to negotiate replacement of the unenforced Treaty of Sèvres, ultimately producing the 1923 Treaty of Lausanne that recognizes Turkish sovereignty over Anatolia and eastern Thrace, with internationalized straits and population exchange provisions.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Turkish nationalists, this is the vindication of armed resistance, the proof that the "sick man of Europe" could rise and reclaim his house. For Britain and France, it is pragmatic retreat dressed as magnanimity, the acknowledgment that Greek irredentism and Kurdish aspiration are prices worth paying for regional stability. For the Greeks of Asia Minor, the Pontic Greeks, it is the beginning of a forced migration that will kill hundreds of thousands; for Armenians, the confirmation that genocide's perpetrators will negotiate as equals. The League of Nations mandate system is exposed as cover for imperial rearrangement.
- **The narrative *now*:** The foundational settlement of modern Turkey and the Middle East's post-Ottoman order, increasingly questioned as the "population exchange" is recognized as ethnic cleansing legitimized by international law. We inherit the story as statecraft's realism, but the 1923 treaty's population provisions have become precedent for "humanitarian" transfer, the dark logic of homogenizing nations.
- **Why it earns a slot:** It is the moment when the postwar settlement is rewritten by force, when a non-European nationalist movement successfully defies the Great Powers, and when the international community learns to codify atrocity as population management.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected to expose the structural hardening of boundaries—national, economic, and legal—that defines 1922 as a year of closure rather than renewal. The Fordney-McCumber Tariff and the March on Rome are paired to show how economic protectionism and political fascism share a root in the rejection of liberal internationalism; similarly, the ACLU's formation is a direct structural response to the `aerial-policing-doctrine` and domestic red scare, institutionalizing the conflict between state security and individual rights. I omitted the discovery of insulin and the publication of *The Waste Land*; while culturally and medically monumental, they obscure the specific geopolitical and legal architectures being built this year that directly constrain the future U.S. trajectory. The focus here is on the walls going up, not the cures or poems emerging despite them.

## 2. Connections Within the Year

- **U.S. ↔ World:** The Fordney-McCumber Tariff (US-2) and the Lausanne Conference (W-3) represent a synchronized global retreat from the liberal internationalism of 1919; as the U.S. erects an economic fortress that starves Europe of the dollar liquidity needed for reparations, the Allied powers simultaneously abandon the punitive geopolitical architecture of Sèvres in favor of pragmatic recognition of nationalist force (Turkey), signaling that both economics and diplomacy have shifted from collective security to unilateral consolidation.
- **Event ↔ Event:** The institutionalization of the ACLU (US-3) and the validation of segregated transit (US-1) are not contradictory but complementary responses to the same structural anxiety: as the state expands its capacity for social control (echoing the Fascist ascent in W-1), it simultaneously codifies the boundaries of who is protected by law and who is excluded, creating a dual track where "liberty" is professionalized for the dissenting citizen while "order" is legally entrenched for the racialized subject.
- **Invisible / structural (shared hidden drivers):** The collapse of the post-war imperial equilibrium is driven by the friction between static political borders and dynamic demographic/industrial realities; whether in the partition of Ireland (W-2), the renegotiation of Turkey's borders (W-3), or the rigidification of the U.S. color line (US-1), the year 1922 reveals a global imperative to stabilize fractured nations through exclusionary definitions of belonging and hardening administrative barriers against fluid movement.

## 3. The Debate

### Round 1 — Opening Interpretations (uninterrupted)

#### 🜂 Herald
1922 is the year the twentieth century learns to tell lies about itself, and to believe them.

Consider the simultaneity: in Rome, blackshirts stage a coup that will be remembered as a "march," as if revolution were a parade; in Washington, Congress builds an economic fortress and calls it prosperity; in Dublin, brothers finish killing each other and call it peace; in Lausanne, diplomats legitimize ethnic cleansing and call it exchange. The ACLU forms to defend truth against power, and is immediately suspected of serving power's enemies. The Supreme Court consolidates apartheid and calls it constitutional fidelity. Everywhere, the story outraces the fact.

The narrative *then* is one of exhaustion dressed as resolution. The Great War's survivors are desperate for stories that make sense: the strong leader who cuts through parliamentary deadlock, the protected home market that rewards sacrifice, the imperfect nation that is still a nation. Mussolini's Italy fascinates precisely because it offers narrative coherence—history as will, society as organism, the individual dissolved into collective purpose. American observers do not yet hear "fascist" as terminal accusation; they hear modernity's tempo, the efficiency that Ford's assembly lines promised now applied to politics. The *New Republic* debates whether Mussolini has discovered "a new technique of government"; Sinclair Lewis's *Babbitt* (published this year) satirizes the American businessman who would admire him.

The Fordney-McCumber tariff participates in the same narrative hunger: the world economy is too complex, too threatening, too full of European debt and Bolshevik contagion. The tariff is a story about self-sufficiency that Americans already know from frontier mythology, now applied to global capital flows. That it will help destroy the very stability it claims to protect is invisible from inside 1922's belief; the story feels like control.

The Irish settlement and Lausanne share a deeper narrative architecture: the empire's story about itself as reluctant modernizer, gracious in concession. The British learned between 1916 and 1922 that direct repression of nationalism costs more than managed devolution. The Free State is the model—Dominion status, the Crown lingering in formula, the substance of independence diluted by partition. In Turkey, Kemal forces the revision: Sèvres is torn up, but the great powers save face by internationalizing the Dardanelles, by blessing the "exchange" of populations that accomplishes what genocide began. The story the diplomats tell is of sensible compromise; the story beneath is of force re-legitimized, of the Wilsonian moment's collapse into realpolitik.

The ACLU's formation is the counter-narrative struggling to be born, and struggling is the operative word. Roger Baldwin's radical pacifism, his willingness to defend wartime dissenters and Wobblies and birth-control advocates, places the ACLU outside the American mainstream of 1922. The organization exists because the Palmer Raids proved that emergency has become permanent, that the wartime security apparatus would outlast its nominal cause. But the civil libertarian story—rights as transcendent, speech as sacred—is not yet the American creed it will become; in 1922, it is still the dissenters' faith, fragile and suspect.

And *Gong Lum*'s shadow—*Plessy*'s consolidation—is where the American narrative most painfully betrays itself. The story then, for white Americans, is of sectional reconciliation finally achieved, of the race problem managed by local wisdom, of the Supreme Court as wise restraint. The NAACP knows better; W.E.B. Du Bois writes in 1922 that "the American Negro" has been "returned to slavery by the silent consent of the nation." The narrative now cannot afford to feel this fully, because to feel it is to recognize that the 1954 of *Brown v. Board* is not redemption but the breaking of a dam that 1922's judges deliberately constructed.

What we inherit from 1922 is the century's characteristic narrative form: the managed contradiction, the story that contains its own opposite. We call Mussolini's seizure "fascism" and thus contain it as past pathology, while forgetting that its techniques—paramilitary spectacle, leader cult, the aestheticization of politics—became available to all politics. We call Fordney-McCumber protectionism and thus dismiss it as economic error, while living in its structural legacy: the American economy as autonomous fortress, the recurrent fantasy that walls generate prosperity. We celebrate the ACLU's founding as origin story, while the organization itself struggles with whether its founding radicalism can survive its institutional success.

The deepest inheritance is methodological: 1922 teaches us to watch how stories harden, how the contingent becomes inevitable in retrospect, how the violence of the present is laundered into the stability of the past. The Irish Civil War's ceasefire becomes "the birth of modern Ireland"; Lausanne's population exchange becomes "the foundation of Turkish secularism"; *Plessy*'s extension becomes "the context for *Brown*." These are not false stories. They are partial stories that have displaced the lived experience of their making—the fear, the betrayal, the hope that each choice might turn otherwise.

1922 is the year when the twentieth century's myths achieved their provisional forms, not yet tested by the greater catastrophes ahead. To read it from inside is to feel the seduction of these stories in their first telling, before they became alibis. To read it from outside is to trace how belief becomes architecture, how the tales we tell about ourselves become the prisons we mistake for foundations.

#### 🜄 Heretic
Steelman first, because the obligation is real. The year 1922 can be read as the moment the liberal order, battered by war, revolution, and economic chaos, begins to rebuild itself on firmer, more realistic foundations. The ACLU’s incorporation represents the maturation of American civil society—a permanent watchdog ensuring that the wartime security state does not become a permanent condition. The Fordney-McCumber tariff, however misguided in retrospect, was a democratic response to a devastated agricultural sector and a war-weary electorate demanding protection from European entanglements. In Italy, Mussolini’s assumption of power through constitutional invitation—the King acting within his prerogative—seemed to foreclose the far worse possibility of Bolshevik revolution, a judgment many respectable Anglo-American observers shared. The Irish ceasefire ended a fratricidal bloodletting and, however painfully, gave the Free State a chance at functional sovereignty. At Lausanne, the powers finally conceded what force had already decided: that the Treaty of Sèvres was a colonial fantasy, and that a stable Turkey required acknowledging its borders. Even the Supreme Court’s steady erosion of Reconstruction’s promise could be framed as a tragic but prudent accommodation to regional realities, preventing the kind of federal occupation that had proven unsustainable.

That is the story as the powerful told it, and as their heirs still prefer it. Now take it apart.

Begin with the ACLU, because its formation is the most seductive narrative of the six—and the most useful to power precisely because it *looks* like opposition. Roger Baldwin incorporates a permanent organization to defend civil liberties. Whose liberties? The ACLU’s founding board is a roll call of Progressive-era elites: Jane Addams, Felix Frankfurter, Norman Thomas. Its immediate targets are the Palmer Raids’ excesses against immigrants, labor organizers, and leftists. But note the structure: the ACLU is incorporated as a *legal* defense organization, not a mass movement. Its model is the courtroom, not the picket line. This channels dissent into procedural channels that the state can manage. The radical is transformed into the client; the systemic critique becomes a billable case. The state’s security apparatus is not dismantled—it is merely given a permanent, domesticated opposition that legitimizes the very legal order it pretends to challenge. The Bureau of Investigation (not yet the FBI) continues its surveillance of Black communities, labor unions, and immigrant groups. The ACLU will defend some of them, selectively, and in doing so will help define the acceptable boundaries of dissent. The real omission: the ACLU in 1922 has virtually nothing to say about the Jim Crow terror regime consolidating across the South. Lynchings continue; the NAACP’s anti-lynching campaign is in full swing, but the new civil liberties organization—headquartered in New York, staffed by white progressives—does not make racial apartheid its central crisis. The silence is the evidence. Civil liberties for whom?

Now the Fordney-McCumber tariff. The official story is that it protects American farmers and manufacturers from a flood of cheap postwar European goods. The reality: it is a machine for transferring wealth from consumers and debtors to concentrated industrial capital. Average duties rise to 38 percent, but the real action is in the scientific tariff provisions, which empower the President to adjust rates based on the “cost of production”—a formula that, in practice, hands tariff-setting to the very industries being protected. The sugar beet lobby, the chemical trusts, the infant automobile oligopoly: they write their own protections. Meanwhile, the tariff walls ensure that European nations cannot earn the dollars necessary to repay their war debts to the United States. This is not an accident or a miscalculation; it is a structural trap. American bankers want repayment, but American industry wants protection. The contradiction is resolved by the subsequent solution—private American loans to Germany, which pay reparations to the Allies, which pay war debts to Washington—a circular flow of credit that enriches Wall Street intermediaries and leaves the underlying imbalance untouched. The omitted voice: the European worker, whose currency collapses, and the American farmer, who is told the tariff is for him but watches agricultural prices fall anyway because protection cannot create foreign demand for surplus crops. The tariff is class warfare disguised as patriotism.

The March on Rome. The steelman is that Mussolini’s government represented order against chaos, a bulwark against the Red menace that had seized factories and land in the *biennio rosso*. Now the forensic question: *cui bono*? The King, Victor Emmanuel III, had legal authority to declare martial law and order the army to disperse the blackshirts gathering outside Rome. He refused. Why? Because the Italian liberal state had already lost its nerve—and because the propertied classes, the industrialists of Milan and Turin, the landowners of the Po Valley, had made their preference clear. Fascist squadrismo had spent 1921-22 breaking strikes, burning union halls, and murdering socialist organizers. The state looked the other way. The March on Rome was not a seizure of power; it was a transfer of power from a liberal parliament that had failed to crush labor to a paramilitary force that had succeeded. The blackshirts were the private army of agrarian and industrial capital, now nationalized. Mussolini’s first cabinet includes liberals, Catholics, even a philosopher (Giovanni Gentile, who will write the manifesto of fascist education). The omission is the corpse: the thousands of socialists, trade unionists, and peasant organizers beaten or killed in the preceding two years, whose elimination made Mussolini “inevitable.” The March was theater; the violence that made it possible was the real event, and it was directed overwhelmingly at the left, with the connivance of the state.

The Irish Civil War ceasefire. The narrative is that the pro-Treaty forces, backed by British artillery and the threat of “immediate and terrible war,” compelled the anti-Treaty IRA to stand down, saving Ireland from further destruction. The omission is what the Treaty actually delivered: partition. The six counties of the north, with their built-in Unionist majority, were carved off not by Irish consent but by the Government of Ireland Act of 1920, passed before negotiations even began. The Free State got twenty-six counties of formal sovereignty and permanent irredentist grievance. The British gain: a friendly, indebted government in Dublin that will police its own republicans, while the strategic ports remain under Royal Navy control and the northern industrial base stays within the imperial economy. The ceasefire is not peace; it is the successful management of nationalist fracture by an empire that has learned to replace direct rule with client-state violence. The anti-Treaty dead are buried, and the Free State government, under British pressure, will execute seventy-seven of their comrades in the coming months. The silence in the archive is the voice of those executed Irish republicans, whose crime was refusing to swear an oath to a British monarch. The Free State’s founding is baptized in their blood, and the official story calls it necessity.

Lausanne. The conference is remembered as the moment the great powers bowed to reality and negotiated with Kemal’s victorious nationalists, replacing the dictated peace of Sèvres with a negotiated settlement. The reality: Lausanne is where ethnic cleansing acquires diplomatic legitimacy. The “population exchange” between Greece and Turkey—negotiated here, formalized in 1923—is not a humanitarian measure. It is the retroactive ratification of mass expulsions, massacres, and forced marches that have already occurred during the Greco-Turkish War. Over a million Greeks are removed from Anatolia, where their ancestors have lived for three millennia; hundreds of thousands of Muslims are expelled from Greece. The great powers, exhausted and eager for stability, bless this as “exchange” rather than what it is: the final stage of a demographic war that began with the Armenian genocide and continued through the burning of Smyrna. The omission is the property, the lives, the graves left behind. The gain: a Turkey that is ethnically “settled” and therefore governable by Kemal’s secular authoritarianism, and a Greece that can absorb refugees into its own nationalist project rather than agitating for a return to Asia Minor. The great powers wash their hands of the Ottoman inheritance by endorsing the logic of the nation-state as ethnic container. Lausanne is the template for every subsequent “orderly” expulsion dressed as treaty.

Finally, the Supreme Court and segregated transit. *Gong Lum v. Rice* will not be decided until 1927, but the lower court trends of 1922 are clear: the federal judiciary is actively dismantling what remains of Reconstruction’s legal architecture. The omission here is the most profound because it is the most American. The Court does not invent segregation; it blesses what the Southern Redeemers have already built through terror, disenfranchisement, and state law. The doctrine of “separate but equal” is a legal fiction that launders a regime of systematic subordination into a question of administrative convenience. Who benefits? The Southern Democratic Party, which rules one-party states on the backs of a disenfranchised Black electorate. Northern industrial capital, which gets a divided Southern workforce that cannot unionize across racial lines. The federal government, which avoids confronting the fact that the Civil War’s outcome has been partially reversed by a sustained campaign of paramilitary violence. The voice omitted is the Black passenger on the interstate train, whose body is the site upon which this legal fiction is inscribed, and whose testimony—in the hundreds of affidavits collected by the NAACP—the courts systematically ignore.

The through-line of 1922 is not exhaustion dressed as resolution. It is consolidation. The propertied, the white, the imperial, the nativist—they are not retreating. They are building the institutions, the legal doctrines, and the narrative cover that will govern the next two decades. The ACLU will defend dissenters, yes, but within a framework that presumes the state’s legitimacy. The tariff will protect capital and immiserate farmers until the whole structure collapses. Fascism will be normalized as a “technique of government” until it is too late. The Irish settlement and the Lausanne exchange will be cited as models of managed transition, teaching empire how to retreat while retaining strategic control. And the Supreme Court will continue to write opinions that treat Black lives as a problem of jurisdictional boundaries. The comfortable version—that 1922 was a year of exhausted peacemaking, of democratic societies groping toward stability—lets us avoid feeling responsible for the violence that stability required, and the violence that it stored up for later.

#### 🜃 Cartographer
The year 1922 is not a moment of recovery, but of calcification. The chaotic energy of the immediate post-war period—the revolutions, the red scares, the redrawn maps—has exhausted itself, and the response of the world's governing structures is to pour concrete. We see a global convergence on a single structural imperative: the containment of volatility through the hardening of boundaries. These boundaries are economic, racial, legal, and geographic, and they are being drawn with a precision that suggests a deep, shared fear of the fluidity that characterized the previous decade.

In the United States, this manifests as a judicial and legislative closing of the door. The Supreme Court's trajectory toward validating segregated transit (US-1) is not an anomaly of Southern prejudice but a federal ratification of a national caste system required to stabilize a rapidly industrializing, urbanizing society. As the Great Migration accelerates, moving Black labor from the agrarian South to the industrial North, the legal superstructure reacts by reinforcing the walls of separation. This is the slow variable of deep culture colliding with the fast variable of demographic shift; the outcome is a legal architecture designed to freeze social mobility in place. Simultaneously, the Fordney-McCumber Tariff (US-2) constructs an economic wall. By raising duties to 38%, the U.S. severs the transatlantic feedback loop of trade and credit. This is a deliberate choice to prioritize domestic price stability over global solvency, effectively exporting the cost of American protectionism to European debtors. The tariff is the economic analog to segregation: both are mechanisms of insulation, designed to protect an internal order from external or internal "contamination."

Yet, within this fortress, a new mechanism of defense is forged: the permanent ACLU (US-3). It is crucial to read this not merely as a triumph of liberty, but as a structural admission that the state's security apparatus has become a permanent feature of modern governance. The Palmer Raids demonstrated that the executive branch could suspend norms in the name of survival; the ACLU's incorporation acknowledges that such power will not recede. It institutionalizes the conflict between the individual and the state, turning civil liberties into a specialized domain of litigation rather than a default condition of citizenship. This mirrors the global trend where rights are no longer inherent but negotiated against the state's monopoly on violence.

That monopoly on violence is the thread connecting Washington to Rome and Dublin. Mussolini's March on Rome (W-1) is the most violent expression of the year's logic: when liberal institutions appear too weak to contain social unrest or nationalist grievance, paramilitary force becomes a legitimate pathway to state power. The King's invitation to Mussolini is a concession that the old order cannot hold without embracing the very forces threatening to destroy it. Similarly, the conclusion of the Irish Civil War (W-2) cements partition as a solution to intractable nationalist conflict. The border between North and South Ireland is a geographic scar tissue, a structural admission that a unified political identity is impossible, so the land itself must be cut to save the state.

Nowhere is the limit of imperial imposition more clearly mapped than at Lausanne (W-3). The abandonment of the Treaty of Sèvres in favor of negotiations with Mustafa Kemal's Turkey signals the end of the era where Western powers could simply draw lines on a map and expect them to hold. The Turkish War of Independence proved that demographic reality and armed will could overwrite diplomatic parchment. The resulting conference is a pragmatic surrender to the force of nationalism, redrawing the Middle East not according to idealistic principles, but according to the balance of power on the ground.

The carry-forward from 1922 is the entrenchment of the **`Fortress Logic`**. This is the operating system for the remainder of the century. The U.S. has chosen isolationism and internal stratification; Europe has begun its slide toward authoritarian solutions for democratic failures; the Middle East is being reshaped by the collision of retreating empire and rising nationalism. The specific inheritance for the present-day United States is the legal and economic precedent set here: the normalization of excluding populations (whether by race or nationality) to preserve internal stability, and the belief that economic sovereignty requires disengagement from global interdependence. The `Fortress Logic` dictates that safety is found behind walls—tariff walls, legal walls, border walls. When we look at the debates over immigration, trade protectionism, and the carceral state in the 21st century, we are looking at the mature architecture of the decisions made in 1922. The walls built this year were intended to keep the chaos of the 1910s out; instead, they trapped the pressures inside, pressurizing the system until it would eventually burst in the Depression and the wars to follow. The map drawn in 1922 did not bring peace; it merely organized the coming conflict into distinct, fortified zones.

### Round 2 — Cross-Examination

**Heretic → Herald:**

Let me restate your argument at its full strength, because it is formidable. Your claim is that 1922 marks the moment when the twentieth century perfects the technique of self-deception—that the year’s events cohere around a shared logic of narrative fabrication. Mussolini’s “March on Rome” is a coup sold as organic national resurgence. The Fordney-McCumber tariff is economic nationalism sold as patriotic self-defense. The Irish ceasefire is fratricidal exhaustion sold as honorable peace. The Treaty of Lausanne is ethnic cleansing sold as orderly population exchange. The Supreme Court’s segregation rulings are racial subordination sold as constitutional modesty.
