## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — The Scopes Trial (July 10–21, 1925)
- **What happened (fact):** High-school teacher John T. Scopes was tried in Dayton, Tennessee for violating the Butler Act, which prohibited teaching "any theory that denies the story of the Divine Creation of man as taught in the Bible." Clarence Darrow led the defense; William Jennings Bryan prosecuted. Scopes was convicted and fined $100 (later overturned on technicality). The trial was deliberately staged as a test case with ACLU backing and media spectacle, including first live radio broadcast of a trial.
- **The narrative *then*:** For modernists, this was the "Monkey Trial"—a last-gasp defense of medieval ignorance against the inevitable march of science, with Bryan as martyred buffoon. For fundamentalists, it was a righteous stand against the moral collapse that evolutionism threatened: if man descended from apes, then biblical authority crumbled, and with it the social order. Both sides claimed victory in the immediate aftermath—modernists in the mockery Bryan endured, fundamentalists in the legal verdict and the publicity for their cause. The trial's theatrical quality (deliberately sought by Dayton boosters seeking tourist revenue) made it feel less like jurisprudence than like a national morality play with assigned roles.
- **The narrative *now*:** The Scopes Trial lives in American memory as the foundational myth of the "culture war"—the moment when religious fundamentalism, defeated in the courtroom, retreated from public engagement into political hibernation, only to reemerge in the 1970s-80s. This is the "Scopes as Waterloo" narrative, reinforced by *Inherit the Wind* (1955 play, 1960 film), which uses the trial to attack McCarthyism more than to recover 1925. What the modern narrative buries: fundamentalism did not retreat but reorganized; Bryan's populism was as anti-corporate as it was anti-evolution; the trial was a media construction from the start; and the "settled" science of eugenics that Darrow and the defense implicitly endorsed would within two decades become American shame.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The trial crystallizes the narrative mechanism itself—how an event staged for publicity becomes, through retelling, the origin story of American secularization. It is indispensable for tracking how "the fundamentalist" was constructed as a national type, and how that construction served both modernist self-congratulation and, later, fundamentalist political mobilization in reaction against it.

#### US-2 — Publication of *The Great Gatsby* (April 10, 1925)
- **What happened (fact):** F. Scott Fitzgerald's third novel was published by Scribner's, selling poorly in its initial run (under 25,000 copies before Fitzgerald's death). The story of Jay Gatsby's doomed pursuit of Daisy Buchanan, narrated by Nick Carraway, traces the corruption beneath Jazz Age prosperity through bootlegging fortune, adulterous elite, and the green light at Daisy's dock.
- **The narrative *then*:** To Fitzgerald's immediate circle, the novel was a disappointment—too short, too quiet after the success of *This Side of Paradise* and *The Beautiful and Damned*. Reviews were respectful but not enthusiastic; the book seemed to miss the moment it described, arriving too early for retrospective elegy. Fitzgerald himself wrote to Perkins that he had "not produced an immediate 'hit'" and worried his gamble on "purely creative work" had failed. For the small audience that engaged it, however, Gatsby named something inarticulate in the air: the sense that the war's survivors had inherited a party they could not enjoy, that wealth had become a religion without transcendence.
- **The narrative *now*:** *The Great Gatsby* is the American novel—assigned to virtually every high school student, invoked in every political speech about inequality, adapted repeatedly for film as visual fetish. It has become the official story of American disillusionment, the green light recoded from ambiguous temptation to democratic aspiration. The novel's contemporary obscurity is forgotten; its commercial failure is now proof of its purity. What the modern narrative requires: that Gatsby be a tragedy of class, not of caste (the Jewish gangster Wolfsheim, the black-skinned "ashen" valley, the Nordicism of Tom's racial theories—all minimized in teaching); that Daisy be villainess rather than fellow victim of the same system; that Nick be reliable moral witness rather than complicit narrator whose final judgment ("better than the whole damn bunch put together") is itself desperate self-justification.
- **Why it earns a slot:** No text better demonstrates how a society constructs its usable past through canonization. The 1925 Gatsby and the classroom Gatsby are different books; tracking that transformation reveals how American liberalism needed a narrative of beautiful failure to legitimate its own compromises with capital.

#### US-3 — Formation of the Border Patrol (May 28, 1925)
- **What happened (fact):** The Labor Appropriation Act of 1924, implemented with funding in 1925, formally established the United States Border Patrol as a bureau within the Department of Labor (not Justice). Initial force: 450 officers. The Patrol was tasked with preventing illegal entry across the Mexican and Canadian borders, with primary early focus on Chinese immigrants evading the 1882 Exclusion Act and on liquor smuggling during Prohibition. The 1924 Johnson-Reed Act had just established the national origins quota system, creating new categories of "illegal" entry.
- **The narrative *then*:** Presented as bureaucratic rationalization—bringing "order" to border enforcement previously handled by ad hoc local authorities and mounted customs inspectors. The Labor Department framing suggested economic management, not ethnic policing. For southwestern agricultural interests, the Patrol represented a threat to their dependence on Mexican labor; for restrictionist nativists, it was insufficiently rigorous. The racial dimensions were explicit in implementation but officially disavowed: the Patrol's early operations concentrated on the Mexican border while the Canadian border remained lightly staffed, but this was not acknowledged in congressional debate as racial policy.
- **The narrative *now*:** The Border Patrol exists in American memory as an inevitable institution, its 1925 origins rarely taught. When invoked, it appears as neutral enforcement of sovereign borders, with the 1924-25 racial quotas treated as regrettable but separate history. The modern narrative of "border security" as non-ideological public good depends on this forgetting. What is buried: the Patrol's formation was specifically about making the new quota system operable; its early violence against Mexican workers (repatriations, "drying out" schemes); its function as a jobs program for returning white veterans; and the continuity between 1925's "illegal alien" construction and contemporary mass deportation infrastructure.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The Border Patrol's creation reveals how narrative works through institutional naming—"Labor" not "Justice," "Patrol" not "Police"—to depoliticize racial exclusion. It is the hinge between 19th-century Chinese exclusion and 21st-century border militarization, and its erasure from popular memory enables the present fantasy of a "broken" system rather than a deliberately constructed one.

#### W-1 — Locarno Treaties Signed (October 5–16, 1925)
- **What happened (fact):** Germany, France, and Belgium signed mutual guarantees of their Rhine borders, with Britain and Italy as guarantors. Germany also signed arbitration treaties with France, Belgium, Poland, and Czechoslovakia. The Eastern borders (with Poland, Czechoslovakia) were not similarly guaranteed—Stresemann deliberately refused, leaving them to bilateral arbitration agreements without British guarantee. The treaties were accompanied by Germany's entry into the League of Nations (September 1926, prepared by Locarno).
- **The narrative *then*:** "The Spirit of Locarno"—Austen Chamberlain spoke of a "new era" of European reconciliation; Stresemann was celebrated as statesman; Briand as poet of peace. The treaties were received as evidence that the Versailles system could be reformed from within, that Germany could be reintegrated without revanchism. The British public in particular embraced Locarno as proof that their sacrifice in 1914-18 had purchased permanent security, that the League system functioned. The omission of Eastern guarantees was noted by critics (Churchill called it "arbitration instead of security") but drowned in general enthusiasm. For Germans, Locarno offered equality without renunciation—a narrative of diplomatic redemption.
- **The narrative *now*:** Locarno survives in textbooks as "a step toward European unity" or "false hope that collapsed with Hitler"—either way, a footnote to the inevitable march toward 1939. The "Spirit of Locarno" is treated as naivete, the enthusiasm of a war-weary generation unwilling to face hard truths. What this narrative requires: that we see the interwar period as prelude to inevitable conflict, rather than as a field of genuine alternatives; that we forget the rationality of Stresemann's strategy (revision through integration, not war); that we ignore how the Western guarantee specifically enabled Eastern instability by signaling to Poland and Czechoslovakia that their security was secondary. The modern European project claims Locarno as ancestor while disavowing its actual structure—great-power guarantee, deliberate hierarchy of European zones.
- **Why it earns a slot:** Locarno demonstrates how diplomatic narrative constructs "spirit" as autonomous from text—the treaties said one thing (guaranteed Western borders, unsecured Eastern ones), but the story told was of general European reconciliation. This gap between document and discourse is the precise operation of myth in international relations, with direct relevance to how NATO expansion narratives operate today.

#### W-2 — Launch of *Mein Kampf* Vol. I (July 18, 1925)
- **What happened (fact):** Adolf Hitler, imprisoned for the failed Beer Hall Putsch of November 1923, dictated the first volume of his political autobiography and manifesto to Rudolf Hess in Landsberg prison. Published by Eher Verlag (the Nazi party press), initial sales were modest—approximately 9,473 copies in 1925, rising to 13,119 in 1926. The book combined autobiographical narrative, racial theory, anti-Semitic conspiracy, and geopolitical strategy (Lebensraum, alliance with Britain and Italy, anti-France orientation). A second volume appeared in 1926.
- **The narrative *then*:** For the Nazi faithful, *Mein Kampf* was the "Book"—Hitler's martyrdom in prison sanctified its words. For the broader German public, it was largely unread or dismissed as the ravings of a failed putschist; reviews in mainstream press were few and mocking. For foreign observers, it was either unknown or considered evidence that Hitler was finished—a man reduced to writing memoirs in prison. The Bavarian government considered banning it but feared making a martyr. For German Jews who read it, the threat was explicit and terrifying; for those who did not, the book's very existence was often unknown until much later. The Dawes Plan's stabilization of German economy in 1924 made Hitler's apocalyptic tone seem anachronistic.
- **The narrative *now*:** *Mein Kampf* is the most demonized book in modern history, treated as a blueprint for the Holocaust that contemporaries foolishly ignored. The "nobody read it" defense and the "everyone should have known" accusation coexist uneasily. The modern narrative requires Hitler to be simultaneously obvious (in retrospect) and inexplicable (in prospect). What this obscures: the book's actual readership was self-selected; its racial theories were continuous with mainstream German and European anthropology; its geopolitical analysis was conventional for the radical right; and its very unreadability (it is badly written, repetitive, nearly unreadable) was part of its function as loyalty test—pledging to a text one has not fully read is a common ritual of political religion. The postwar ban in Germany (ended 2016) itself became a narrative of democratic self-protection that arguably preserved the book's mystique.
- **Why it earns a slot:** No text better illustrates the temporal asymmetry of political prophecy—dismissed in its moment, overdetermined in retrospect. The narrative of "ignored warning" serves present-day security politics while preventing honest assessment of how political ideas actually circulate and acquire force.

#### W-3 — May Fourth Movement Anniversary & Nationalist Consolidation (1925)
- **What happened (fact):** The May Fourth Movement of 1919 (protests against Versailles transfer of German Shandong concessions to Japan) had its sixth anniversary in 1925. The more immediate catalyst was the May 30th Shanghai Massacre, when British-led police fired on Chinese demonstrators, killing at least nine. This sparked nationwide anti-imperialist strikes and boycotts. The Kuomintang (KMT), reorganized with Soviet Comintern assistance since 1923 and in uneasy United Front with the Chinese Communist Party (CCP), used the surge to advance its Northern Expedition against warlords. Sun Yat-sen had died March 12, 1925; Chiang Kai-shek was emerging as military leader.
- **The narrative *then*:** For urban Chinese students and workers, 1925 felt like the fulfillment deferred from 1919—the moment when anti-imperialist sentiment found organizational form. The KMT-CCP alliance was experienced by participants as genuine revolutionary unity, with the Soviet model offering a path that seemed to have succeeded in a backward country. For foreign observers, the strikes threatened concession privileges; for the British specifically, the May 30th shootings were initially defended as necessary order maintenance. The "Red Menace" narrative competed with "awakening Asia" narrative in Western press. For warlords, the nationalist surge was a threat to be co-opted or crushed; for peasants in the interior, largely uninvolved, it was distant thunder.
- **The narrative *now*:** 1925 is claimed by three incompatible narratives: the PRC's "inevitable revolution" teleology, in which the KMT-CCP alliance was merely tactical prelude to Communist victory; the KMT's Taiwan narrative, in which the United Front was Soviet manipulation that Chiang wisely purged in 1927; and Western liberal narratives of "failed democratic transition," in which the alliance represented a missed alternative to both Communist and Nationalist authoritarianism. All three require 1925 to be read backward from 1949 or 1949/1989. What is buried: the genuine contingency of the United Front; the diversity of anti-imperialist visions (federalist, anarchist, liberal); the specifically anti-Japanese focus that would shift after 1937; and the role of Comintern agents in constructing the very narrative of "national liberation" that participants experienced as spontaneous.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The May 30th movement and its KMT-CCP framing demonstrate how anniversary commemorations construct revolutionary continuity—1919 to 1925 to 1949— that did not exist as experienced. This narrative labor, performed by political entrepreneurs with foreign backing, is the core process by which modern nationalism is manufactured from contingent violence.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected because they collectively expose the structural tension between the era's surface-level stabilization (Locarno, Dawes-funded prosperity) and its deepening ideological fractures (Scopes, *Mein Kampf*, border militarization). The Scopes Trial and *The Great Gatsby* bracket the American cultural experience: one revealing the violent resistance of deep culture to modernity, the other diagnosing the spiritual bankruptcy of the consumer boom fueled by `managed-voluntarism`. Globally, the Locarno Treaties and the launch of *Mein Kampf* represent the dual trajectory of 1925: a diplomatic attempt to freeze the post-war status quo while a radical counter-force organizes explicitly to shatter it. I deliberately omitted the death of Sun Yat-sen (March 1925); while significant, the ongoing nationalist mobilization and the Shanghai Massacre better illustrate the active, violent struggle against imperial structures that defines the year's global shift, rather than the passing of a single figurehead.

## 2. Connections Within the Year

- **U.S. ↔ World:** The logic of exclusion operates simultaneously domestically and internationally: the formation of the Border Patrol (US-3) and the Scopes Trial's cultural policing (US-1) are the internal enforcement mechanisms of a nation retreating from global entanglement, mirroring the Locarno Treaties (W-1) which stabilize Western Europe by formally accepting the status quo while leaving Eastern borders fluid—a geographic triage that parallels the U.S. drawing a hard line at the Rio Grande while ignoring the ideological contagion brewing in Germany (W-2).
- **Event ↔ Event:** *The Great Gatsby* (US-2) provides the aesthetic autopsy of the economic engine that fuels the contradictions elsewhere: the speculative wealth and social mobility it depicts are the direct products of the same industrial acceleration that necessitates the Border Patrol to manage labor supply, while its theme of "recapturing the past" is the secular echo of the fundamentalist desperation seen in Dayton; both the novel's hollowness and the trial's fervor are reactions to a modernity that has severed ties with traditional anchors, just as Hitler's *Mein Kampf* (W-2) offers a violent, racialized attempt to reclaim a mythic past against the cosmopolitan order Locarno (W-1) attempts to cement.
- **Invisible / structural (shared hidden drivers):** The crisis of legitimacy in liberal institutions driven by the mismatch between 19th-century political boundaries and 20th-century industrial/demographic flows; whether in Tennessee, Texas, the Rhineland, or Shanghai, the central structural tension is the inability of existing legal and cultural frameworks to contain the velocity of change, leading to a global surge in boundary-making—biological (evolution), national (immigration), territorial (Locarno), and racial (Nazism/Chinese nationalism)—as a desperate containment strategy.

#### 🜄 Heretic
The Herald has given us the story as we prefer to remember it: a morality play. Dayton as a stage, Bryan as a buffoon, Darrow as reason’s knight. And there is something seductive in this framing, because it flatters our own self-image as the inheritors of enlightenment, the ones who would never be so foolish as to prosecute a schoolteacher for reading Darwin. Let me steelman this properly. The Scopes Trial *did* function as a national seminar on intellectual freedom. The ACLU’s intervention was genuine, not merely performative. Mencken’s dispatches, however cruel, exposed a genuine authoritarian impulse within populist Protestantism—the Butler Act was not a hypothetical, it was a statute with penalties, and John Scopes faced them. The trial’s transmutation into cultural myth served a real purpose: it gave the modernizing, urban, professional classes a usable past, a founding legend of their own ascendancy. There is truth in the Herald’s claim that this story became armor.

But armor against *what*? That is the question the Herald does not ask. And it is the question that, if we follow the money and the silences, transforms 1925 from a year of narrative construction into a year of systematic violence laundered through law, literature, and treaty.

Start with Dayton. The Herald notes the trial was staged. It does not ask who paid for the stage, or what else was playing while the audience watched. The Butler Act itself was less than three months old in July 1925. It was not an ancient folkway bubbling up from the holler; it was fresh legislation, lobbied through the Tennessee legislature by a well-funded fundamentalist network that included the World’s Christian Fundamentals Association. And what else was that network doing? It was fighting the teaching of evolution, yes. But it was also fighting the teaching of *anything that might lead white children to question the natural order of segregation*. The same legislative session that produced the Butler Act also produced bills to ban private schools—a direct attack on Catholic institutions, which fundamentalists saw as un-American. The same coalition that wanted Genesis in the classroom wanted immigrants out of the country and Black citizens fixed permanently at the bottom of the social ladder. The Scopes Trial was not a battle between science and religion. It was a skirmish in a broader war over who would control the production of obedient subjects in a multiracial empire. The “fundamentalist” was invented as a figure of fun by Mencken precisely so that the *other* projects of that coalition—the racial projects—could proceed without the same national scrutiny. Cui bono? The urban elite who wanted to manage the working-class whites they needed for labor without having to accommodate their theology. Let them have their monkey trial. We’ll take the border.

Because three weeks before Bryan and Darrow faced off, Congress passed the Labor Appropriation Act and created the Border Patrol. This is not a coincidence of the calendar. It is the same logic applied to different bodies. The Butler Act policed the minds of the young. The Border Patrol policed the movement of the brown. Both were instruments of a nativist consensus that the Scopes Trial, by focusing national attention on the cartoonish spectacle of Tennessee anti-intellectualism, effectively shielded from serious challenge. The 1924 Immigration Act had already slammed the door on southern and eastern Europeans and banned Asian immigration entirely. The Border Patrol was the enforcement arm, built to hunt Mexicans—who had been exempted from the quota system at the behest of Southwestern agribusiness, which wanted labor without citizens. The silence here is deafening. The Border Patrol was created to enforce a regime of illegalization that served growers perfectly: workers could come, but they could not stay, could not vote, could not organize, could not claim the protections of law. They were wanted as hands, not as humans. The distance between rhetoric and action? The rhetoric was “secure boundaries.” The action was the construction of a permanent, state-funded machinery for the racial management of a disposable workforce. We are still living in the legal architecture built that year. The Scopes Trial is a metaphor we argue about. The Border Patrol is a reality people die from.

Now turn to *The Great Gatsby*. The Herald reads it as a novel about the hollowness of wealth. That is the tenth-grade English version. The steelman: Fitzgerald did indeed write a critique of the Jazz Age’s moral vacancy, and the book’s endurance suggests it touched something real. But let me ask the Heretic’s question: whose voice is absent from that novel? The answer is everyone who built the wealth that Gatsby and his guests consume. The Valley of Ashes is a brilliant symbol—but it is a symbol of industrial waste, not of the industrial *workers* who labored in the factories that produced it. The servants, the cooks, the chauffeurs, the bootleggers’ foot soldiers—they are furniture. The novel’s great moral insight is that the rich are empty. Its great omission is that their emptiness is purchased with the fullness of other people’s stolen lives and labor. Gatsby’s fortune is built on crime, but the crime is selling alcohol. What about the fortunes built on the open shop, on the violent suppression of the strikes of 1919-1922, on the racial wage that kept white workers from making common cause with Black ones? The novel is a masterpiece of class critique that carefully, exquisitely, avoids the question of class power. It lets the reader feel superior to Tom Buchanan without ever asking what Tom Buchanan’s money *does* in the world, who it grinds down, who it excludes from the green light entirely. The novel is a moral sedative for the professional-managerial class that reads it: it allows them to despise the vulgar rich while enjoying the comforts the system provides.

Across the Atlantic, the same pattern holds: narrative management in service of power. The Locarno Treaties are remembered as the dawn of peace, the “Spirit of Locarno,” the moment when Germany, France, and Belgium guaranteed their borders and Austen Chamberlain and Aristide Briand shared a Nobel handshake. The steelman: Locarno *did* stabilize Western Europe for a half-decade. It *did* represent a genuine diplomatic achievement, a repudiation of the punitive rigidity of Versailles. But cui bono? The guarantees ran in one direction only. Germany’s western borders were inviolable. Its *eastern* borders—with Poland, with Czechoslovakia—were left explicitly unguaranteed. The message was clear, and everyone in the room understood it: the great powers would not go to war over the territorial integrity of the states created at Versailles in the east. This was not an oversight. It was an invitation, a green light dimmed just enough to maintain deniability. The “Spirit of Locarno” was the spirit of Western solidarity against the Bolshevik specter, a gentleman’s agreement that revision would be permitted so long as it moved east. The missing voice? The Poles. The Czechs. The millions of people whose borders were declared, by treaty, to be negotiable. Their absence from the table was the point of the table.

And who was reading the invitation? Adolf Hitler, in his cell at Landsberg, dictating the first volume of *Mein Kampf* to Rudolf Hess. The book was published in July 1925, three months before Locarno was signed. The Herald is right to link them. But the link is darker than mere “exploitation of instability.” Hitler’s argument in that volume is precisely that the Western powers can be bought off, that they will accept revision in the east in exchange for a crusade against Bolshevism, that the racial struggle for *Lebensraum* can be waged with the tacit consent of Britain and, eventually, a France too exhausted to resist. Locarno did not contradict this analysis. It *confirmed* it. The treaty was the proof of concept for the geopolitical strategy *Mein Kampf* was selling. The Western statesmen at Locarno did not intend to enable the Holocaust. Intent is the wrong category. They intended to secure their own borders and push the cost of revision onto populations they considered, at best, an inconvenience of the peace settlement. The distance between the rhetoric of “mutual guarantee” and the action of “eastern sacrifice” is the distance between the official story of interwar diplomacy and the truth.

Finally, China. The May Thirtieth Movement, the Shanghai Massacre, the surge of anti-imperialist protest and the consolidation of the KMT-CCP alliance—the Herald casts this as a story of nationalist awakening. It was. The steelman: British officers did fire on Chinese protesters, and the outrage was genuine and galvanizing. But cui bono from the story we tell about it? The Western narrative, then and now, tends to frame Chinese nationalism as a problem of “instability” or “xenophobia,” a disruption to the orderly management of treaty ports and concessions. The missing perspective is the one that sees the KMT’s reorganization not as chaos but as a rational, desperate response to a century of systematic extraction. The opium trade, the unequal treaties, the gunboat diplomacy—these were not errors of imperial overreach. They were the mechanisms by which Western capital bled China. The nationalist consolidation of 1925 was an attempt to stop the bleeding. The Western press covered it as “anti-foreign riots.” That framing served power by delegitimizing the victims of that power, by turning the colonized’s demand for sovereignty into a pathology.

What does the comfortable version of 1925 let us avoid feeling responsible for today? It lets us remember the Scopes Trial as a victory for science, when it was a sideshow that distracted from the construction of a carceral border regime whose descendants we still operate. It lets us read *Gatsby* as a tragic romance, when it is a document of class power’s ability to aestheticize its own guilt. It lets us celebrate Locarno as peacemaking, when it was a rehearsal for appeasement and abandonment. It lets us treat Hitler as a demonic aberration, when his strategic reading of Western priorities was, in 1925, entirely accurate. And it lets us see Chinese nationalism as a distant, alien upheaval, rather than as a mirror in which we might recognize the predictable, justified response to the kind of empire we prefer not to name. The inheritance is specific. It is the Border Patrol. It is the carceral logic of immigration enforcement. It is the cultural machinery that teaches us to despise the provincial white fundamentalist while depending on the exploited labor of the invisible brown migrant. That machinery was built in 1925. The stories we tell about that year are the grease that keeps it running.

#### 🜃 Cartographer
The year 1925 presents not as a collection of disparate headlines, but as a synchronized global reflex: the construction of walls where the ground is shifting fastest. The primary variable constraining human action this year is the collision between the inertia of nineteenth-century institutions and the velocity of twentieth-century forces—industrial production, mass migration, and ideological radicalization. When the system cannot adapt its core logic, it resorts to boundary enforcement. We see this in the physical hardening of the U.S.-Mexico border with the Birth of the Border Patrol, a bureaucratic response to the demographic pressures created by the very quotas that sought to freeze the American ethnic composition. This is not an isolated policy shift; it is the spatial manifestation of the same anxiety driving the Scopes Trial. In Dayton, the boundary being defended is not geographic but epistemological; the Butler Act is a legislative wall erected against the encroachment of evolutionary time on the static timeline of fundamentalist belief. Both the fence in the desert and the gag order in the courtroom are attempts to arrest motion, to declare that certain flows—of people, of ideas—are illegitimate.

This impulse to freeze reality connects directly to the international sphere. The Locarno Treaties represent the diplomatic equivalent of the Scopes defense: an attempt to codify a momentary stability in Western Europe by guaranteeing existing borders, effectively declaring the post-WWI settlement final for France and Germany while deliberately leaving the Eastern frontier undefined. This "liquid east" is the structural vacuum into which *Mein Kampf* pours its poison. Hitler's publication is not merely the ranting of a fringe figure; it is the extreme logical conclusion of a system that has prioritized order over justice. The Dawes Plan and Locarno manage the symptoms of German instability through capital and treaties, but they do not resolve the underlying grievance of a nation humiliated and hemmed in. Hitler identifies the flaw in the Cartographer's map: if the West is fortified, the expansion must go East. The structural driver here is the mismatch between national ambition and territorial constraint, a pressure cooker that Locarno seals tight on one side while leaving the valve open on the other.

Meanwhile, in China, the May Fourth anniversary and the Shanghai Massacre reveal the same dynamic playing out in the context of imperial fracture. The consolidation of the Kuomintang and its alliance with the Communists is a reaction to the inability of warlord fragmentation to resist foreign economic penetration. Just as the U.S. builds a border to keep the world out, and Europe draws lines to keep peace, Chinese nationalists realize that only a unified, hardened political structure can withstand the centrifugal forces of imperialism. Underpinning all six events is the shadow of *The Great Gatsby*. Fitzgerald's novel is the cultural seismograph recording the tremors of this new era. The "green light" represents the future that is constantly receding, the impossibility of fixing the past in place. Gatsby's failure to recreate his history mirrors the failure of the fundamentalists to stop evolution, the failure of isolationists to stop migration, and the failure of diplomats to stop the march toward total war.
