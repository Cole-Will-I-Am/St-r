## 0. Context Carried Forward

- **Reconstruction's Liquidation — `reconstruction-fatigue`:** The informal "Compromise" has hardened into operational reality; with Hayes inaugurated, federal troops are withdrawn from the South Carolina and Louisiana statehouses in April, leaving Republican governments to collapse under Democratic paramilitary pressure and effectively ending federal protection of Black civil rights.
- **The Gold Straitjacket — `currency-war`:** As the 1879 resumption deadline approaches, deflationary pressure intensifies, triggering the first nationwide railroad strikes in July as wages are slashed to maintain dividend payments for bondholders, merging agrarian debt distress with industrial labor radicalization.
- **Total Plains Pacification — `frontier-containment`:** The winter campaign of 1876–77 has broken the logistical backbone of the Northern Cheyenne and Lakota resistance; by mid-1877, most bands are surrendering or fleeing toward Canada, shifting the U.S. strategy from battlefield engagement to the bureaucratic management of confinement and forced assimilation.
- **The Exclusion Firewall — `racial-gatekeeping`:** With both parties now explicitly endorsing restriction, the legislative focus shifts from state-level harassment to the drafting of federal statutes, while diplomatic tensions with China rise over the treatment of merchants and students already residing in the U.S.
- **The Communication Monopoly — `private-infrastructure`:** The Bell Telephone Company moves aggressively to litigate or acquire competitors following the 1876 patent grant, setting the stage for 1877 as the year the network topology shifts from local exchange experimentation to a consolidated, privately held national utility model.
- **Imperial Realignment — `global-imperial-shift`:** The conclusion of the Great Eastern Crisis at the Congress of Berlin reinforces the validity of great-power spheres of influence; the U.S. observes this consolidation of European imperial authority while simultaneously securing its own hemispheric dominance through the final suppression of internal dissent and border closure.

## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — The Great Railroad Strike (July 14–July 25, 1877)
- **What happened (fact):** A spontaneous walkout by B&O workers in Martinsburg, West Virginia, spreads to major hubs including Pittsburgh and Chicago, halting half the nation's freight and requiring federal troop deployment to break the uprising.
- **The narrative *then*:** "The labor question" had arrived as a national emergency. To railroad executives and the Hayes administration, this was insurrection verging on revolution—a terrifying demonstration that the industrial workforce could paralyze the republic. Workers spoke of "the rights of labor" against "monopoly," of starvation wages and the moral economy of toil. The press oscillated between sympathy for "worthy" workingmen and panic at "communistic" mobs. For the emerging labor movement, it was baptism by fire; for the bourgeoisie, confirmation that order required the mailed fist.
- **The narrative *now*:** A foundational moment of American labor history, precursor to the Pullman Strike and the Wagner Act. Often taught as the first national strike, evidence of growing class consciousness. The violence against strikers—Pittsburgh's burning—gets memorialized more than the strikers' actual demands. It sits in a teleology of labor's "long road" toward organized power, with the federal intervention read as overreach rather than as the normalized baseline of American labor relations it became.
- **Why it earns a slot:** 1877 is unimaginable without this rupture. The strike collapsed in days but established the template: industrial capital, federal troops, and the language of "riot" versus "strike." It made visible what the Gilded Age preferred hidden—the human cost of continental integration.

#### US-2 — Withdrawal of Federal Troops (April 24, 1877)
- **What happened (fact):** President Hayes orders the removal of the last remaining federal soldiers from the South Carolina and Louisiana statehouses, causing the immediate collapse of the last Reconstruction-era Republican governments.
- **The narrative *then:** To white southerners, "redemption"—the restoration of self-rule and the natural order. To Black southerners, abandonment; the betrayal of a promise still vibrating from 1868. To northern Republicans, a pragmatic exchange (the presidency for the South) or a relief from an exhausting commitment. The story told in drawing rooms was of sectional reconciliation, of the South "regaining" its place. The story in Black churches and conventions was of the Exodus, of a covenant broken.
- **The narrative *now*:** The formal end of Reconstruction, conventional wisdom's terminus for "the failure" of biracial democracy. Recent scholarship has complicated this: Reconstruction's collapse was gradual, local, violent; 1877 is symbolic more than causal. Yet the date persists because it serves a national need—a single moment to contain a messy, shameful story. "The Compromise of 1877" implies negotiation; the reality was capitulation to terror. The myth of Reconstruction as "tragic era" (Dunning School) has been dismantled in academe but persists in popular memory as "too much, too soon."
- **Why it earns a slot:** The hinge. Every subsequent narrative of American race relations pivots here—Jim Crow as "natural" regional expression, federal power as illegitimate intrusion, the Lost Cause as romance. The date is overdetermined but inescapable.

#### US-3 — Surrender of Chief Joseph (October 5, 1877)
- **What happened (fact):** After a 1,200-mile fighting retreat toward Canada, the Nez Perce leader surrenders in Montana, marking the effective end of organized armed Indigenous resistance in the Pacific Northwest.
- **The narrative *then*:** For eastern newspapers, a "savage war" concluded with civilization's inevitable triumph; for readers, a thrilling chase narrative, the Nez Perce as noble but doomed. Chief Joseph's eloquence—"I will fight no more forever"—was immediately seized and polished, made safe as tragedy. For the Nez Perce, dispossession and exile; for the army, vindication after embarrassment. The frontier was closing, Turner would soon declare, and this was its elegiac final movement.
- **The narrative *now*:** Chief Joseph enters the pantheon of "good Indians"—dignified, non-threatening, ultimately accommodating. The 1,200-mile retreat is celebrated as military achievement, eliding what was being achieved: ethnic cleansing. The surrender speech, likely reconstructed by a white translator, has become a text of Native American literature courses, its authenticity less important than its serviceability to a narrative of noble defeat. The Wallowa Valley is occasionally "remembered" in Oregon place-names; the war itself remains peripheral to most Americans' sense of their history.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The last act of the Northwest Indian Wars, and a masterclass in narrative capture. Joseph's voice survives because it could be made to speak resignation rather than resistance. The alternative—remembering the war as systematic removal—requires confronting the settler-colonial project directly.

#### W-1 — The Congress of Berlin (June 13–July 13, 1877 [convened], concluded 1878)
- **What happened (fact):** European powers meet to redraw the map of the Balkans following the Russo-Turkish War, establishing a precedent for diplomatic arbitration of imperial spheres that excludes non-European voices.
- **The narrative *then*:** The Concert of Europe functioning as designed—civilized diplomacy preventing general war, Bismarck the honest broker. For Disraeli, "peace with honor" and a stake in Ottoman survival; for Russia, humiliation moderated by gains. The small nations whose fates were traded—Bulgaria, Bosnia, the others—were not present; their future was "the Eastern Question," a problem for great powers to solve. This was progress: war abbreviated by conference, violence made rational and bounded.
- **The narrative *now*:** The template for twentieth-century great-power management and its failures—Versailles, Yalta, the endless "peace processes" that manufacture instability. The Congress is studied for what it reveals about imperial habit: the assumption that European interests define legitimacy, the confidence that maps redrawn in drawing rooms produce durable order. The Balkan crises it failed to prevent, the resentments it planted, make it prologue to 1914. Yet its basic grammar—"international community" as great-power club—persists in the Security Council.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The moment when the Eastern Question's management becomes visible as a system. The exclusion of the actual inhabitants is not a bug but the feature: this is how "civilization" was administered, and how its crises were deferred until they could no longer be.

#### W-2 — Opening of the Second Anglo-Afghan War (November 1877)
- **What happened (fact):** British forces invade Afghanistan from India to counter perceived Russian influence, illustrating the aggressive "Great Game" dynamics of borderland containment shaping global imperialism.
- **The narrative *then*:** For British India, a necessary forward policy; for the metropole, a distant frontier of the Russian threat made concrete. The "Great Game" was already romance—Kipling's Kim would cement it—of daring agents, mountain passes, and the defense of India. Afghanistan was abstraction, buffer, stage. The invasion's brutality (Kabul's occupation, the Khyber's cost) was reported; the strategic logic—hold the line against the tsar—was rarely questioned. This was empire's daily work, unremarkable.
- **The narrative *now*:** The Anglo-Afghan wars have become, in American memory, preface to Soviet and then U.S. intervention—a pattern of great-power burial. "Graveyard of empires" is the cliché, making Afghans into a force of nature rather than actors with agency. The Second War specifically is obscure; the First (1842) and the Soviet debacle claim attention. The continuity—foreign powers defining Afghanistan's significance without Afghan participation—is rarely examined as a structure.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The Great Game as lived ideology, not retrospective metaphor. The "perceived Russian influence" was often phantom; the war demonstrates how threat inflation serves imperial expansion. The pattern—intervention, withdrawal, return—establishes itself here.

#### W-3 — Proclamation of Queen Victoria as Empress of India (January 1, 1877)
- **What happened (fact):** The Royal Titles Act formally binds the British monarchy to the Indian subcontinent, signaling a shift from corporate (East India Company) to direct state imperialism and reinforcing racial hierarchies within the empire.
- **The narrative *then*:** The Durbar at Delhi—extravagant, Orientalist, announcing a new relationship. For Disraeli's Conservatives, a stroke of political imagination: the crown as binding force, empire as romance, the Queen-Empress as figurehead for subjects who would never see Westminster. For Indian elites, mixed signals: flattery and incorporation, but on terms that fixed subordination. The title "Empress" carried Roman echoes, Catholic associations debated and overcome; it was modernity and antiquity simultaneously.
- **The narrative *now*:** A curiosity of imperial kitsch, the Durbar reproduced in documentaries as spectacle without consequence. The shift from Company to Crown is noted in textbooks as administrative reform; the racial hierarchy it reinforced is less visible. The Royal Titles Act's repeal (1948) is forgotten; the Commonwealth's ceremonial continuities obscure the violence of 1877's claims. For Britain, the Empress title is embarrassment or nostalgia; for India, a moment in the longer narrative of colonial subjection that culminates in 1947 and beyond.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The formalization of imperial racial order. The corporate mask drops; the state claims its possession openly. This is the empire that would govern through the twentieth century, and whose unravelling still structures South Asian politics.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected because they collectively demonstrate the hardening of structural boundaries: the domestic color line (`reconstruction-fatigue`), the class line (`currency-war`), and the geographic line (`frontier-containment`), mirrored globally by the formalization of imperial spheres. The Great Railroad Strike is indispensable as the moment economic deflation transformed into physical insurrection, proving that the "gold standard" required violence to enforce. I deliberately omitted the exact passage of the Chinese Exclusion Act (which occurred in 1882) because 1877 represents the critical *conjuncture* where the political consensus formed, making the later statute inevitable; including the 1882 law would obscure the specific ideological shift occurring in this year. Similarly, while the Battle of Little Bighorn is more famous, it belongs to 1876; 1877 is defined by the *surrender* and the transition to reservation life, which is the structural outcome that matters for the long term.

### Round 1 — Opening Interpretations (uninterrupted)

#### 🜂 Herald
In 1877, the stories a civilization tells about itself achieved a terrible coherence. This is the year when the American republic and the British Empire, each in their fashion, confessed what they had become—and immediately began constructing myths to survive that confession.

Consider the simultaneity. In April, Hayes withdraws federal troops from the South Carolina and Louisiana statehouses; in July, the Great Railroad Strike convulses the industrial North; in October, Chief Joseph surrenders. The narrative then available to Americans was not sequential but cumulative: the South "redeemed," the laborer disciplined, the Indian subdued. Each event was reported in its own register—sectional reconciliation, riot, frontier closing—but together they composed a single story about the kind of nation emerging from the Civil War's wreckage. It was not the nation abolitionists had imagined. It was something harder, more hierarchical, more thoroughly committed to order as its highest value. The hope of 1868, of a republic re-founded on universal citizenship, had not been defeated in debate but dissolved in practice—dissolved by the withdrawal that made white terror sovereign, by the troop deployments that made corporate property sacred, by the campaigns that made the continent finally and fully "American" in the only sense that mattered to power.

The contemporaries knew this, many of them, in their bones. Black southerners who had voted in 1876 found themselves navigating "redemption" as a lived catastrophe, not a constitutional moment. Railroad workers who struck for the restoration of wages cut in the depression of 1873 discovered that the army they had not thought to face would shoot them where they stood. Chief Joseph, who had never wanted war, who had sought only the Wallowa Valley his father had promised him, learned that American honor extended only to the vanquished who would perform dignity for their conquerors. The felt experience of 1877 was abandonment and violence, the state's withdrawal from protection and its deployment in repression. Yet this was not the story that could be told and sustained. It had to be translated.

The translation was already underway. The withdrawal of troops became "the end of Reconstruction," a term that carried the Dunning School's assumptions before that school existed: that Reconstruction had been an error, that federal intervention had been unnatural, that the South was merely recovering its proper self-government. The strike became "the Great Railroad Strike," its violence emphasized over its causes, its suppression narrated as restoration of order rather than as class war from above. Chief Joseph became the noble savage, his surrender speech a text for schoolchildren, the Nez Perce war a tragedy of inevitable progress. Each translation served the same function: to make the outcomes of 1877 appear as natural growth rather than as political construction, to make the victims complicit in their own defeat, to make the winners appear as stewards rather than conquerors.

The British Empire performed its own translations with equal virtuosity. The Proclamation of Victoria as Empress of India on January 1 transformed a commercial extraction into a sacred trust, a corporate looting into a crown responsibility. The Durbar's theatricality—elephants, jewels, the Queen represented by a Viceroy kneeling to himself—was not mere spectacle but narrative argument: this rule was legitimate because it was magnificent, justified because it was ancient in form if not in fact. The Congress of Berlin, convening six months later, extended this logic to the Balkans: European powers would dispose of non-European peoples as their interests dictated, and call it peace. The Second Anglo-Afghan War, opening in November, demonstrated what "peace" required—the perpetual expansion of violence to maintain the system that claimed to limit it. The "Great Game" was already, by 1877, being narrated as adventure rather than as the strategic calculus of empire; Kipling had not yet written, but the romance was ready for him.

What connects these events across their geographical separation is the work they performed for the societies that produced them. Each was, in its moment, experienced as crisis; each was, in memory, converted into progress. The conversion required specific mechanisms: the schoolbook that made Chief Joseph's speech a literary text rather than a document of conquest; the monument that made "redemption" a regional recovery rather than a racial terror; the diplomatic history that made the Congress of Berlin a success of the concert system rather than a seedbed of future wars; the imperial nostalgia that made the Durbar a pageant rather than a proclamation of subjecthood. These mechanisms operated then and operate now, because the stories they produce are not merely false consciousness but functional necessities. A society that believed the truth of 1877—that its order rested on abandoned Black citizens, shot strikers, exiled Indians, subjugated Afghans, and Indian peasants ruled by a distant crown—would have to become something else entirely. The myths permit continuation.

The inheritance is specific and present. The American labor movement's weakness, its inability to develop class-wide institutions comparable to European social democracy, traces partly to 1877's lesson: that the federal government would intervene for capital, and that the language of "riot" would always be available to discredit collective action. The racial order of the American South, its carceral and electoral mechanisms, descends directly from the "redemption" that 1877 completed and sanitized. The place of Native Americans in national memory—the noble savage, the ecological mystic, the casino operator—derives from the narrative capture performed on Chief Joseph, the reduction of sovereign peoples to symbols of a completed conquest. The British inheritance is equally concrete: the Commonwealth's ceremonial bonds, the persistent difficulty of acknowledging empire as violence rather than as "mistake" or "anachronism," the racial hierarchies that structured imperial rule and persist in post-imperial Britain's self-understanding. The Congress of Berlin's legacy lives in the Security Council's composition, in the assumption that great powers manage the periphery, in the "international community" that excludes those it claims to protect.

The official story of 1877 is a story of exhaustion and restoration. It says: after the terrible exertions of civil war and the bitter experiments of Reconstruction, the American republic chose peace. It withdrew an overreaching federal hand from the South, allowing white and black southerners to work out their own destiny—a process, regrettably, marred by violence but ultimately necessary for sectional reunion. It defended property and public order against the anarchy of the Great Strike, proving that the rule of law could not be held hostage by mobs. It completed the pacification of the frontier with the noble surrender of Chief Joseph, whose eloquence in defeat confirmed both his people's dignity and the inevitability of civilization's advance. Abroad, the British Empire brought the same civilizing logic to its own domains: Victoria's new imperial title formalized a benevolent union of crown and colony, the invasion of Afghanistan was a regrettable but necessary measure of frontier security, and the Congress of Berlin demonstrated that the Great Powers could manage their rivalries through diplomacy rather than war. The year, in this telling, was a pivot from the chaos of the mid-century toward the stability of the modern order—a necessary closing of open questions, a consolidation, a maturing.

Now let us examine what this story buries, who paid for it, and who profited from the telling.

**The Withdrawal: Cui Bono?**

The withdrawal of federal troops from South Carolina and Louisiana was not a restoration of self-government. It was the formal surrender of the United States government to a terrorist insurgency. The "Redeemers" who took power in April 1877 had not won elections; they had won a paramilitary campaign of assassination, night-riding, and ballot-box destruction that stretched back to the Colfax massacre of 1873, where white militias murdered at least sixty black men after they had surrendered. The Republican governments that collapsed were the only governments in those states that had ever been elected by a biracial electorate. Their collapse was not a return to normalcy but the installation, by force, of a regime whose first act was to begin stripping black citizens of the vote, the courts, the schoolhouse, and the right to own land free of predatory lien laws. The "compromise" that seated Hayes—and let us be precise, the Wormley House Bargain was a deal among railroad men and political bosses, not a constitutional settlement—traded the physical safety of four million people for the presidency and the promise of federal subsidies for a southern transcontinental rail route. The beneficiaries were not abstract "reconciliation." The beneficiaries were the former Confederates who regained state power, the northern industrialists who wanted a pacified southern market and labor force, and the railroad corporations—chief among them the Texas and Pacific and the Southern Pacific—whose land grants and bond issues depended on political stability. The silenced are the black citizens of the South, whose testimony about what was happening to them was systematically excluded from the national narrative for the next half-century, replaced by the minstrel-show fiction of "black incapacity" and the Dunning School's slander against Reconstruction. The absence of those voices from the record is itself the evidence: we do not have, in the mainstream archive of 1877, the letters of the freedpeople of Edgefield County describing what it meant to watch the bluecoats ride away. We have instead the editorials of northern newspapers declaring that the "negro question" had been settled. The gap between the event and the record is the crime.

**The Strike: Whose Order?**

The Great Railroad Strike was crushed by the same army that had been withdrawn from the South. This is not a coincidence; it is a revelation of the state's actual priorities. Federal troops were not available to protect black voters in Hamburg, South Carolina, in July 1876, when a white mob executed six black militiamen in cold blood. They were available, in overwhelming force, to protect the property of the Baltimore and Ohio, the Pennsylvania Railroad, and the other corporations whose wage cuts—in some cases the second or third cut in three years, while dividends continued to flow to shareholders—had driven their employees to desperation. The strike was not a riot; it was a coordinated work stoppage that spread because railroad workers across multiple companies and cities shared a common grievance: they were being immiserated by monopolies that had received enormous public subsidies in land and bonds and were now pleading poverty. The official narrative labeled the strikers "communards" and "mob," invoking the specter of the Paris Commune to justify the use of lethal force. In Pittsburgh, the militia fired into crowds of unarmed civilians, killing at least twenty, including women and children. In Chicago, police and federal troops battled strikers in the streets. In Martinsburg, the governor requested federal troops before any significant property destruction had occurred. The message was unmistakable: the state would not intervene to protect the rights of citizens, but it would intervene instantly and violently to protect the balance sheets of corporations. The beneficiaries were the railroad owners, who broke the back of organized labor on their lines for a generation, and the political class that demonstrated its reliability to capital. The silenced are the strikers themselves, whose demands—a living wage, an end to the arbitrary power of foremen and managers, some recognition of their humanity—were translated by the press into the primal scream of the dangerous classes. The inheritance for the present is the doctrine, forged in the blood of Pittsburgh, that the protection of private property is the state's highest obligation, while the protection of citizens is a matter of discretion.

**The Surrender: The Management of Defeat**

Chief Joseph's surrender speech—"I will fight no more forever"—is one of the most celebrated texts in the American canon, taught to schoolchildren as an example of tragic nobility. What is omitted from the schoolroom version is that the Nez Perce had been legally guaranteed the Wallowa Valley by the treaty of 1855, that the treaty was broken when gold was discovered on their lands, that Joseph's "fighting retreat" was an attempt to reach Canada because the United States had made it impossible for his people to exist within its borders, and that the surrender terms promised by General Howard—return to the reservation in Idaho—were immediately violated. The Nez Perce were shipped to a malarial camp in Kansas, then to Indian Territory, where more than half of them died of disease. The speech itself was likely edited and embellished by the Army officer who transcribed it; we have no verbatim record of what Joseph said in his own language. The event was immediately aestheticized, turned into a tableau of the "vanishing Indian" that allowed white Americans to mourn the people they were exterminating without ever questioning the process of extermination. The beneficiaries were the settlers and mining companies that acquired the Wallowa Valley, the military officers whose reputations were burnished by a "humane" campaign, and the national narrative that required a story of closure rather than a story of ongoing theft. The silenced are the Nez Perce themselves, whose political and legal arguments—rooted in treaty law, in prior occupancy, in the simple proposition that a people has a right to its homeland—were rendered inaudible by the aesthetic of the noble surrender. The gap between the rhetoric of "I will fight no more forever" and the reality of the death camps in Kansas is the gap between the story America tells about its conquests and the facts of those conquests.

**The Empire: The Logic of the Title**

Victoria's proclamation as Empress of India on January 1, 1877, was not a ceremonial flourish. It was the formal announcement that the British state had absorbed the extractive apparatus of the East India Company and would now run it directly, with the full prestige of the crown attached. The timing is instructive: the proclamation occurred just weeks after a devastating famine in the Madras and Bombay Presidencies had killed an estimated five million Indians. The famine was not a natural disaster; it was a policy disaster, the result of the British administration's insistence on maintaining grain exports, continuing tax collection, and refusing to interfere with the market while people starved. The famine was the direct consequence of the same logic that the new imperial title celebrated: that India existed to produce revenue for Britain, that Indian lives were secondary to British ledgers. The proclamation, with its elaborate durbar and its carefully managed press coverage, was a propaganda operation designed to overwrite the reality of mass death with the pageantry of imperial benevolence. The beneficiaries were the British state, which consolidated its control over the subcontinent's resources, and the British aristocracy, which acquired a new theater for the distribution of titles and sinecures. The silenced are the five million dead, whose names do not appear in the Illustrated London News coverage of the Delhi durbar, and the millions more whose land, labor, and lives were now explicitly the property of a distant queen.

**The Congress: The Cartography of Exclusion**

The Congress of Berlin, convened in June 1877, is celebrated in diplomatic history as a triumph of multilateralism, a moment when the Great Powers chose negotiation over war. What this celebration omits is that the Congress was a meeting of empires to divide the territory of other empires without the presence or consent of the people who lived there. The Balkans were carved into spheres of influence—Austrian, Russian, Ottoman, British—with no regard for the ethnic, linguistic, or religious realities on the ground, realities that the Great Powers understood perfectly well and chose to ignore in favor of strategic calculation. The Congress created the template for the Berlin Conference of 1884, which would partition Africa with the same serene disregard for African agency. It established the principle that "international law" meant the law made by the strong to regulate their competition, not the law that protected the weak from predation. The beneficiaries were the European chancelleries, which preserved their balance of power at the cost of storing up ethnic grievances that would detonate in 1914 and again in the 1990s. The silenced are the Balkan peoples, who were not consulted, and the Ottoman subjects whose fate was decided by men who had never set foot in their villages.

**Afghanistan: The Logic of the Buffer**

The Second Anglo-Afghan War, launched in November 1877, was not a response to genuine Russian aggression. It was a preemptive invasion designed to install a puppet ruler in Kabul who would exclude Russian influence from a region the British had unilaterally declared their sphere. The "Great Game" was not a game for the Afghans who died in it. The British invaded because they feared that an independent Afghanistan might make its own choices about its alliances—choices that might not align with the security of the Raj. The same logic would govern British policy for the next forty years: the right of the empire to determine the foreign policy of its neighbors, backed by the threat of invasion. The beneficiaries were the British military and political establishment, which acquired another theater for career advancement and another justification for the expansion of the Indian Army. The silenced are the Afghans, whose sovereignty was treated as a temporary inconvenience, and whose resistance to foreign occupation—already fierce in the First Anglo-Afghan War—was dismissed as fanaticism rather than recognized as the predictable response of a people to yet another invasion.

**The Inheritance**

What is passed forward to the present-day United States from 1877 is not a lesson in reconciliation or order. It is a working model of how a republic can maintain its rhetoric of freedom while practicing systematic coercion, how it can celebrate its democratic institutions while emptying them of substance for the people who most need their protection, how it can mourn its victims while continuing to profit from their dispossession. The year 1877 taught the American governing class that the state could be simultaneously withdrawn and deployed—withdrawn from the obligation to protect citizens, deployed to protect property and racial hierarchy—without losing democratic legitimacy, provided that the story was told correctly.
