## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — The Creek Treaty of Washington (January 24, 1826)
- **What happened (fact):** A factional minority of Creek leaders signs a treaty ceding all remaining Creek lands in Georgia to the state, directly contradicting the National Council's laws and inviting immediate civil conflict within the tribe.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Georgia officials and the Monroe administration, this was the fulfillment of "civilization" policy—proof that Indians could be induced to see progress in removal. The treaty signers (led by Opothleyahoga's faction) were celebrated as pragmatists who understood the inevitable. For the Creek National Council and the majority faction under Opothleyahola, this was apostasy and forgery; the signers were not authorized, and the National Council's 1824 law explicitly forbade such sales. The immediate violence—assassinations of the treaty signers, intra-tribal warfare—was narrated by whites as "savage" confirmation that Indians lacked the capacity for ordered self-governance, justifying federal intervention and eventual forced removal.
- **The narrative *now*:** The Treaty of Washington appears in textbooks as a tragic precursor to the Trail of Tears, an example of fraudulent dealing. The internal Creek politics are occasionally noted, but the dominant frame is victimization by American expansion. The National Council's constitutional sophistication and the assassinations as enforcement of tribal law are less emphasized than the gross asymmetry of power.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the mechanism of dispossession in microcosm: not overwhelming force but engineered division, the exploitation of internal faction to produce a "voluntary" cession that authorizes the narrative of consent. The Creek civil war that follows is the hidden cost of the "civilization" myth.

#### US-2 — Founding of the American Temperance Society (February 13, 1826)
- **What happened (fact):** Evangelical reformers in Boston establish the first national organization dedicated to total abstinence, marking the shift from individual moral suasion to coordinated social engineering against alcohol.
- **The narrative *then*:** For the founders—Lyman Beecher's network, the Congregationalist elite—this was the Second Great Awakening applied to the social body. The drunkard was not merely a sinner but a symptom of disordered community; total abstinence (not mere moderation) was the test of Christian commitment. The narrative drew on visible crisis: the "moral statistics" of poverty and crime linked to ardent spirits, the erosion of republican virtue in a nation of tipplers. For opponents—tavern keepers, ethnic Catholics, Jacksonian democrats—this was clerical overreach, the same elite that demanded bank charters now dictating bodily practice, a conspiracy against working-class culture.
- **The narrative *now*:** Temperance is remembered as well-meaning folly, the ancestor of Prohibition's disaster, or as a women's movement prelude (the WCTU). The evangelical theology is muted; the ATS becomes a public health story, a step toward modern social policy. The class and ethnic warfare embedded in the movement—its function as Anglo-Protestant cultural imperialism—is acknowledged but not central.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the invention of the modern pressure group: national in scope, statistical in argument, aiming at state power through moral panic. The template—Beecher's sermons, the tracts, the local auxiliary societies—will be copied by abolitionists, suffragists, and every subsequent reform movement.

#### US-3 — Death of Jefferson and Adams (July 4, 1826)
- **What happened (fact):** Both former presidents die on the fiftieth anniversary of the Declaration of Independence, an uncanny synchronicity that instantly mythologizes the Revolutionary generation while obscuring the deep sectional rifts they left unresolved.
- **The narrative *then*:** This was received as divine punctuation. Jefferson's last words—"Is it the Fourth?"—and Adams's reported last words—"Thomas Jefferson survives"—were circulated within hours by the fast telegraph and newspaper networks. The coincidence was impossible to ignore: the author and the defender of independence, the partisan rivals reconciled in correspondence, extinguished together as the nation marked its majority. For contemporaries, this was providential confirmation of American exceptionalism, a sign that the Founding was not merely human achievement but covenant. The mourning was ecumenical; even Federalists who had despised Jefferson joined the chorus. The timing authorized a particular reading of the Founding as unified, providential, and complete.
- **The narrative *now*:** The "coincidence" remains in civic catechism, the stuff of July 4th speeches and trivia questions. But the myth has been complicated: we now know Jefferson died impoverished and indebted, his agrarian vision already defeated by the market revolution; Adams died fearing the dissolution his son's presidency would fail to prevent. The reconciliation narrative obscures that their correspondence was private, their political legacies antagonistic, and the republic they celebrated was already dividing over the very institution Jefferson had blamed on the British king.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the most concentrated moment of American civil religion in the nineteenth century, the instant when historical accident becomes national scripture. The myth constructed in July 1826 will be invoked at every subsequent crisis as evidence of original unity.

#### W-1 — Coronation of Nicholas I (September 3, 1826)
- **What happened (fact):** Following the suppression of the Decembrist revolt, Nicholas I is crowned Tsar of Russia, institutionalizing a doctrine of "Orthodoxy, Autocracy, and Nationality" that positions Russia as the ideological antithesis of American republicanism.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Nicholas and his ideologue Uvarov, the coronation was restoration after the earthquake of December 1825. The Decembrists—Westernized officers, freemasons, readers of Jefferson and Voltaire—were the disease; "Orthodoxy, Autocracy, Nationality" was the cure, a positive Russian alternative to the "abstract" rights of the French and American revolutions. For American observers, this was confirmation of European backwardness; the *North American Review* and similar organs used Nicholas's Russia as a foil for American self-congratulation. For European liberals, it was the installation of the "gendarme of Europe," the power that would crush 1848 and sustain the Holy Alliance.
- **The narrative *now*:** Nicholas I is remembered as a caricature of reaction, the tsar of the secret police and the Crimean War disaster. The Uvarov triad is occasionally cited as early Russian nationalism, but the Cold War framing has faded; Russia is no longer the permanent "other." The Decembrists, meanwhile, have been recuperated as noble precursors—martyrs whose failure was redeemed by 1917, or by 1991, depending on the teller.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the crystallization of an alternative modernity, a claim that the nineteenth century need not belong to liberalism. The ideological confrontation constructed here will structure American foreign policy assumptions through 1991 and beyond; every subsequent Russian ruler will be measured against or in reaction to this founding.

#### W-2 — Battle of Ituzaingó (February 20, 1826)
- **What happened (fact):** Argentine and Brazilian forces clash in a bloody stalemate over the Cisplatine Province, demonstrating the inability of post-colonial states to resolve border disputes through diplomacy and drawing British naval intervention.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Buenos Aires and Rio de Janeiro, this was the continuation of colonial war by post-colonial means, the inability to imagine borders not drawn by Iberian monarchs. Each side claimed the territory by right of former vice-royal jurisdiction; neither could concede without delegitimizing the very basis of independence. For Britain, the stalemate was opportunity: Admiral Sir John Poo Beresford's mediation would eventually produce the 1828 treaty creating Uruguay as a buffer state, British-sponsored and British-indebted. For Latin American liberals, this was the first demonstration that formal independence did not mean sovereignty; the British Empire remained the real arbiter.
- **The narrative *now*:** Ituzaingó barely registers in U.S. consciousness; Latin American history remains peripheral. When noted, it appears as a step toward Uruguayan independence, a "good" outcome. The British intervention is remembered (if at all) as balance-of-power statecraft, not as the inauguration of informal empire in the Southern Cone. The larger pattern—post-colonial states inheriting unresolvable borders from empires—is recognized but not traced to its contemporary consequences.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the template for post-colonial conflict throughout the Americas: territorial claims based on colonial administrative boundaries, military incapacity, great-power arbitration. The Monroe Doctrine, proclaimed the previous year, promised Americans a sphere; Ituzaingó demonstrated that the British navy would define the sphere's boundaries.

#### W-3 — Signing of the Treaty of Yandabo (February 24, 1826)
- **What happened (fact):** The First Anglo-Burmese War ends with Burma ceding Assam and Manipur to the British East India Company, securing the eastern flank of British India and tightening the global monopoly on opium production.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Calcutta and London, this was the unfortunate but necessary correction of a "barbarous" power that had threatened the northeastern frontier. The war's origins in Burmese expansion into Assam and British commercial grievances were narrated as self-defense; the enormous indemnity (one million pounds) and territorial cession as the just price of aggression. For the Court of Ava, this was catastrophe and humiliation, the first of three Anglo-Burmese wars that would end in total annexation. For British merchants, particularly the opium interest, Yandabo secured the eastern opium belt and opened new markets; the connection between the treaty and the China trade was understood if rarely avowed.
- **The narrative *now*:** The First Anglo-Burmese War appears in British memory (when it appears) as minor imperial frontier conflict, overshadowed by the Mutiny and the World Wars. In Myanmar, it is the beginning of national trauma, the first Western penetration. The opium dimension is occasionally noted by historians of the drug trade but remains marginal to both national narratives. The treaty itself is obscure; the war is remembered, if at all, as background to the Raj's eastern consolidation.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the hidden infrastructure of the nineteenth century: the military securing of narcotics monopoly, the connection between Indian opium and Chinese silver that will fund British global power and provoke the Opium Wars. The narrative of "frontier defense" here, as in North America, authorizes expansion as security.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected to expose the structural tension between the myth of national unity and the reality of fragmenting systems: the deaths of Jefferson and Adams (US-3) provide a convenient, nostalgic cover for the very real constitutional crisis ignited by the fraudulent Creek Treaty (US-1) and the rise of moral absolutism in the Temperance Society (US-2). Globally, the coronation of Nicholas I (W-1) and the Treaty of Yandabo (W-3) illustrate the hardening of imperial and autocratic structures that constrain U.S. diplomatic maneuvering, while the Battle of Ituzaingó (W-2) reveals the violent instability of the hemispheric order the U.S. claims to protect. I deliberately omitted the opening of the Erie Canal's full commercial operation (often misdated to 1825 but fully impactful in 1826) not because it lacks importance, but because its narrative of triumph obscures the more critical story of 1826: the *failure* of federal mechanisms to manage the growth it sparked, forcing reliance on the fragile, speculative private networks highlighted in the `cotton-frontier` thread.

## 2. Connections Within the Year

- **U.S. ↔ World:** The Treaty of Yandabo (W-3) and the Creek Treaty of Washington (US-1) are twin manifestations of the same imperial logic: the conversion of indigenous sovereignty into fungible real estate to secure monoculture export zones. Just as the British East India Company seized Assam to tighten its grip on opium production for the China trade, Georgia's factional elites seized Creek lands to expand the cotton frontier, relying on a shared structural mechanism where international capital demand overrides local legal autonomy and indigenous title.
- **Event ↔ Event:** The founding of the American Temperance Society (US-2) and the coronation of Nicholas I (W-1) represent divergent institutional responses to the same crisis of social atomization caused by rapid market integration. As the "slow variables" of displacement (Creek removal) and industrialization destabilized traditional community bonds in the U.S., reformers engineered voluntary association (Temperance) to impose order from below; simultaneously, as the Decembrist revolt revealed the fragility of Russian hierarchy, Nicholas I imposed order from above through the doctrine of "Orthodoxy, Autocracy, and Nationality." Both are attempts to manufacture social cohesion in an era where old structures were dissolving under economic pressure.
- **Invisible / structural (shared hidden drivers):** The underlying driver connecting the Battle of Ituzaingó (W-2), the Creek Treaty, and the death of Jefferson/Adams (US-3) is the *crisis of post-colonial border definition*. Whether in the Rio de la Plata, the American Southeast, or the ideological borders of the new republics, the transition from revolutionary war to stable statehood failed to produce diplomatic mechanisms for conflict resolution. Instead, these zones defaulted to military stalemate or forced cession, revealing that the "Republic of Letters" inherited by 1826 lacked the institutional architecture to manage the territorial contradictions it had unleashed, leaving violence as the primary arbiter of property rights.

## 3. The Debate

### Round 1 — Opening Interpretations (uninterrupted)

#### 🜂 Herald
1826 is the year the American experiment chooses its ghost. The simultaneous deaths of Jefferson and Adams on the nation's semicentennial do not merely close the revolutionary generation; they consecrate a particular memory of it, one that the living desperately need. Consider the temporal geometry: fifty years to the day from the proclaimed birth of independence, the two remaining founders expire in mutual unknowing, their last words already becoming liturgy. This is not coincidence managed by providence; it is coincidence managed by narrative demand. The nation in 1826 is not the nation of 1776. The Missouri crisis has revealed that the republic can fracture; the tariff divides section from section; the Bank controversy looms. The "Era of Good Feelings" was always a press invention, and by 1826 the presses know it. What the synchronized death supplies is a story of unity so overpowering that its factual fragility becomes irrelevant. Jefferson and Adams had not spoken for a decade after 1801; their reconciliation was epistolary, private, carefully performed for posterity. Their political visions were antagonistic: Jefferson's empire of liberty versus Adams's fear of democratic excess, the Virginia agrarian versus the Massachusetts commercial republican. But in death they are merged into "the Founders," a collective singular that will be invoked against every subsequent division. The narrative work is immediate and enduring. Daniel Webster's Bunker Hill oration the following year, Edward Everett's career of commemorative eloquence, the entire culture of July Fourth oratory—these draw their authority from 1826. The "mystic chords of memory" Lincoln would invoke in 1861 are strung in this year, and they are strung across an abyss. The inheritance for the present is the still-operative assumption that the Founding was singular and unanimous, that disagreement with contemporary opponents is defensible only if framed as fidelity to a unified origin. The originalist jurisprudence of our own time, the "what would the Founders think" of political rhetoric, descends from this consecration. We have never stopped needing 1826 to have happened as it was narrated.

The Creek Treaty of Washington operates in the same register of necessary fiction, though its myth serves narrower interests. The official story then was of willing sellers and inevitable progress; the National Council's repudiation, the assassinations of the signers, the ensuing civil war—these were narrated as confirmation of Indian incapacity for self-rule rather than as evidence of treaty fraud. What contemporaries believed this event meant was that the "civilization" policy had worked: Indians educated in Anglo-American ways would recognize the superiority of those ways and surrender their land. The Treaty of Washington was the proof text. What we inherit is the sanitized version in which the Trail of Tears emerges from abstract federal policy, not from specific local conspiracies like this one, in which federal commissioners collaborated with a minority faction to produce a document that the "civilized" Creek constitutional structure explicitly forbade. The narrative work here is the construction of "consent" where none existed, the transformation of internal colonial manipulation into the appearance of indigenous choice. The present inheritance is the persistent pattern of recognizing tribal governments only when they produce outcomes acceptable to the United States, the cycle of treaty-making and treaty-breaking that continues into the twenty-first century. The American Temperance Society, founded weeks later, represents the domestic application of the same logic: the reformer's certainty that his moral vision is universal, and that social engineering in its service is not coercion but liberation. The ATS narrative then was of Christian duty and republican virtue; the drunkard was a threat to ordered liberty, and total abstinence was the test of citizenship in the moral community. The reformers believed, genuinely, that they were saving souls and saving the nation. What they also did—what the narrative then partially obscured and the narrative now largely forgets—was to construct a machinery of cultural surveillance and class discipline that targeted Irish and German immigrants, Catholics, and the urban working class. The temperance narrative was ethnic warfare by other means, the Anglo-Protestant elite's response to demographic transformation. The "moral statistics" that the ATS pioneered—the quantification of social pathology—would become the language of every subsequent reform movement, including those the ATS founders would have abhorred. The inheritance is double: the progressive assumption that social problems yield to organized moral pressure, and the conservative recoil from that assumption as elite imposition. Both sides of our culture war descend from 1826.

Beyond the Atlantic, the coronation of Nicholas I establishes the antithetical narrative that will structure American self-understanding for a century and a half. "Orthodoxy, Autocracy, Nationality" was not merely Russian state doctrine; it was a positive argument against the American proposition, a claim that order and faith required the very structures Americans had rejected. Contemporaries on both sides understood the confrontation. American observers used Nicholas's Russia as a foil; Russian ideologues used American democracy as a warning. The Decembrist revolt, suppressed with the hangman's noose that Nicholas would commute to Siberian exile, was the last gasp of the Westernizing aristocracy; the coronation was the installation of a rival modernity. The narrative now has softened; the Cold War frame in which every Russian ruler was measured against Nicholas's absolutism has relaxed, and Putin's Russia is analyzed through other lenses. But the binary construction—American liberty versus Russian despotism—remains available for mobilization, as we have seen in every confrontation since 1945. The present inheritance is the ease with which Americans revert to this template, the assumption that Russian political culture is essentially and permanently authoritarian

#### 🜄 Heretic
Let me begin by giving the received story its due, because it is a beautiful story, and its beauty is precisely what makes it dangerous.

The year 1826, in the version that reaches us, is a year of providential closure and moral awakening. Jefferson and Adams die on the Fourth of July, and the coincidence is so perfect that it cannot be accident—it must be meaning. The revolutionary generation exits together, their reconciliation sealed by simultaneity, their quarrels dissolved into a shared apotheosis. The nation pauses to mourn, and in mourning, rediscovers its unity. That same year, the American Temperance Society forms in Boston, and decent people begin organizing against the scourge of drink—a moral reform movement that will, in time, transform the nation's character. The Creek Treaty is signed, and Georgia finally secures the lands that, by every logic of civilization and progress, belong to its jurisdiction. In Russia, a new Tsar is crowned, and the contrast is clarifying: autocracy there, republicanism here. In South America, the new republics fight their border wars, and the hemisphere learns that independence is difficult. In Burma, the British East India Company concludes a war, and the map of empire settles into its rational shape. The year is, in sum, a hinge: the old guard departs, the new nation organizes its virtues, and the world arranges itself into the patterns that will define the century.

Now let us look at what this story buries, and who paid for it.

Start with the Creek Treaty of Washington, because it is the event that most efficiently exposes the machinery. The official narrative—the one Georgia's delegation carried to Washington, the one the Adams administration permitted—holds that the Creek Nation, through its legitimate representatives, freely ceded its remaining lands in Georgia and agreed to remove west of the Mississippi. This is the story that enabled the surveyors to move in, that justified the forced dispossession, that laundered theft into contract. Here is what the story omits. The treaty was signed by a factional minority led by William McIntosh, a Creek leader who had been cultivated for precisely this purpose by federal agents and Georgia speculators. McIntosh was not the Creek Nation; he was a man who had been paid, repeatedly, for his compliance—land grants, cash, protection. The Creek National Council had passed a law explicitly making unauthorized land cessions punishable by death. McIntosh knew this. He signed anyway. The Adams administration knew this. They accepted the treaty anyway. Within months, McIntosh was executed by his own people—a lawful execution under Creek law, which the United States simply ignored, because the dead signatory was more useful than the living one. The treaty stood. The land was taken. The Creek civil war that followed—the violence between the McIntosh faction and the National Council—was not a tragic tribal conflict; it was the predictable consequence of a deliberate strategy of bribery, faction-splitting, and legal nullification that the United States had perfected over four decades of Indian diplomacy. The rhetorical cover was consent. The operational reality was: find someone who will sign, call him a chief, and enforce the signature with troops if necessary. The gap between word and deed is not a gap; it is the mechanism itself.

Now ask the Heretic's first question: *cui bono*? Who gained? Georgia's planters and land speculators gained—the same class that would, within a decade, orchestrate the Trail of Tears. The federal government gained a resolution to a jurisdictional irritant. The cotton economy gained new soil. Who was silenced? The Creek majority, whose laws were violated, whose leaders were murdered, whose removal was narrated as voluntary. And note the secondary gain: the story of the treaty as legitimate agreement absolves the present. If the Creeks consented, then the dispossession was not theft—it was transaction. The comfortable version lets us avoid feeling responsible for the fact that the United States systematically manufactured the "consent" it then cited as justification.

The Temperance Society belongs in the same analysis, though the mechanism is subtler. The received story treats the founding of the American Temperance Society as a moment of moral awakening—evangelical reformers, alarmed by the social costs of alcohol, organizing for the common good. And certainly there were drunkards, and certainly there were ruined families, and certainly some of the reformers were sincere. But look at who founded the Society and who it served. The leadership was drawn from the same New England mercantile and clerical elite that was, in this same decade, consolidating control over the industrializing labor force. The temperance movement was not merely a moral crusade; it was a project of labor discipline. The factory owner who wanted sober, punctual, predictable workers found in temperance an ideology that transformed his economic interest into a virtue. The evangelical minister who worried about the unwashed democracy of the tavern found in temperance a mechanism of social control. The middle-class woman whose husband's wages disappeared into the saloon found in temperance a language for domestic grievance—but that language was quickly captured by male elites who used it to discipline working-class men rather than to empower women. The movement's shift from "moderation" to "total abstinence" is telling: moderation is a negotiation; abstinence is a command. The American Temperance Society was, from its founding, a vehicle for the imposition of bourgeois norms on a resistant population, and its rhetoric of moral uplift concealed a project of class discipline. Who gained? Employers, clerics, the respectable classes who could afford to drink in private. Who was silenced? The immigrant laborer for whom the saloon was not a vice but a community center, a hiring hall, a political club. The comfortable version lets us avoid feeling responsible for the fact that American moral reform has always been, in significant measure, a way of making the powerful feel virtuous while disciplining the powerless.

The deaths of Jefferson and Adams require the sharpest scalpel, because here the myth-making is most complete and most consequential. The Herald has already described the narrative work: the coincidence consecrates unity, the quarrels are dissolved, the Founders become a collective singular. I will not dispute this; I will ask what the myth was *for*. It was for suppressing the fact that the American republic in 1826 was not a single nation with a shared origin but two increasingly distinct civilizations held together by a federal architecture that was cracking under the strain. Jefferson died in Virginia, a slaveholder who had written that slavery was a "fire bell in the night" and then done precisely nothing structural to extinguish it, whose "empire of liberty" was built on the labor of the people he owned. Adams died in Massachusetts, a man who had feared the slave power's political dominance and then made his peace with it. Their deaths on the Fourth of July allowed the nation to mourn them together rather than to reckon with what they had left unresolved. The myth of the unified Founding was not a harmless sentiment; it was an active suppression of the sectional conflict that would, in thirty-five years, kill six hundred thousand Americans. The originalist jurisprudence the Herald mentions is not merely descended from this consecration—it is a continuation of the same work. To invoke "the Founders" as a singular authority is to erase the fact that they disagreed fundamentally about what they had made, and that some of them—Jefferson chief among them—understood that their compromises were time bombs. The comfortable version lets us avoid feeling responsible for the fact that the veneration of 1776 has always been, in part, a refusal to face 1861.

The three world events confirm the pattern, though I must be briefer. Nicholas I's coronation and the doctrine of "Orthodoxy, Autocracy, and Nationality" are typically narrated as the antithesis of American republicanism—the dark mirror that clarifies our own liberty. But this framing serves a function: it allows the United States to define itself against autocracy while practicing its own forms of domination. The same year that Nicholas I crushed the Decembrists and announced his trinity of reaction, the United States was enforcing the Creek removal, expanding slavery into new territories, and constructing its own racial-nationalist ideology. The contrast is real but it is also alibi: we are not like *that*, so what we do cannot be tyranny.

The Battle of Ituzaingó and the Treaty of Yandabo are the year's lessons in how post-colonial violence and imperial expansion are narrated as regrettable necessities. The Argentine-Brazilian war over the Cisplatine Province is a border dispute—but "border dispute" is the language that naturalizes the fact that both states were built on the violent incorporation of indigenous and African populations whose claims to the land preceded and exceeded either republic's. The British naval intervention that followed was not neutral peacekeeping; it was the assertion of imperial interest under the cover of stability. The Treaty of Yandabo, meanwhile, secured the British East India Company's eastern flank and tightened its control over opium production—the same opium that would, within a decade, become the instrument of British penetration into China. The First Anglo-Burmese War is narrated as a colonial skirmish; it was, in fact, a critical link in the global infrastructure of drug trafficking that would, by the 1840s, produce two wars against China to force open its markets. The comfortable version—"the map of empire settles into its rational shape"—is the language of the victor, and it erases the Burmese dead, the Assamese dispossessed, and the Chinese addicts whose suffering was already being engineered in 1826.

The year's inheritance for the present-day United States is specific and uncomfortable. We are the beneficiaries of the Creek dispossession; the land under our feet was taken by the legal logic perfected in the Treaty of Washington. We are the inheritors of a temperance tradition that taught the American middle class to mistake its own class interests for universal morality. We are governed by a political culture that still invokes the unified Founders to foreclose argument, and we live in a nation whose self-image as the antithesis of autocracy has always coexisted with the practice of domination. 1826 is not the year the American experiment chose its ghost. It is the year the ghost was manufactured, and the manufacturing process is still running.

#### 🜃 Cartographer
The year 1826 is not a collection of discrete headlines but a stress test of the structural containers built during the revolutionary era. The map reveals a world where the slow, heavy variables of geography and demography have finally caught up with the fast variables of political ideology. The Revolutionary generation believed they had created systems of liberty that would self-regulate; the events of 1826 demonstrate that without a mechanism to constrain the hunger for land and the volatility of labor, those systems immediately calcify into engines of extraction or fracture into civil conflict.

Consider the spatial logic binding US-1 and W-3. The Treaty of Yandabo did not merely end a war in Burma; it secured the eastern flank of the British Empire's most lucrative narcotic enterprise. This global demand for opium—and the parallel global demand for cotton—created a price signal that traveled instantly to the frontier of Georgia. The Creek Treaty of Washington was not an isolated diplomatic failure; it was the local execution of a global imperative. When a factional minority of Creeks signed away their nation's remaining land, they were acting as the weak link in a chain of pressure applied by the expanding capitalist core. The structure here is clear: where high-value export crops meet indigenous land tenure, the legal superstructure (even the Creek National Council's laws) collapses under the weight of economic gravity. The "contingency" of which specific leaders signed is real, but the *availability* of the outcome—the total cession of land—was determined decades prior by the integration of these regions into the Atlantic market.

This integration produces a secondary shockwave visible in US-2 and W-1. As the map of production expands, the map of social cohesion shrinks. The displacement of the Creeks and the encroachment of market relations into every corner of life dissolved the organic, face-to-face constraints that had previously regulated behavior. In the United States, this vacuum was filled by the American Temperance Society, an attempt to build a new, voluntary architecture of control to replace the lost communal norms. It is a structural admission that the free market, left to its own devices, produces social chaos (drunkenness, instability) that threatens labor discipline. Across the ocean, Nicholas I faced the same dissolution of order but diagnosed it as a failure of vertical authority rather than horizontal association. His coronation doctrine was a direct structural counter-move to the Decembrists, asserting that if the state does not rigidly define identity (Orthodoxy, Autocracy, Nationality), the empire will fragment. Both the Boston reformer and the Russian Tsar were reacting to the same centrifugal force: the inability of traditional hierarchies to contain the energies released by modernization.

The death of Jefferson and Adams on July 4 serves as the symbolic capstone to this structural shift. Their simultaneous passing allowed the living generation to mythologize the Revolution as a completed, perfect project, thereby obscuring the unresolved contradictions they left behind. The "through-line" here is dangerous: by treating the Founders as demigods rather than architects of an unfinished and flawed system, the political culture of 1826 insulated itself from the necessary work of institutional repair. They buried the men but canonized the contradictions—specifically the contradiction between republican liberty and slave-based expansion, and between federal authority and local sovereignty.
