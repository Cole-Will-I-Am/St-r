## 0. Context Carried Forward

- **Thread — `missouri-boundary`:** The dormant volcano has begun to emit smoke; while the federal pledge regarding free Black citizens remains technically intact, Southern state legislatures are actively codifying "police powers" to harass and imprison any free Black person entering the state, testing whether the Constitution's privileges and immunities clause can survive localized administrative suffocation.
- **Thread — `cotton-frontier`:** Capital migration accelerates as the panic of 1819 recedes; the opening of the Santa Fe Trail and the stabilization of Mexican independence have redirected speculative fervor away from seaboard banks toward interior land grants, creating a debtor class in the Southwest whose economic survival now depends on the expansion of slavery into territories where its legal status is ambiguous.
- **Thread — `monroe-doctrine-seed`:** The diplomatic window is narrowing; British merchants have effectively colonized the trade networks of Latin America without political commitment, forcing the Adams administration to choose between immediate recognition of new republics to secure market share or a delay that cedes the architectural framework of the hemisphere to London.
- **Thread — `judicial-centralization`:** The friction has hardened into open hostility; following *Cohens v. Virginia*, the Virginia legislature is no longer merely debating nullification but drafting operational resolutions to ignore Supreme Court mandates, setting a direct collision course between Marshall's docket and the sovereignty claims of the Old Dominion.
- **Thread — `imperial-vacuum`:** The administrative void in the borderlands has deepened into anarchy; with Spain expelled and Mexico unable to project power north of the Rio Grande, U.S. settlers are operating as a de facto government, establishing "squatter sovereignty" patterns that will soon demand either military intervention or diplomatic annexation.
- **Thread — `haitian-contagion`:** (New carry-forward from 1821 dynamics) The successful consolidation of Haitian rule over the entire island of Hispaniola creates a psychological and material firewall against U.S. southern expansion, intensifying Southern paranoia about slave revolts while simultaneously offering a refuge that undermines the domestic logic of permanent bondage.

## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — Denmark Vesey Conspiracy Trial (June–July 1822)
- **What happened (fact):** A special court in Charleston convicts and executes 35 enslaved men for plotting a massive uprising, triggering a wave of restrictive Black Codes across the South.
- **The narrative *then*:** Charleston's white citizenry lived through the summer of 1822 in a state of evangelical terror and providential relief. They believed they had narrowly averted an apocalypse—Vesey's planned uprising was whispered to involve thousands, to include house servants who knew their masters' sleeping arrangements, to promise fire and blade in every quarter. The special court became a theater of purification; each confession extracted, each body hanged, each head displayed on the workhouse wall, testified to God's mercy in revealing the plot before it bloomed. The *Charleston Courier* spoke of "the monster" and the "infernal scheme," while Governor Thomas Bennett privately worried about judicial overreach but publicly endorsed the verdict of necessity. For white Charleston, Vesey became a type: the educated, freed-status Black man who had tasted freedom and returned to destroy his benefactors. The narrative was one of deliverance through vigilance, a covenant renewed in blood.
- **The narrative *now*:** The textbook version, when it appears at all, labels Vesey's conspiracy "perhaps the most elaborate slave revolt ever planned" and pivots quickly to the tightening of slave codes as evidence of antebellum paranoia. The modern story is more skeptical of the plot's scale—historians have debated whether Vesey constructed a genuine military apparatus or whether white panic manufactured one through coerced testimony. What survives in public memory is the code, not the man; the restrictions, not the aspiration. Vesey enters curricula as a martyr of resistance only in specialized contexts; more commonly, he is absent, and the narrative of 1822 belongs to the slow march toward the 1850s. The emotional work the story once did—collective gratitude for racial order—has been replaced by a story about the pathology of slaveholder fear, but this itself performs work: it lets the present imagine itself as cured of such terrors.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the year the South chose surveillance over stability. The Black Codes that followed did not merely regulate; they constructed a narrative of permanent Black threat that would outlast slavery itself. Vesey's trial is the forge of the antebellum South's self-understanding.

#### US-2 — American Colonization Society Founding of Liberia (January 7, 1822)
- **What happened (fact):** The first group of freed African Americans arrives at Cape Mesurado to establish a settlement, institutionalizing the removal of free Blacks as a solution to domestic racial tension.
- **The narrative *then*:** For the ACS and its supporters—Henry Clay, Francis Scott Key, Bushrod Washington—the arrival at Cape Mesurado was a providential convergence: philanthropy and self-interest married under the banner of republican virtue. The *National Intelligencer* celebrated "the happy means of disposing of a description of persons whose situation among us is neither comfortable to themselves, nor, in many respects, consistent with our own welfare." For free Blacks who emigrated, the narrative was more fractured: some heard genuine promise of citizenship denied at home, a Liberia where Blackness was not a mark of exclusion. Others recognized coercion dressed in benevolence. The African Repository constructed a story of pioneer heroism, of jungle converted to Christian settlement. The deeper then-narrative was ontological: America was white, and Black presence was a temporary aberration to be gently undone. Liberia was the moral alibi for a society that could not imagine coexistence.
- **The narrative *now*:** Liberia appears in textbooks as a "controversial" solution, a footnote between abolitionism and the Civil War. The modern story emphasizes the settler-native violence that followed, the Americo-Liberian domination of indigenous populations, the colony's function as a safety valve for slaveholder anxiety. We tell it now as a cautionary tale of paternalism, or more often we do not tell it at all. The ACS has been absorbed into a genealogy of white liberal racism, but the narrative work it performed—allowing the North to feel engaged with slavery without confronting it, allowing the South to imagine benevolent separation—finds eerie echoes in later removals and urban renewals. The present inherits not Liberia's existence but its logic: the persistent American fantasy that spatial separation can resolve racial contradiction.
- **Why it earns a slot:** 1822 institutionalizes removal as policy. The ACS settlement is the first federal-adjacent experiment in solving race by export, and its assumptions persist in every later debate about where Black Americans properly belong.

#### US-3 — Recognition of Latin American Republics (March 8, 1822)
- **What happened (fact):** President Monroe sends a message to Congress recommending formal recognition of Buenos Aires, Chile, Peru, Colombia, and Mexico, breaking the European diplomatic embargo on the hemisphere.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Monroe and his Secretary of State Adams, recognition was a calculated gamble dressed in republican principle. The message to Congress spoke of "the general interest of mankind" and the right of peoples to choose their own governments, but the diplomatic correspondence revealed anxiety: Would Britain, already meddling, seize the vacuum? Would France's restored Bourbons intervene? The narrative in the Republican press was triumphal—American sympathy for sister republics, the New World shaking off the Old. Federalist critics warned of entanglement with unstable regimes. For Latin American agents in Washington, recognition was the difference between existence and extinction; José Manuel Zozaya, Mexican minister, had lobbied since 1819. The then-narrative was hemispheric solidarity, but the emotional core was competitive relief: Europe had its Congress system, America would have its own geometry, and recognition was the first line drawn.
- **The narrative *now*:** The 1822 recognition is absorbed into the pre-history of the Monroe Doctrine (1823), stripped of its specific drama. We tell it as inevitable—of course the United States recognized its neighbors—rather than as a contingent break with European consensus. The modern narrative emphasizes commercial ambition and strategic anxiety more than republican solidarity, and it notes the asymmetry: the U.S. recognized but did not aid, extracting recognition's prestige while avoiding its costs. The story now serves as origin myth for hemispheric hegemony, with 1822 as the first assertion of American primacy. What we lose in this telling is the genuine uncertainty of the moment, the sense that the hemisphere's architecture was still unbuilt.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the year the United States begins acting as a hemispheric power with a story to tell about what the Americas mean. The recognition message is the first draft of American exceptionalism applied to foreign policy.

#### W-1 — Greek War of Independence Escalation (April 1822)
- **What happened (fact):** The First National Assembly at Epidaurus declares Greek independence from the Ottoman Empire, igniting a humanitarian crisis that fractures the Holy Alliance's unity.
- **The narrative *then*:** For European liberals and Romantics, Epidaurus was Marathon reborn, Thermopylae rekindled. The declaration arrived in London and Paris through dispatches that emphasized classical topography—this was Sparta, this was Athens, the sacred soil where freedom had once lived. For the Holy Alliance, the narrative was more threatening: Greek revolt was not merely rebellion but a crack in the post-Napoleonic order, a precedent for subject peoples everywhere. Metternich saw contagion; Alexander I, Greek by name and Orthodox by faith, experienced private anguish that would soon become public paralysis. The then-narrative in Western capitals was already bifurcated: governments spoke of stability, publics spoke of crucified Christianity. The massacre at Chios (March 1822), reported by Byron and others, provided the atrocity imagery that made neutrality morally costly. Epidaurus gave the revolt a government; the humanitarian narrative gave it a constituency.
- **The narrative *now:*:** The Greek War of Independence has become the prototype of liberal interventionism, studied for how atrocity reports mobilize distant publics. Epidaurus itself is rarely detailed; the narrative centers on Byron, on Chios, on the eventual Great Power intervention. We remember Greek independence as romantic success, not as the decade of civil war and great-power manipulation it actually was. The modern story performs selective amnesia about the philhellenic movement's combination of genuine idealism and cultural imperialism—Greece as Europe's origin myth, to be saved from the Oriental Other. The inheritance is the template: humanitarian crisis, media mobilization, eventual armed rescue, messy aftermath forgotten.
- **Why it earns a slot:** 1822 is the year humanitarian narrative becomes a geopolitical weapon. The Greek declaration and its reception establish the pattern by which Western publics will be mobilized for distant causes.

#### W-2 — Death of Lord Byron in Missolonghi (April 19, 1822)
- **What happened (fact):** The prominent Romantic poet dies in Greece while supporting the revolution, transforming a regional conflict into a transatlantic cause célèbre that pressures Western governments to intervene.
- **The narrative *then*:** Byron's death was received as secular martyrdom, the most famous death in Europe since Napoleon. The poet who had embodied Romantic rebellion—against convention, against morality, against his own body—had found its fitting terminus in a malarial Greek marsh. The letters that preceded his death, particularly his final missives about Greek liberty, were published and republished. Trelawney's accounts constructed a deathbed scene of classical stoicism. For British radicals, Byron's end was vindication: the aristocrat who had chosen the people, the skeptic who had found faith in action. For governments, it was nuisance—a popular saint whose cult demanded policy response. The then-narrative was explicitly hagiographic; Byron had died for Greece as Christ for man, and Missolonghi became a pilgrimage site before his body reached England. The emotional work was immense: it made intervention feel personal, a debt to the dead.
- **The narrative *now*:** Byron survives in curricula as poetic genius and biographical scandal; his Greek sojourn is a colorful episode, his death a romantic punctuation. We have lost the sense of his death as a political event, a moment when private celebrity became public pressure. The modern narrative, when it addresses Missolonghi at all, tends toward debunking: Byron was militarily useless, his death from fever ignoble, the philhellenic cult overwrought. What we inherit is the mechanism more than the man—the celebrity activist, the mediated death, the cause made flesh. Byron's Missolonghi is the template for every later figure whose body becomes argument: Livingstone in Africa, Che in Bolivia, the White Helmets in Syria. The specific Greek context has faded; the narrative technology endures.
- **Why it earns a slot:** No single death in 1822 did more political work. Byron's Missolonghi is the invention of the celebrity martyr as foreign policy instrument, a narrative technology the present has never abandoned.

#### W-3 — Brazilian Declaration of Independence (September 7, 1822)
- **What happened (fact):** Prince Pedro proclaims Brazil's independence from Portugal on the banks of the Ipiranga River, preserving the monarchy and slave system while altering the balance of power in South America.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Brazilian elites, the Grito do Ipiranga was a family quarrel that preserved the furniture. Pedro's cry—"Independence or Death!"—was performed for a small entourage, then amplified into founding myth. The narrative then was deliberately ambiguous: independence from Portugal, yes, but not revolution; separation, but not rupture. The monarchy continued, slavery continued, the administrative class remained. For Portuguese loyalists, it was betrayal by a spoiled prince; for Spanish American republicans, it was independence's conservative double, a mockery of their own sacrifices. For Britain, it was commercial opportunity; Canning would soon recognize and protect. The then-narrative's genius was its denial of its own radicalism: Brazil became independent as if by accident, by the personal decision of a prince who would remain emperor. This allowed slaveholders to have sovereignty without contagion, to join the age of revolution without its social dangers.
- **The narrative *now*:** Brazil's independence is remembered as exceptional—the only American independence that produced a monarchy, that preserved slavery until 1888. The modern narrative emphasizes this exceptionalism as pathology: Brazil's incomplete rupture with colonial structures, the long shadow of patrimonialism, the racial democracy myth that would later obscure slavery's legacy. The Ipiranga moment is celebrated nationally but critically examined: the preservation of slavery, the exclusion of indigenous and African populations from the founding "we." For the United States, Brazilian independence enters only marginally, as a counter-example to hemispheric republicanism. The narrative work it performs now is negative: it confirms American self-image as the hemisphere's genuine revolutionary, however compromised that revolution was. What we lose is the genuine ingenuity of the Brazilian solution, the way it solved problems American independence had not.
- **Why it earns a slot:** 1822 is the year the Americas' independence movements reveal their divergent possibilities. Brazil's monarchical independence is the road not taken for the United States, and its study illuminates what American republicanism chose and what it cost.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected because they collectively expose the structural tension between the ideology of liberty and the mechanics of empire in 1822: the Vesey trial and the founding of Liberia represent the dual American response to the threat of Black agency (brutal suppression vs. removal), directly feeding the `missouri-boundary` thread; the recognition of Latin American republics and Brazil's separation are the kinetic realization of the `monroe-doctrine-seed` and `imperial-vacuum` threads, locking the U.S. into a hemispheric strategy based on commercial access rather than democratic solidarity. I deliberately omitted the detailed passage of the Tariff of 1822 (which was largely preparatory for the major 1824 tariff) because its structural impact was less immediate than the geopolitical realignment in the Americas and the internal security crisis in Charleston; focusing on the tariff would obscure the more vital narrative of how racial fear and imperial ambition drove policy this year.

## 2. Connections Within the Year

- **U.S. ↔ World:** The recognition of Latin American republics (US-3) and the Greek declaration (W-1) are not parallel moral awakenings but divergent applications of the same structural imperative: the collapse of the Iberian and Ottoman empires created power vacuums that Atlantic powers rushed to fill, yet the U.S. embraced republican fragmentation in the Americas while the Holy Alliance fractured over monarchical preservation in Europe, revealing that "liberty" was a geopolitical tool contingent on whether the new state preserved or threatened existing property regimes.
- **Event ↔ Event:** The arrival of freed Blacks in Liberia (US-2) and the execution of Denmark Vesey's co-conspirators in Charleston (US-1) form a single feedback loop of racial containment; the terror of the failed uprising accelerated the institutionalization of colonization as a "safety valve," proving that the removal of free Black people was the necessary corollary to the tightening of slave codes, while Brazil's independence (W-3) under a monarchy secured the hemisphere's largest slave economy, effectively encircling the U.S. South with friendly slave-holding states and reducing the existential fear of external emancipation models.
- **Invisible / structural (shared hidden drivers):** The primary driver is the *demographic pressure of the enslaved population* colliding with the *geographic limits of imperial control*; in 1822, the slow variable of explosive Black population growth in the Americas made the status quo untenable without either massive violence (Charleston), exportation (Liberia), or constitutional re-engineering that preserved slavery under new flags (Brazil, Latin America), while the fast variable of Romantic idealism (Byron, Greece) masked the underlying economic calculation that independence movements were only viable where they did not threaten the transatlantic capital flows dependent on coerced labor.

## 3. The Debate

### Round 1 — Opening Interpretations (uninterrupted)

#### 🜂 Herald
1822 is the year the modern world learns to tell stories about itself with lethal precision. I do not mean that the events of this year are more violent than others; I mean that the narratives constructed around them—the mechanisms by which belief is forged, transmitted, and weaponized—achieve a maturity that will define the century.

Consider the symmetry. In Charleston, a conspiracy trial manufactures a myth of Black danger that justifies an entire society's architecture of control; the story is more important than the plot's reality, and the story wins. In Missolonghi, a poet's death manufactures a myth of romantic sacrifice that pressures governments toward intervention; the body becomes text, the text becomes policy. At Cape Mesurado, a settlement manufactures a myth of benevolent separation that allows a nation to defer its racial contradiction indefinitely; the colony is less place than promise, the promise less honest than it appears. At Epidaurus and Ipiranga, declarations of independence manufacture myths of national origin that will outlive their authors' intentions; the performed moment becomes the eternal return. And in Washington, a president's message manufactures a myth of hemispheric destiny that will eventually be called doctrine; the recognition of others is really self-recognition, the first draft of a story the United States will tell until it believes it.

What binds these events is not their content but their narrative architecture. Each is a moment when something happens and something else is made to mean—a meaning that will be revised, polished, and deployed. The Herald's lens insists that we take this seriously as historical force. The Charleston court believed it was saving civilization; we believe it was constructing white supremacy. Both beliefs are true, but the first belief moved men to hang their neighbors, and that movement is the fact we must explain. Byron's contemporaries believed they had witnessed sanctity; we believe we have witnessed celebrity culture's political birth. Both are true, but the first belief moved Palmerston toward the navy, and that movement is the fact we must explain. The American Colonization Society believed it was solving a problem humanely; we believe it was exporting an intractable contradiction. Both are true, but the first belief moved ships across the Atlantic, moved free Black men and women to gamble their lives on a fever coast, and that movement is the fact we must explain.

The gap between then and now is not error to be corrected but sediment to be read. The then-narrative of 1822 is everywhere optimistic about stories: a good enough story can found a nation, redeem a poet, civilize a wilderness, save a Greece. The now-narrative is everywhere suspicious: we have learned to read for power, for exclusion, for the buried victim. This suspicion is necessary and insufficient. Necessary because the stories of 1822 did serve power, did exclude, did bury victims by the thousand. Insufficient because to stop at suspicion is to miss the genuine hope that moved men and women, the hope that is itself a historical fact no less

#### 🜄 Heretic
The Herald’s opening is itself a beautiful story, and that is precisely the problem. It offers us narrative architecture, symmetry, the elegant machinery of myth-making—and in doing so, it performs the very operation it claims to anatomize. It gives us a year of meanings, of beliefs sincerely held, of stories that “moved men.” But notice what this framing omits: the bodies. The money. The land titles. The whips. The Herald asks us to marvel at how the Charleston court *believed* it was saving civilization. I ask: who owned that court? Who sat on its benches? Who signed the warrants, and what did they stand to lose if the enslaved population of Charleston—which outnumbered whites in the surrounding lowcountry—ceased to be property?

Let us steelman the received narrative first, because it is powerful and it is not entirely false. The standard account of 1822 runs like this: a year of liberation movements, of new nations born, of a hemisphere throwing off European dominion, of a poet dying for Greek freedom, of a fledgling American project to solve the problem of slavery through colonization. It is a year of idealism, however flawed, however incomplete. The Denmark Vesey trial, in this telling, was a legitimate if harsh response to a genuine threat; the colonization of Liberia was a well-meaning if paternalistic attempt at racial reconciliation; the recognition of Latin American republics was a principled stand for self-determination; the Greek declaration was the rebirth of democracy; Byron’s death was martyrdom; Brazilian independence was national awakening. This story has the virtue of taking historical actors at their word, and the Herald is right that their words mattered. But the Heretic’s obligation is to ask what those words were *for*, and who paid for them.

Begin with Charleston, because Charleston is the year’s rawest exposure of the gap between rhetoric and reality. The official story of the Vesey conspiracy—and I use the word “conspiracy” advisedly, because we must flag here a contested fact: the extent to which a coordinated plot actually existed versus the extent to which it was extracted, embellished, or invented by a terrified white elite—the official story is that a free Black carpenter named Denmark Vesey, inspired by the Bible and the Haitian Revolution, organized thousands of enslaved men across the Charleston district for a coordinated uprising on Bastille Day. The court, a special tribunal of seven white men operating in secret, heard testimony, convicted 131 people, hanged 35, and exiled dozens more. The narrative that emerged from this proceeding was one of Black barbarism barely restrained, of a white civilization that had shown mercy by executing only the ringleaders, of the necessity of ever-tighter controls on both enslaved and free Black populations.

Now let us follow the money, the power, and the silence. The court was composed of prominent slaveholders. The evidence consisted almost entirely of testimony extracted under conditions we would now call torture—the lash, solitary confinement, the threat of execution—and much of it came from witnesses who were themselves facing the gallows and offered leniency in exchange for naming names. The trial records were sealed or selectively published. The executions were public, theatrical, designed as terror. And the aftermath was not grief or reflection but legislation: the Negro Seamen Act, which imprisoned free Black sailors who docked in Charleston; tighter restrictions on manumission; bans on Black literacy; a comprehensive regime of surveillance that would become the model for the antebellum South. Cui bono? The planter class, which had just faced a mortal challenge to its labor system and used the crisis—real, exaggerated, or fabricated—to fortify that system for another forty years. The silence in the archive is the voice of the accused, who left no testimony that was not filtered through their interrogators. We do not know what Vesey actually planned, or said, or believed. We know only what the court needed him to have planned, said, and believed in order to justify its own existence. The comfortable version of this story—a dangerous plot, a necessary crackdown—lets us avoid feeling responsible for the fact that the legal apparatus of the United States has, from its earliest decades, been perfectly capable of manufacturing the threat it then punishes.

Now turn to Liberia, which the Herald frames as a myth of benevolent separation. Steelman it: the American Colonization Society, founded by prominent men including Henry Clay and Francis Scott Key, argued that slavery was a moral evil but that free Blacks could never be integrated into white American society. Colonization was the humane solution—a return to Africa, a Christianizing mission, a new start. In January 1822, the first ship of settlers, the *Elizabeth*, arrived at Cape Mesurado, a stretch of coast purchased (under what conditions of consent we will examine) from local rulers. These settlers, mostly free Blacks from the American South, founded Monrovia and began building a society that would eventually become the Republic of Liberia. The ACS presented this as philanthropy, as a solution to a seemingly intractable problem, as a project that benefited everyone: whites relieved of a feared population, Blacks given their own nation.

Now the Heretic’s lens. The ACS was overwhelmingly funded by slaveholders and their political allies. Its largest donors included men who had made fortunes in the domestic slave trade. The organization’s public rhetoric emphasized benevolence and uplift; its private correspondence, which we can read, reveals a different calculus. Free Blacks were a threat to the slave system simply by existing—they demonstrated that Black people could live as free laborers, they formed networks of mutual aid, they sometimes helped enslaved people escape, and in the wake of Vesey, they were seen as potential insurrectionists. Colonization was not a solution to the problem of slavery; it was a solution to the problem of free Blacks. It removed the most visible contradiction to the ideology of white supremacy. And it did so by deporting people, many of whom had been born in the United States and had no connection to Africa, to a continent they had never seen, to a coastline that was not empty but inhabited by peoples who had their own political structures, their own economies, their own claims to the land. The purchase of Cape Mesurado was conducted at gunpoint—the ACS agent Eli Ayres and the U.S. naval officer Robert Stockton trained cannons on the shore and threatened to destroy the town unless the local Dei and Bassa leaders signed over the territory. The price was roughly $300 worth of trade goods. This is not a myth of benevolent separation; this is a land grab dressed in missionary clothes, and the people who paid for it with their lives were the settlers, who died in staggering numbers from malaria and yellow fever in the first years, and the indigenous populations, who would be dispossessed and subordinated for generations. The absence in the archive is the voice of the Dei and Bassa, who did not write their account of the transaction. The comfortable version lets us remember Liberia as a flawed but noble experiment in Black self-governance. It lets us forget that it was, from its inception, a project of white racial management, executed through force and fraud, whose primary beneficiaries were the slaveholders who wanted their problem shipped overseas.

The recognition of the Latin American republics—Monroe’s message of March 8, 1822—is the event the Herald frames as the first draft of hemispheric destiny. The steelman: after years of neutrality, the United States finally extended formal diplomatic recognition to the revolutionary governments of Buenos Aires, Chile, Peru, Colombia, and Mexico, breaking with the European powers who still hoped to restore Spanish dominion. This was a principled stand for republicanism, for self-determination, for an American hemisphere free of European colonialism. It was the precursor to the Monroe Doctrine, the assertion of U.S. leadership in the Americas.

Now follow the incentives. The United States delayed recognition for years while the revolutions were uncertain, while Spain might have retaken its colonies, while American merchants hedged their bets. Recognition came in 1822 not because the principle had suddenly become clear but because the military situation had. The Spanish Empire was effectively finished on the mainland; the new republics were open for business. And business was the point. American merchants, shippers, and investors wanted access to Latin American markets without the interference of Spanish mercantilist restrictions. The recognition message was accompanied by appropriations for new naval vessels to protect American commerce in the Caribbean and the Pacific. The rhetoric of republican solidarity was genuine enough—there were sincere enthusiasts for the Latin American revolutions in the United States—but it served as the public face of a commercial expansion that would, within decades, turn into something harder: the projection of U.S. power into the hemisphere, the backing of friendly dictators, the repeated interventions in the name of stability and order. Whose voice is absent from Monroe’s message? The indigenous peoples of the Americas, who had their own claims to the territories now being carved into republics, and the enslaved populations of the new nations, whose status would not be altered by independence. Brazil, notably, was not among the recognized republics, because Brazil was not a republic—it was a monarchy that had preserved slavery intact. The United States would recognize Brazil in 1824, once it was clear that the slave system there was secure. The comfortable version of 1822 as a year of hemispheric liberation lets us avoid noticing that the liberation was for white creole elites, that the economic structures of extraction and enslavement continued largely unchanged, and that the United States was positioning itself not as a liberator but as a successor to European imperial power.

The Greek War of Independence and Byron’s death are the events where the Herald’s narrative-architecture lens is most seductive and most incomplete. The steelman: the Greek revolution against Ottoman rule, declared at Epidaurus in April 1822, was a struggle for national self-determination, for the rebirth of classical civilization, for Christian freedom against Muslim tyranny. It provoked a massive humanitarian crisis—massacres, displacement, the Chios atrocity in which tens of thousands of Greeks were killed or enslaved—and it fractured the Holy Alliance, the conservative coalition of Russia, Austria, and Prussia that had pledged to suppress revolution everywhere. Byron, the most famous poet in Europe, went to Greece to support the cause, died of fever at Missolonghi, and became a martyr whose death galvanized public opinion across Europe and North America, eventually pressuring the British and French governments to intervene militarily at Navarino in 1827. This is a story about the power of belief, of art, of individual sacrifice to move the machinery of states.

Now the Heretic’s lens. The Greek revolution was not simply a popular uprising; it was a project of a particular class—Greek merchants, shipowners, and intellectuals of the diaspora, many of whom had grown wealthy in the Ottoman system and now sought to replace Ottoman rule with their own. The revolution was financed in significant part by Greek shipping families who stood to gain control of Mediterranean trade routes. The humanitarian crisis was real and horrific, but the Western response to it was highly selective. The same British public that wept for Missolonghi had little to say about the famines in British India, the displacement of indigenous peoples in the settler colonies, or the conditions on the slave ships that still crossed the Atlantic. Philhellenism was a safe cause for European elites: it allowed them to feel righteous about defending Christian civilization without challenging the class structure at home or the imperial structure abroad. Byron’s death was genuinely moving, genuinely consequential—I concede this to the Herald—but the Byron myth also served specific interests. It gave the British government a narrative cover for naval intervention in the eastern Mediterranean, which was strategically vital for the overland route to India. #### 🜃 Cartographer
The year 1822 does not offer a story of expanding liberty; it offers a map of containment strategies failing and reforming under the weight of demographic inevitability. The surface narrative speaks of revolutions—in Greece, in Brazil, in Spanish America—but the structural reality is the frantic fortification of the racial hierarchy upon which the Atlantic economy rested. When we strip away the rhetoric of self-determination, the six events of this year reveal a singular, terrifying consensus among the ruling classes of the West: the system of chattel slavery is indispensable, but the presence of free Black people within slave societies is fatal to its security.

Consider the geometry of fear. In Charleston, the Denmark Vesey conspiracy (US-1) was not an anomaly; it was the logical output of a city where enslaved people outnumbered whites and where the Haitian Revolution remained a living memory. The response—executing 35 men and erecting a legal architecture of total surveillance—was a structural correction. But violence alone was insufficient. The simultaneous launch of the Liberian settlement (US-2) by the American Colonization Society reveals the complementary strategy: if the enslaved cannot be kept in perpetual terror, the free must be removed. This is not benevolence; it is demographic engineering. The "invisible" thread connecting the gallows in Charleston to the shores of Cape Mesurado is the conviction that a biracial republic is impossible. The U.S. structure bends here, choosing to export its internal contradiction rather than resolve it.

This domestic panic is mirrored and magnified by the global rearrangement of empire. The recognition of Latin American republics (US-3) and the Brazilian declaration of independence (W-3) are often taught as triumphs of democracy. Structurally, they are something else: the preservation of the plantation complex through regime change.
