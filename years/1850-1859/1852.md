## 1. The Six Events

#### US-1 — Death of Daniel Webster (October 24, 1852)
- **What happened (fact):** Webster died of cirrhosis at his Marshfield estate after a lifetime as senator, secretary of state, and the preeminent orator of the American political tradition. His final years were tarnished: the Seventh of March Speech supporting the Compromise of 1850 had destroyed him in New England, his 1852 bid for the Whig nomination collapsed humiliatingly, and he spent his last months drinking and brooding over his diminished stature.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Unionists across the North, this was a genuine tragedy—the passing of the last man who could still make the Union sound like a *sacred* proposition rather than a pragmatic convenience. The eulogies cast him as Moses glimpsing Canaan, the voice that had held back chaos. Even his enemies granted the magnitude; abolitionists mourned the *possibility* of him, the Webster who might have been. The Whig press treated it as an omen: the party that had produced Clay, Webster, and Harrison now had no one.
- **The narrative *now*:** Webster survives in textbooks as a cautionary exhibit—"The Godlike Daniel" who chose Union over conscience, the archetype of the Northern doughface. We remember the Seventh of March as betrayal, not oratory. The "Union" he died defending has become, in retrospective judgment, a euphemism for complicity. His death marks not tragic loss but necessary clearing, the end of an evasion that could no longer be sustained.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The death removes the last performer of a specific political *genre*—the nationalist oratory of Union as mystical bond—which had structured American political imagination since 1787. Without Webster, that language goes unspoken; others may invoke Union, but none can make audiences *feel* it. The Whig collapse that follows is partly organizational, partly the extinction of this rhetorical possibility.

#### US-2 — Publication of Uncle Tom's Cabin (Book Form) (March 20, 1852)
- **What happened (fact):** Stowe's serialized novel appeared in two-volume book form after successful *National Era* serialization; sold 300,000 copies in twelve months in the United States alone, with unauthorized translations and stage adaptations proliferating internationally. The narrative converted the Fugitive Slave Act's legal abstractions—property rights, due process, federal compulsion—into domestic tableaux: Eliza crossing ice, Eva dying, Tom beaten to death.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Northern readers, this was *witnessing*. Stowe constructed an experiential bridge: you did not debate slavery, you *entered* it, felt the nursery and the whipping post as contiguous spaces. The Christian framing made abolitionism emotionally available to readers who found Garrisonian moralism abrasive. Southern critics recognized the weapon immediately; reviews attacked its accuracy with the desperation of those who understood narrative power. The "Tom" stage plays, beginning within months, began the complex process of appropriating and diluting Stowe's radicalism.
- **The narrative *now*:** We inherit "Uncle Tom" as epithet before we encounter the novel, a linguistic pollution that makes original reading nearly impossible. The book sits in curriculums as problematic ancestor—paternalistic, sentimentally Christian, complicit in racial stereotypes even while indicting slavery. Its actual political effect is harder to recover beneath layers of critical suspicion. We remember its fame; we have forgotten the *felt urgency* that made 300,000 readers urgent purchasers in a nation of 23 million.
- **Why it earns a slot:** This is the moment American political argument becomes *domestic* and *affective* at mass scale. The Fugitive Slave Act had made slavery a Northern legal reality; Stowe made it a Northern *moral* emergency experienced through family feeling. The narrative technology—sentimental novel as political instrument—establishes a template that persists through abolitionism, temperance, and eventually Progressive reform.

#### US-3 — Election of Franklin Pierce (November 2, 1852)
- **What happened (fact):** Pierce defeated Winfield Scott 254-42 in the Electoral College, carrying 27 of 31 states. The Whig candidate Scott, a Mexican War hero, lost because the party's Southern wing could not stomach his tepid enforcement of the Compromise, while Northern Whigs could not vote for a slaveholder. The Free Soil Party collapsed from 1848's 10% to 5%. Pierce's platform promised aggressive expansion—"Young America"—and strict enforcement of the Fugitive Slave Act.
- **The narrative *then*:** Democrats read this as national consolidation, the final burial of the Wilmot Proviso heresy. Pierce's personal narrative—son sacrificed to Mexico, handsome, conciliatory—seemed to embody healing. "Young America" intellectuals celebrated the triumph of democratic energy over Whig restrictionism. The scale of victory suggested sectionalism had been definitively rejected by voters. Northern Democrats, especially, told themselves this proved the party could contain and manage the slavery question through popular sovereignty.
- **The narrative *now*:** We see the landslide as structural illusion—the last gasp of a party system about to shatter. Pierce's victory appears not as consolidation but as compression, the suppression of anti-slavery expression that would detonate more violently for having been excluded. The election demonstrates not democratic health but democratic failure: the inability of the Second Party System to process the slavery question, forcing it into new channels. "Young America" reads as imperial prelude, the Democratic Party's expansionism preparing the ground for Kansas-Nebraska and Dred Scott.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The election exposes the party system's breakdown with peculiar clarity: a massive victory that destroys the winning coalition. The Whig collapse is immediate; the Democratic fracture is latent but determinant. The suppression of anti-slavery politics within established channels—the Free Soil collapse, the Whig implosion—directly creates the conditions for Republican emergence and the violence of the 1850s.

#### W-1 — Proclamation of the French Second Empire (December 2, 1852)
- **What happened (fact):** One year after his coup, Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte completed the constitutional transformation: plebiscite ratification (92% approval, under conditions of press suppression and restricted suffrage), formal coronation as Napoleon III, establishment of an authoritarian constitution with limited legislative power. The Second Republic, born in 1848's revolutionary cascade, ended not with counter-revolutionary restoration but with Caesarist innovation.
- **The narrative *then*:** For European liberals, this was the death of hope. The 1848 revolutions had promised, at minimum, constitutional frameworks; by 1852, every major state had retreated—Russia never moved, Austria under Schwarzenberg, Prussia under Manteuffel, now France under Bonaparte. Yet the Bonapartist narrative had genuine purchase: *order* after chaos, *prosperity* after June Days bloodshed, *glory* after bourgeois mediocrity. The name "Napoleon" still operated as mythic machinery; the nephew's bizarre ascent demonstrated its residual power. French peasants and Parisian bourgeois alike voted for the *idea* of Napoleonic resolution.
- **The narrative *now*:** We classify Napoleon III as prefascist or modernizing authoritarian, depending on interpretive school—the Bonapartist state as prototype of twentieth-century mass politics, or as transitional form between aristocratic and democratic ages. The 1848-1852 arc becomes, in standard narratives, the "springtime of peoples" crushed by reaction, with France merely the most dramatic case. We underestimate the *positive* appeal of Bonapartism, its promise of national unity without class war, which would echo through later crises.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The Second Empire's consolidation signals the European-wide failure of liberal constitutionalism in the mid-century, with consequences for American observers. The "failure of 1848" as transatlantic narrative—democratic revolutions crushed by order—shapes how American radicals and conservatives alike read their own prospects. For slaveholders, European reaction confirms that property and hierarchy can survive democratic surges; for abolitionists, it demands renewed commitment to the American experiment as *last* democratic hope.

#### W-2 — Death of the Duke of Wellington (September 14, 1852)
- **What happened (fact):** Arthur Wellesley died at Walmer Castle, aged 83, the last of the Napoleonic generation's dominant figures. His state funeral in November was the grandest since Nelson's, with Victorian ceremonial innovation that established templates still operative. He had served as Prime Minister (1828-1830), but his political significance had long been eclipsed by his symbolic function as embodiment of aristocratic-military virtue.
- **The narrative *then*:** This was experienced as genuine caesura, the end of the world that had made the present world. Wellington had fought at Assaye, Waterloo, sat in Cabinets from Pitt to Palmerston. The funeral oratory emphasized *continuity*—Victoria's Britain as fulfillment of the struggle against Napoleon—while the ceremonial form, with industrial-era mass participation, demonstrated transformation. The Crystal Palace, that iron-and-glass temple of 1851, had already announced the new Britain; Wellington's death confirmed the aristocratic-military past could now be safely monumentalized rather than actively governed.
- **The narrative *now*:** We remember Wellington selectively—Waterloo, perhaps the Catholic Emancipation he resisted then accepted. The funeral's Victorian ceremonial is studied as invention of tradition, the modern mass spectacle disguised as ancient ritual. The "passing of the old order" narrative is familiar to the point of cliché. What we miss is the *active work* this death performed in 1852: the deliberate construction of a usable past, the aristocracy's self-transformation from ruling class to heritage industry, which would prove extraordinarily successful.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The funeral crystallizes the Victorian narrative of progress-as-continuity, industrial modernity legitimated through appropriation of pre-industrial symbols. This model—preserving aristocratic forms while transferring substantive power to bourgeois and bureaucratic actors—becomes globally influential, including in American attempts to manage their own revolutionary heritage against sectional rupture. The contrast with Webster's comparatively unceremonious death is instructive: American nationalist mythology lacked comparable ceremonial infrastructure.

#### W-3 — Taiping Army Breaks Siege of Changsha (November 1852)
- **What happened (fact):** After failing to capture Changsha, where Xiang Army resistance and Qing reinforcement proved unexpectedly formidable, Hong Xiuquan's forces pivoted north, abandoning the Guangxi-Hunan borderlands for the Yangtze valley. The march that followed, through winter 1852-1853, swelled Taiping ranks through incorporation of Triad societies, disaffected peasants, and ethnic minorities, transforming a regional sectarian revolt into a movement capable of capturing Nanjing and establishing rival capital.
- **The narrative *then*:** For Qing officials, this confirmed the dynasty's comprehensive crisis: military failure, fiscal exhaustion, ethnic insurgency, and now pseudo-Christian millenarianism. Western observers, initially dismissive, began recalibrating; American missionaries noted the Taiping's biblical references with complex reactions—hope for Chinese Christianization, horror at heretical deviation. The British press, informed by the expanding treaty-port network, began treating the rebellion as determinant of Asian futures. For Chinese subjects in the path of the march, the narrative was immediate and catastrophic: the Heavenly Kingdom's armies as apocalyptic scourge or liberation, depending on position.
- **The narrative *now*:** The Taiping Rebellion enters world-historical narratives as the nineteenth century's bloodiest conflict (20-30 million dead), a figure that overwhelms specific events. The 1852 pivot is typically submerged in the larger arc; we remember Nanjing 1853, not Changsha's failure. In Chinese historiography, the rebellion is alternatively celebrated as proto-revolutionary peasant movement (Marxist) or condemned as destructive fanaticism (nationalist). The Western attention shift—from Americas to Asia—that 1852 initiates is rarely traced forward to its consequences for American policy, particularly during the Civil War's simultaneous occurrence with the Taiping's climax.
- **Why it earns a slot:** The Changsha pivot demonstrates how failed military operations can be strategically transformative—the Taiping's inability to take provincial capitals directly forced the northern march that ultimately succeeded. More broadly, 1852 marks the point where Qing instability becomes undeniable to Western observers, beginning the process that will make British policy in Asia, not the Americas, the determinant of global imperial strategy. This diversion of attention and resources has underappreciated consequences for American continental expansion's relative freedom from European intervention.

> **Slate note:** These six events were selected to map the hardening of structural divergences: Webster's death and Pierce's election bookend the collapse of the political center in the U.S., while *Uncle Tom's Cabin* weaponizes the `information-velocity` thread to bypass legislative compromise entirely. Globally, the rise of Napoleon III and the death of Wellington mark the end of the post-Napoleonic order, shifting the world toward industrial authoritarianism and empire, mirroring the U.S. drift toward centralized conflict. I deliberately omitted the specific details of the Whig National Convention's disintegration because it is subsumed by the broader electoral mandate given to Pierce; similarly, I excluded minor filibuster attempts in 1852 because the `imperial-slave-safety-valve` thread is better represented by the *election* of a president who would officially sanction such aims, rather than the failed acts of individuals.

Consider the structure of feeling as Americans and Europeans entered it. 1848 had offered narratives of liberation—springtime, peoples, manifest destinies—that 1849-1851 had progressively complicated without yet dissolving. 1852 completes the dissolution by demonstrating that the narratives themselves have become incompatible, that the same words now produce opposite hearers.

The death of Wellington in September performs this operation with elegiac clarity. #### 🜄 Heretic
The received story of 1852, and it is a beautiful story, is one of narrative exhaustion and symbolic transition. The Herald has laid it out with characteristic sensitivity: Wellington’s funeral as the invention of tradition, the French plebiscite as a dark democratic mirror, *Uncle Tom’s Cabin* as the translation of law into feeling. The year, in this telling, is a hinge where old stories—of revolution, of union, of aristocratic continuity—failed, and new, incompatible ones took their place. It is a story about stories, about the felt experience of ideological collapse.

Let me steelman this further. One could argue that 1852 represents the moment when the transatlantic world was forced to confront the fact that its governing abstractions—liberty, progress, nation—had become weapons wielded by opposing hands. Daniel Webster’s death removed the last voice capable of making the Union’s preservation *sound* like a coherent moral project rather than a series of grubby legislative patches. Franklin Pierce’s landslide was a mandate for a story of national unity through aggressive expansion, a “Young America” narrative that promised to drown sectional discord in the Pacific. Even the Taiping Rebellion, sweeping northward through a collapsing Qing state, offered a perverse mirror: a Christian-inflected millenarian narrative of liberation that was, in its own context, as destabilizing to an old order as anything in Europe. The story of 1852 as a crisis of narrative is compelling, even elegant. It is also a luxury.

Because the question my lens demands is not *what stories were told*, but *whose bodies paid for the telling*. The beautiful story about stories is itself an act of erasure. It invites us to mourn dead statesmen, analyze plebiscites, and admire iron-and-glass architecture while the actual engine of the year—the thing that made the narratives necessary in the first place—churns on, unmentioned in the polite obituary of an era. That engine is the expansion and consolidation of a specific, brutal property regime: chattel slavery in the Americas, and the global systems of coerced labor and imperial extraction that fed it.

Let us begin with the event the Herald’s opening has not yet reached, the one that exposes the lie beneath all the eulogies. *Uncle Tom’s Cabin* was published in book form in March 1852. The official story, then and now, is that it was a triumph of moral suasion, a woman’s novel that made the abstract horrors of the Fugitive Slave Act viscerally real for a white Northern audience. It sold 300,000 copies. It is the great cultural fact of the year. But cui bono? Who gained from the story told about this book? Not the enslaved. Harriet Beecher Stowe’s novel, for all its power, ends by shipping its virtuous Black characters off to Liberia. Its most enduring character, Uncle Tom, is a figure of Christ-like submission whose death at the hands of a brutal master is meant to inspire Christian abolitionism, not Black rebellion. The book’s politics are colonizationist at their core. The narrative it offered white America was one of *feeling right* about slavery without *acting right*—a cathartic cry that did not demand the dismantling of the economic order that made the entire nation, North and South, complicit. The publishers got rich. The abolitionist movement got a propaganda tool that could be embraced without endorsing racial equality. The enslaved got a story in which their most saintly representative is beaten to death. That is the gap between the rhetoric and the action. The comfortable version lets us celebrate a book while ignoring that its central political solution was the removal of Black people from the body politic.

Now, look at the year’s political architecture through this lens. The death of Daniel Webster on October 24 is mourned as the loss of the Great Orator, the man who could argue for Union. But what was the Union he argued for? Webster’s last great act was his support for the Compromise of 1850, including the Fugitive Slave Act, which he defended in his infamous “Seventh of March” speech. The Union he preserved was a Union where federal power would be deployed to hunt down human beings fleeing bondage. His death in 1852 was not a tragedy for the cause of freedom; it was the removal of a man whose towering reputation had been used to launder a massive expansion of slave-catching power as a noble act of national salvation. The Whig Party he left leaderless was already a corpse, hollowed out precisely because its narrative of commercial nationalism could not survive the moral and economic gravity of slavery. Who gained from the story of Webster as tragic hero? The interests that needed the Fugitive Slave Act to appear as a painful but necessary compromise rather than what it was: a federal mandate for kidnapping. His death allowed him to be sanctified, his complicity washed clean in eulogy, his name made safe for Northern textbooks while Black Americans in the North lived in terror of being seized on the street.

Then comes the election of Franklin Pierce in November, a landslide that the Herald correctly notes suppressed anti-slavery dissent. But we must be more precise. The story then was that Pierce’s victory was a national endorsement of the Compromise and of “Young America” expansionism. The story now is often one of a nation sleepwalking toward catastrophe, duped by a false consensus. Both stories omit the mechanism. Pierce’s victory was engineered by a Democratic Party that had become, explicitly, the political instrument of the slaveholding class. His “pro-expansion” mandate was a mandate for the acquisition of new territory suitable for plantation agriculture—for Cuba, for Nicaragua, for the reopening of the African slave trade. The “Young America” rhetoric of national vigor and democratic destiny was a propaganda shell around a core of capital accumulation through enslaved labor and land speculation. The voters who gave Pierce his landslide were not merely avoiding the slavery question; they were endorsing a political economy that promised national prosperity on the backs of Black bodies. The distance between the rhetoric of “manifest destiny” and the action of filibustering expeditions to seize slave territory is the central fact of the election. The comfortable version lets us pity a naive electorate. The evidence demands we see a coalition built on the promise of stolen land and stolen labor.

Turn to Europe, and the pattern holds. The proclamation of the French Second Empire on December 2, 1852, is presented as a dark authoritarian turn, a Caesarist plebiscite. The official story is one of democratic failure. But cui bono? Louis-Napoléon’s coup was not merely a response to the chaos of 1848; it was the violent reassertion of a property order that the revolution had threatened. The French peasants and bourgeois who voted “yes” were voting to protect their material interests—their land, their capital—from the specter of redistribution. The Second Empire was the political form that guaranteed the security of property against the “socialist” menace. The narrative of national glory and order was the sugar-coating on a bullet. The omission in the story of 1852 as a crisis of liberal narrative is the class war that the narrative was designed to win. The silence in the archive is the voice of the June Days of 1848, the massacred workers of Paris, whose deaths were the precondition for the Empire’s legitimacy. Napoleon III’s empire was built on their graves, and the plebiscite was the ceremony of forgetting.

Across the Channel, the death of Wellington on September 14, 1852, and the subsequent state funeral, are read as the invention of tradition. But what tradition, and for whom? The funeral was a massive piece of political theater designed to fuse aristocracy, industry, and empire into a single national pageant. The working-class crowds who lined the streets were not participants; they were props in a display of national unity that masked the deep fractures of industrial capitalism. The Crystal Palace, that monument to free trade and peaceful progress, was the economic engine that required the brutal suppression of labor at home and colonial subjects abroad. The Duke of Wellington, the man who had presided over the Corn Laws that starved the poor to keep land rents high, was rebranded in death as a national unifier. The story of his funeral is a story about the management of memory to serve the interests of a ruling class terrified of Chartism and continental revolution. The absence in the record is the hunger, the factory accident, the colonial war that made the prosperity on display possible.

Finally, the Taiping Rebellion’s northward march in 1852, its failed sieges and swelling ranks. The Western-centric narrative treats this as a distant tremor, a diversion of imperial attention. This is a profound omission. The Taiping Heavenly Kingdom, led by a man who claimed to be the brother of Jesus Christ, was a massive, millenarian revolt against a Qing state hollowed out by the opium trade—a trade enforced on China by British gunboats. The chaos that the Taiping forces unleashed in 1852 was a direct, structural consequence of Western imperial violence. The British Empire, mourning Wellington and celebrating its industrial peace, was simultaneously profiting from the drug trade that was immiserating millions of Chinese peasants and fueling the very instability that the Taiping rebels exploited. The story of 1852 as a year of symbolic transition in the West omits the fact that the West’s symbols were written in the blood of the global periphery. The comfortable version lets us study the Crystal Palace without smelling the opium smoke that helped pay for it.

The year 1852 is not a crisis of narrative. It is a year of consolidation for the powerful, achieved through the violent suppression or erasure of those who threatened their property. The stories that died—Webster’s compromise, the Second Republic, the old aristocratic order—were not tragic losses. They were discarded tools, replaced by more effective instruments of control: the Fugitive Slave Act, the Bonapartist state, the myth of industrial-national unity. The real story of 1852 is the tightening of a global system of extraction, and the successful deployment of narrative to make that tightening look like a eulogy, a plebiscite, or a bestselling novel.

### Round 2 — Cross-Examination

**Heretic → Herald:**

I begin by restating your argument, because it deserves to be taken seriously. Your claim is that 1852 marks a rupture in the *experience* of narrative—that the stories which had organized political and moral life across the Atlantic world ceased to function as shared frames and became instead incompatible, weaponized, mutually unintelligible. Wellington’s funeral was not merely a state ceremony but a desperate act of narrative construction, an attempt to forge continuity where none existed, to make the Iron Duke’s corpse carry a story his living politics had refused. Uncle Tom’s Cabin, in your reading, did not simply argue against slavery; it transposed the entire debate from the cold language of constitutional compromise into the hot language of familial feeling, making sectional reconciliation narratively impossible because no one can compromise over a child being sold. The French plebiscite, the death of Webster, the rise of Pierce’s “Young America”—all of these, you argue, are symptoms of a single condition: the collapse of a shared symbolic order. The year is a hinge not because of any single event, but because the *means of making meaning* fractured. I grant you this: you have captured something real about the felt texture of 1852, the way historical actors experienced their own moment as a crisis of legibility. And I grant you further that attending to this texture is necessary historical work. The past is not merely a sequence of material transactions; it is lived through categories, symbols, stories, and when those break, something genuinely changes.

Now let me press.

Your entire framework—narrative exhaustion, incompatible stories, the collapse of shared symbolic orders—is a phenomenology of the powerful. Whose stories stopped working? Wellington’s funeral was a narrative crisis *for the British ruling class*, which had to reconcile its actual history of reactionary obstruction with its desired self-image as the organic guardian of national progress. That is a real problem for dukes and prime ministers. But for the Irish tenant farmer evicted in the Clearances that Wellington’s government had enforced, for the Bengali weaver whose livelihood had been destroyed by the cotton tariffs Wellington’s party maintained, for the English factory operative working fourteen hours in a mill built on the political economy Wellington defended—for them, the story had never *worked*. It had never been a story at all. It was a condition. The crisis you describe as a rupture in 1852 was, for the majority of human beings living under British or American power, the permanent background noise of a story told about them but never to them. What you call “narrative exhaustion” I call the moment when the ruling class briefly panicked because its own propaganda stopped being convincing *to itself*. That is interesting. It is not the same thing as a general crisis of meaning.

Let me be more specific. You invoke *Uncle Tom’s Cabin* as a translation of law into feeling, a narrative act that made compromise impossible. But this treats the enslaved as a *subject of representation* rather than as historical actors. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 did not become real in 1852 because Harriet Beecher Stowe wrote a novel about it. It became real in 1850, when Black families in Pennsylvania and Ohio and Massachusetts—people whose names Stowe never knew—began fleeing to Canada, when slave-catchers began kidnapping free Black Northerners under color of law, when the organized Black communities of Boston and Syracuse began physically resisting rendition. The “story” that stopped working was not the story of the Union; it was the story that Black Americans would wait patiently for white people’s legal processes to recognize their humanity. That story had stopped working long before 1852. The Fugitive Slave Act merely made the contradiction visible to white Northerners who had previously been able to look away. Your focus on Stowe’s novel as the transformative event centers the white reader’s dawning moral awareness as the engine of history. I am asking you to consider that the engine was elsewhere—in the actions of the enslaved and the free Black communities who had been forcing the crisis for decades, and whose resistance was the condition of possibility for Stowe’s book to find its audience. The story that mattered in 1852 was not the story white people told about slavery. It was the story Black people were enacting with their feet, their bodies, their organized defiance. That story was not exhausted. It was just beginning.

So my question to you is this: when you say “the stories stop working,” whose stories do you mean? And what does it cost your analysis that the people whose stories had never been permitted to work in the first place—the enslaved, the colonized, the displaced—do not appear in your account as bearers of narrative at all, but only as occasions for the narrative crises of others?

### Round 3 — Pressure on the Narrative (then vs. now vs. reality)

In 1852, the received story of the American experiment was undergoing a surgical intervention, and the surgeon was speaking with the voice of a witness. *Uncle Tom's Cabin* arrived not as a novel but as a national reckoning—Harriet Beecher Stowe's weekly installments in the *National Era* had already made the slave's body into every parlor's secret companion, but the bound volume consecrated what the installments had prepared: a conversion narrative for a Protestant nation that had somehow baptized human property. Contemporaries did not read this as literature primarily. They read it as *evidence*—Stowe's appendix of documentation, her affidavit of authenticity, her claim to have drawn Legree from life. The book's power lay in its generic confusion: it asked to be received as documentary, as sermon, as sentimental tear-duct irrigation, and as constitutional brief against the Fugitive Slave Act all at once. The weeping reader of 1852 wept not for aesthetic pleasure but for *moral participation*, for the sensation of having finally *seen* what the South's apologists had kept veiled.
